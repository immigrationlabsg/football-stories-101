Ronaldo came off the bench in Rome on 12 April 2000.

It was the Coppa Italia final, first leg, at the Stadio Olimpico. His first match since November.

Within minutes, six by most accounts and seven by others, he shaped to turn and his right knee buckled beneath him.

The patellar tendon, repaired five months earlier, had ruptured completely.

He was still in his early twenties. At 20 he had been the youngest player ever named FIFA World Player of the Year. At 21 he had won the Ballon d'Or.

He would not play another competitive match for 17 months.

Go back a little. Ronaldo grew up in Rio de Janeiro supporting Flamengo, and Flamengo let him go.

The story, as told by his youth coach at São Cristóvão, Alfredo Sampaio, is that the boy missed training because he could not afford the bus fare. The club offered no help.

So a future World Player of the Year slipped through the net over the price of a bus ride.

Cruzeiro took him on instead. On 7 November 1993 he scored five in a 6–0 win over Bahia.

Then came Europe. Thirty league goals in his first season at PSV Eindhoven. Then Barcelona, and 47 goals in 49 games by the club's own count.

Away at Compostela in October 1996, he rode a trip and a tug from the first defender, ran past several more and scored. On the touchline Bobby Robson lifted his hands to his head.

Inter paid a world-record fee, reported at £19.5 million, in 1997.

And then his body started sending the bill.

On 21 November 1999, against Lecce, he felt his right knee give and limped off. Tendon ruptured. Surgery.

Five months later came Rome, and the knee lasted a matter of minutes.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The comeback was not one return but a run of false starts.

The knee was rebuilt in Paris by the surgeon Gérard Saillant. Ronaldo's first competitive minutes came on 20 September 2001, the last 28 of a UEFA Cup tie against Brașov.

His return to league football that autumn lasted 13 minutes before another, smaller injury.

His first league goal on the new knee finally came at Brescia on 9 December 2001. It was his first since the afternoon against Lecce, two years earlier.

On the last day of that season Inter lost 4–2 at Lazio and handed Juventus the title.

Ronaldo was substituted and wept on the bench.

A few weeks later he was at the World Cup in South Korea and Japan.

Then came the part that surprised me.

He had been troubled by thigh injuries before the tournament. So he shaved his head, all except a patch of hair at the front.

Years later he said it was deliberate. Once he turned up to training with it, he said, nobody talked about the injury any more.

The haircut was a decoy.

He scored against Turkey, against China, twice against Costa Rica, and late against Belgium. In the semi-final he beat Turkey with a toe-poke.

The final, on 30 June 2002, put him against Oliver Kahn, who had conceded one goal all tournament.

In the 67th minute Kahn failed to hold a low shot from Rivaldo, and Ronaldo scored from the rebound.

In the 79th Rivaldo dummied Kléberson's pass across the area, and Ronaldo finished low.

Brazil won 2–0.

Eight goals, the Golden Boot, and a third FIFA World Player of the Year award. Two years after a knee injury that might have ended his career.

The knees never stopped failing him. In February 2008, playing for Milan, his left patellar tendon went three minutes after he came on.

He retired in February 2011, aged 34. As he put it: "Mentally, I wanted to continue, but I have to acknowledge that I lost [the fight] to my body."

Here's what stays with me.

We usually measure a striker by what he did. Ronaldo makes us count what he was prevented from doing, and the sum never settles.

He was the best in the world before the knee went, and again after it was rebuilt.

Almost nobody manages that once.

Which player's career do you still wonder about: what they did, or what their body stopped them doing?
