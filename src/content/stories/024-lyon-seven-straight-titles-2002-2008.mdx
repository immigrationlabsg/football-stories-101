On the last day of the 2001–02 French season, 4 May 2002, the two teams that could still win the title met each other at the Stade de Gerland in Lyon. Lens were a point ahead and needed only a draw for their second championship in four years. Lyon, founded in 1950, had never in 52 years been champions of France.

Lens arrived as leaders, but Lyon were in control from the start. Sidney Govou, Philippe Violeau and Pierre Laigle scored, Jacek Bąk pulled one back for Lens, and Lyon won 3–1 to finish on 66 points, two ahead. The coach, Jacques Santini, had built his team around the goals of Sonny Anderson, Govou and Éric Carrière.

It was the first title. Nobody at Gerland that afternoon could have guessed that Lyon would win the next six as well.

## The software man

Fifteen years earlier, in 1987, Lyon had been stuck in the second division for four years, with debts of about €15 million, when they were taken over by a local businessman, Jean-Michel Aulas. His computer software company, Cegid, was beginning to flourish, and he brought a businessman’s habits to a club that, by UEFA’s account, had mainly lived on grants from the city. He promised a sceptical public that Lyon would be in Europe within four years.

The step that changed the scale of the club was the arrival of Jérôme Seydoux and the film company Pathé, which UEFA’s history of the club calls the moment that really launched it. Aulas has said that without Seydoux and Pathé, Lyon would have had great difficulty making that step. In 1999, with Seydoux’s backing, Lyon bought Anderson from Barcelona.

In the summer of 2001 Lyon signed a 26-year-old Brazilian midfielder from Vasco da Gama on a free transfer. Juninho Pernambucano made his debut away to Lens on 28 July 2001, at the start of the season that would end with Lens coming to Gerland with the title in their hands. Over the next eight seasons he scored 100 goals for Lyon, and 44 of them came direct from free kicks. He was there for all seven titles.

## Seven

The run was remarkable not only for its length but for how often the people in charge changed. In May 2002, with the first title just won, Santini stepped down as coach; that summer he succeeded Roger Lemerre as manager of France, with the job of taking the national team to Euro 2004. His replacement was Paul Le Guen, and under him Lyon won the title in 2003, again in 2004 and again in 2005. The 2004 title was the hardest of the three: Lyon did not go top of the table until March, when Monaco slipped, and even after losing 1–0 to Paris Saint-Germain, their closest challengers, in the run-in, they settled it a week later with a 3–0 win over Lille.

Gérard Houllier took over from Le Guen and won two more. The fifth, in 2006, was a French record, and Lyon marked it in mid-April with a 1–0 win over Paris Saint-Germain at the Parc des Princes.

Behind the coaches, the players changed too, and that was the design. Lyon bought young players, often from other French clubs, developed them, and sold them on at a profit. Michael Essien arrived from Bastia, in Corsica, in June 2003. In August 2005 Chelsea made him their record signing, at a fee reported at between £24.4 million and £26 million. Florent Malouda arrived from Guingamp in 2003 for a reported €6.5 million and later followed Essien to Stamford Bridge. Each summer the team was taken apart a little; each spring it won the league again.

What Lyon could not do was win in Europe. They reached the Champions League quarter-finals three seasons running, from 2004 to 2006, and went no further. In April 2005 they met PSV Eindhoven. Malouda scored first at Gerland and Phillip Cocu equalised; in Eindhoven Lyon again took the lead and were again pegged back to 1–1, and after extra time they lost the penalty shoot-out 4–2, with the goalkeeper Heurelho Gomes saving from Essien and Éric Abidal.

A year later Lyon met PSV again, in the round of 16, and beat them home and away. The quarter-final was crueller still. After a goalless first leg against Milan in Lyon, the return at San Siro on 4 April 2006 stood at 1–1 with two minutes left, Mahamadou Diarra having equalised Filippo Inzaghi’s opener. On away goals, Lyon were going through. Inzaghi scored again in the 88th minute and Andriy Shevchenko added a third in stoppage time. The club’s first semi-final would not come until 2010, after the run had ended.

## The turn

The decision that made Lyon different was Aulas’s choice to run the club with a businessman’s methods. He sold his best players at the top of their value and changed his coach while the team was still winning, and the team kept winning anyway. In February 2007 he took that further: OL Groupe, the club’s parent company, floated on Euronext Paris. Trading began on 9 February 2007 at €24 a share, and the offer raised €94.3 million. A football club now had outside shareholders and a share price, and the results on the pitch had become part of a stock-market story.

The same logic applied to coaches. In 2007 Houllier resigned and Alain Perrin took over. Perrin’s season was the most successful in the club’s history. Lyon went to Auxerre on the last day, 17 May 2008, needing a win to secure a seventh title. Karim Benzema, who finished as the league’s top scorer with 20 goals, scored in the first minute. He was the other half of the Lyon method: a boy from the city who had joined the club’s training centre in 1996 and made his league debut as a 17-year-old in April 2005, against Lens. Lyon won 3–1. A week later Govou’s goal beat Paris Saint-Germain 1–0 in the Coupe de France final at the Stade de France. It was the first league and cup double in Lyon’s history, and the first by any French club since Auxerre in 1996.

On 16 June 2008, Lyon announced that Perrin was leaving, by what Aulas described as mutual agreement. Claude Puel was named as his successor two days later. Aulas called Perrin’s appointment a casting error and explained it this way.

> He obtained titles, including the double, but managing is foreseeing.
> — Jean-Michel Aulas on Alain Perrin, Lyon, 16 June 2008

The seventh title was the last. In 2009 Laurent Blanc’s Bordeaux took the title on the final day with a 1–0 win at Caen, finishing three points clear of Marseille. Lyon, champions for seven years, finished third. That May Juninho, aged 34, asked to be released from the final year of his contract. At the press conference in Lyon on 26 May 2009 he sat next to Aulas and said nothing; it was Aulas who told the room that “Juni” was going to leave them, and Juninho who broke down in tears. A month later, signing for Al-Gharafa in Qatar, he found his words.

> I spent the best years of my life in Lyon.
> — Juninho Pernambucano, June 2009, as reported by Reuters

## What it tells us

Lyon show that a club without a history of winning can build one by treating continuity as a matter of method rather than of people: the players, the coaches and even the trophies changed, while the way of working stayed the same. Seven straight titles remain the French record. The same method that kept them in front at home never carried them past a European quarter-final in those seven years, and that gap is the honest measure of what a well-run club in a smaller league can and cannot do.
