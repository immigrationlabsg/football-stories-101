On 4 May 2002, the last day of the French season, the only two teams that could still win the title met at the Stade de Gerland in Lyon.

Lens were a point ahead. A draw would do.

Lyon, founded in 1950, had never in 52 years been champions of France.

They won 3–1.

Then they won the next six titles as well. Seven in a row is still the longest run in French football.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Lyon kept winning while changing almost everything.

Jacques Santini stepped down that same month and went on to manage France. Paul Le Guen replaced him and won three more. Gérard Houllier followed and won two. Alain Perrin won the seventh.

Four coaches. Seven titles.

The players changed too, and that was the design.

Lyon bought young players, often from other French clubs, developed them and sold them on at a profit. Michael Essien arrived from Bastia in 2003. Two years later Chelsea made him their record signing, at a fee reported at between £24.4 million and £26 million.

Each summer the team was taken apart a little. Each spring it won the league again.

The one constant was a Brazilian who had arrived on a free transfer from Vasco da Gama in 2001.

Juninho Pernambucano scored 100 goals for Lyon.

Forty-four of them came direct from free kicks.

He was there for all seven titles.

It was all built by a club that, 15 years before that first title, had been stuck in the second division with debts of about €15 million.

In 1987 it was taken over by a local businessman, Jean-Michel Aulas, whose computer software company was beginning to flourish. By UEFA’s account, the club had mainly lived on grants from the city.

Aulas promised a sceptical public that Lyon would be in Europe within four years.

In February 2007 he took the business logic further. OL Groupe, the club’s parent company, floated on the Paris stock exchange at €24 a share, raising €94.3 million.

A football club now had a share price.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Perrin’s season, 2007–08, was the most successful in Lyon’s history.

On the last day they went to Auxerre needing a win. Karim Benzema, a boy from the city who had joined the club’s training centre in 1996, scored in the first minute. Lyon won 3–1 for a seventh title.

A week later Sidney Govou’s goal beat Paris Saint-Germain in the Coupe de France final. It was the first league and cup double in the club’s history.

On 16 June 2008, Lyon announced that Perrin was leaving.

Aulas called his appointment a casting error. As he put it: “He obtained titles, including the double, but managing is foreseeing.”

That was the method in one sentence. Results were not enough. The machine came first.

What the machine could not do was win in Europe.

Lyon reached the Champions League quarter-finals three seasons running and went no further. In 2005 they lost to PSV Eindhoven on penalties.

In 2006, at San Siro, they were going through on away goals against Milan with two minutes left. Filippo Inzaghi scored in the 88th minute. Andriy Shevchenko added a third in stoppage time.

The club’s first semi-final did not come until 2010, after the run had ended.

The seventh title was the last. In 2009 Bordeaux took the crown and Lyon finished third.

That May, Juninho, aged 34, asked to leave. At the press conference he sat next to Aulas and said nothing. It was Aulas who told the room that Juni was going to leave them.

Juninho broke down in tears.

Here's what stays with me.

Lyon treated continuity as a method rather than a matter of people. The players, the coaches and even the trophies came and went while the way of working stayed the same. But the man everyone remembers is the one who stayed, and could not speak when it was time to go.
