On 4 May 2002, the last day of the French season, the only two teams that could still win the title met in Lyon.

Lens were a point ahead. A draw would do.

Lyon had never been champions of France.

They won 3–1.

Then they won six more. Seven in a row is still the longest run in French football.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Lyon kept winning while changing almost everything.

Jacques Santini stepped down that month. Paul Le Guen replaced him and won three more. Gérard Houllier won two. Alain Perrin won the seventh.

Four coaches. Seven titles.

The players changed too, and that was the design. Lyon bought young players, developed them and sold them at a profit.

Michael Essien arrived from Bastia in 2003. Two years later Chelsea made him their record signing.

The one constant was Juninho Pernambucano, a Brazilian who arrived on a free transfer in 2001.

He scored 100 goals for Lyon. Forty-four came direct from free kicks.

It was all built by Jean-Michel Aulas, a software businessman who took over in 1987, with the club stuck in the second division.

Then came the part that surprised me.

On the last day of 2007–08, Karim Benzema scored in the first minute at Auxerre, and Lyon won 3–1 for a seventh title.

A week later Sidney Govou’s goal beat Paris Saint-Germain in the Coupe de France final. It was the club’s first double.

Weeks later, Lyon let Perrin go.

As Aulas put it: “He obtained titles, including the double, but managing is foreseeing.”

In 2009 Bordeaux took the title. That May, Juninho asked to leave. At the press conference he said nothing. It was Aulas who told the room.

Juninho broke down in tears.

Here's what stays with me.

At Lyon, continuity was a method, not a matter of people. Players and coaches came and went while the way of working stayed the same. But the man everyone remembers is the one who stayed, and could not speak when it was time to go.
