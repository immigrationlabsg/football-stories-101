On the evening of Monday 2 May 2016, the Leicester City squad gathered at Jamie Vardy's house in Melton Mowbray to watch a match they were not playing in.

Tottenham Hotspur had to beat Chelsea to keep the title race alive. At half-time Tottenham led 2–0. It finished 2–2.

Leicester were champions of England for the first time in their 132-year history.

Before the season, bookmakers had made them 5,000 to 1.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

It was not a silly price. Twelve months earlier, Leicester had been bottom of the Premier League on Christmas Day.

By early April 2015 they had won four of their first 29 league matches and were seven points from safety.

They survived under Nigel Pearson, winning seven of their last nine. That summer the club's Thai owners dismissed him anyway.

In his place came Claudio Ranieri, whose last job had ended with the sack as coach of Greece after a 1–0 home defeat by the Faroe Islands. Much of the reaction in England was sceptical.

He and the chairman set a target of 40 points, enough to stay up.

The squad was a collection of bargains. Vardy had begun in non-League football at Stocksbridge Park Steels and joined Leicester from Fleetwood Town for a fee reported at £1 million.

Riyad Mahrez had come from Le Havre for less than £500,000, spotted in France by the club's scout, Steve Walsh.

N'Golo Kanté arrived from Caen in August 2015. Ranieri later wrote that he seemed to have batteries hidden in his shorts.

By the end of the season Kanté led the league in both tackles and interceptions.

Vardy would not stop scoring. On 28 November he scored in his 11th consecutive Premier League match, one more than Ruud van Nistelrooy's record.

The defence took longer. Two months in, Leicester still had not kept a clean sheet.

So Ranieri offered the players pizza if they kept one.

On 24 October they beat Crystal Palace 1–0. Ranieri took them to a pizzeria in the city centre and made them roll the dough and choose the toppings themselves.

By Christmas they were top. No Premier League side had gone from bottom at one Christmas to top at the next.

Then came the part that surprised me.

At Chelsea, years earlier, Ranieri's habit of rotating his team had earned him the nickname the Tinkerman.

At Leicester he watched recordings of every match of the previous season, and decided the side did not need reinventing.

He promised the players at least two days off a week. In return, they had to run as hard as they had at the end of the previous season.

According to the Premier League's own analysis, he made only 33 changes to his starting line-up all season, the second-fewest by any Premier League champion.

On 14 February Danny Welbeck's stoppage-time header won it for Arsenal. It was Leicester's third league defeat.

It was also their last.

That April, Ranieri explained what he told supporters who said they were dreaming. As he put it in The Players' Tribune: "We do not dream. We simply work hard."

On 2 May, with Tottenham 2–0 up, Gary Cahill pulled one back for Chelsea. Late in the second half Eden Hazard, on as a substitute, equalised.

Ranieri was not at Vardy's house. He had flown to Rome that day to have lunch with his mother, who was 96. Accounts differ on whether he saw the match live or landed after it was over.

On 7 May Wes Morgan, the captain, lifted the trophy. Leicester finished on 81 points, ten clear of Arsenal.

William Hill had taken 25 bets at 5,000 to 1 and reported a loss of about £2.2 million.

And Gary Lineker, who had promised to present Match of the Day in his underwear if Leicester won the league, did it in August 2016, in white shorts.

Here's what stays with me.

The odds were not wrong. In a league where money buys points, 5,000 to 1 is a fair price. What the odds cannot see is a manager willing to leave a working team alone. Sometimes the bravest decision is to change nothing.
