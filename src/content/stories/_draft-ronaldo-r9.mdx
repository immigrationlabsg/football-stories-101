Six minutes into the Coppa Italia final at the Stadio Olimpico on 12 April 2000, Ronaldo took a pass on the left of the Lazio penalty area, dropped his shoulder, pushed the ball on to his right foot and planted that foot to turn. The knee folded underneath him. He went down holding it with both hands, and the first people to reach him were not his team-mates but the Inter medical staff, who had spent the previous five months rebuilding it. The patellar tendon they had repaired in November had torn again. Inter's players stood around him with their hands on their heads. On the television pictures the only sound is the crowd going quiet.

He was 23. Four years earlier he had been the youngest FIFA World Player of the Year in history, and most people who watched football believed he was going to be the best player who had ever lived. He was about to spend the next 17 months learning to walk down stairs.

## The phenomenon

Ronaldo Luís Nazário de Lima grew up in Bento Ribeiro, a working-class suburb in the north of Rio de Janeiro, and did not play for either of the city's big clubs. Flamengo turned him away as a boy because he could not afford the bus fare to training. He joined Cruzeiro of Belo Horizonte at 16, and in a little over a year scored 44 goals in 47 matches, including five in a single game against Bahia. Brazil took him to the 1994 World Cup at 17. He did not play a minute, but he came home with a winner's medal and a nickname that stuck: O Fenômeno, the phenomenon.

PSV Eindhoven bought him that summer. He scored 30 league goals in his first Dutch season, more than 50 in two, and the first warning sign arrived there too: in 1996 he had surgery on his right knee and missed most of the spring. Barcelona, where Bobby Robson had just taken over, paid a world-record fee anyway. Robson later wrote that he had never seen anyone run with the ball at that speed.

What followed was the single best season a striker has had in the modern era: 47 goals in 49 games in all competitions, 34 of them in the league, the Spanish Cup, the Cup Winners' Cup, and a goal against Compostela on 12 October 1996 in which he picked the ball up inside his own half, was fouled twice, kept running, beat four men and scored, while Robson stood on the touchline with both hands on his head. He was 20 when FIFA named him World Player of the Year in December 1996 and 21 when he won it again, and the Ballon d'Or with it, a year later.

Inter paid another world-record fee in 1997. In his first season in Italy he scored 34 goals, won the UEFA Cup with a goal in the final against Lazio, and went to the 1998 World Cup as the best player in the tournament. He won the Golden Ball there, too. What he did not win was the final.

## Paris, 12 July 1998

The team sheet Brazil handed to FIFA an hour before the final against France at the Stade de France did not have Ronaldo's name on it. A second team sheet, delivered minutes before kick-off, did. In between, the story came out in pieces that have been argued over ever since: that afternoon in the team hotel he had suffered a convulsive fit, his room-mate Roberto Carlos had raised the alarm, and he had been taken to hospital for tests that found nothing. He was cleared to play, insisted on playing, and played like a man who had not slept. France won 3–0. Ronaldo has said many times since that he remembers none of the fit, and that he regrets nothing about asking to play. Nothing better than his own account has ever been established, and this story does not pretend otherwise.

Fifteen months later came Lecce. On 21 November 1999, in an ordinary Serie A match, he stretched for a ball and ruptured the patellar tendon of his right knee. The surgery and rehabilitation took the winter. He came back in the cup final in April, and the knee lasted six minutes.

## Eighteen months

Very few athletes had come back from a double rupture of the patellar tendon to play at the top level, and none in football at his standard. The French surgeon Gérard Saillant rebuilt the knee in Paris, and Ronaldo spent most of 2000 and 2001 in rehabilitation, first unable to bend the joint, then unable to run, then able to run but not to turn. He played a handful of minutes for Inter at the end of 2001, and ten league games in the spring of 2002, scoring seven goals. He also sat on the bench in tears on 5 May 2002 as Inter lost the title at Lazio on the final afternoon of the season.

Brazil's coach, Luiz Felipe Scolari, took him to the World Cup in Japan and South Korea four weeks later on the strength of those ten games and nothing else. There were people in Brazil who thought it was sentiment.

He scored in the first match against Turkey. He scored against China, and against Costa Rica, and against Belgium, and in the semi-final against Turkey again, a toe-poke under the goalkeeper from a position where a toe-poke was the only shot available. Before the final he shaved his head except for a half-moon of hair at the front, a haircut that was mocked around the world; he said years later that he had done it on purpose, to give the press something to talk about other than a thigh strain he was carrying. In the final in Yokohama on 30 June 2002, against Germany and Oliver Kahn, who had conceded one goal in six matches, he scored twice in the second half: a rebound after Kahn spilled Rivaldo's shot, and a low finish into the corner from Kléberson's cut-back. Brazil won 2–0. Ronaldo finished with 8 goals and the Golden Boot, and was named World Player of the Year for the third time.

> Every day I trained and played with him, I saw something different, something new, something beautiful.
> — Zinedine Zidane, on his Real Madrid team-mate

## The second career

Real Madrid signed him from Inter that summer. He scored twice on his debut as a substitute against Alavés, won the league in 2003, and in April that year scored a hat-trick at Old Trafford in the Champions League that ended with the Manchester United crowd standing to applaud him off the pitch. He was top scorer in Spain in 2004. He was heavier now, and slower, and the knees were strapped every time he played, and he still scored 104 goals in 177 games for the club.

At the 2006 World Cup he scored three times to take his total to 15, one more than Gerd Müller, a record that stood until Miroslav Klose passed it in 2014. In February 2008, playing for Milan, his left knee went the way the right one had. He went home to Brazil, won the Copa do Brasil with Corinthians in 2009, and on 14 February 2011, aged 34, stood in front of the cameras in São Paulo and stopped.

> It's very hard to leave something that made me so happy. Mentally I wanted to continue, but I have to acknowledge that I lost to my body.
> — Ronaldo, retirement press conference, 14 February 2011

## The turn

The decision that explains this career was taken in a hospital in Paris in April 2000, when Ronaldo, 23 years old and holding a knee that had failed twice, chose the long operation and the longer rehabilitation over an easier life as a very rich retired footballer. He had already won everything an individual player could win. What he had not done was win a World Cup on the pitch, and the two years that followed were, by his own account, the hardest of his life. Everything after 2002, the second career in Madrid, the record in Germany, the sad coda in Milan, was lived on knees that the medical staff who treated him had not expected to carry a professional footballer again.

The what-if is unavoidable and he has answered it himself, more than once: he does not know how good he would have been without the injuries, and neither does anyone else. The numbers that exist are 62 goals in 98 games for Brazil, 15 at World Cups, three World Player of the Year awards, and the only World Cup final in which every goal was scored by the same man, a man who had been told he might never run properly again.

## What it tells us

Greatness in a striker is usually measured in what the player did. Ronaldo's case forces the other measurement, what the player was prevented from doing, and makes it impossible to settle. He was the best in the world at 21 and again at 25 on a different body, which is a thing almost nobody manages once. The honest version of his story is not the tragedy of the lost years in between, but the fact that he chose to have them.
