Ten minutes into the first European Cup final, in Paris on 13 June 1956, Real Madrid were losing 2–0 to Stade de Reims.

Alfredo Di Stéfano pulled one back after 14 minutes. Héctor Rial equalised. Reims went ahead again, and the defender Marquitos levelled. Rial scored the winner 11 minutes from time.

Real Madrid won 4–3.

They would not lose a European Cup final for five years. And Di Stéfano, who started the comeback in Paris, would score in every one of the five.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Di Stéfano was nearly a Barcelona player. For a while, he was meant to be both.

He was an Argentine centre-forward who had left River Plate for Millonarios of Bogotá in 1949. River still considered him theirs.

In 1953 Barcelona agreed a deal with River. Real Madrid bought the rights Millonarios claimed. Each club believed it owned him.

The Spanish federation's solution was one of the oddest rulings in football history. Di Stéfano would alternate seasons: Real Madrid in 1953–54, Barcelona the next, and so on.

The Barcelona president signed up to it and was criticised so heavily at home that he announced his resignation. Within weeks Barcelona withdrew their claim.

How that happened is still argued over. Barcelona's own history says the federation, under pressure from the state and from Real Madrid, blocked the move. Madrid-based writers reject the idea that the Franco regime stole the player.

Both clubs agree on what came next. That October, in his first clásico, Di Stéfano scored in a 5–0 win over Barcelona.

The man who wanted him most was Santiago Bernabéu, the club's president. He had seen Di Stéfano in a Millonarios friendly against Madrid and, by the accounts of people around him, decided on the spot.

In December 1954 the French sports paper L'Équipe supplied the opponents. Its journalist Gabriel Hanot, irritated by English newspapers declaring Wolverhampton Wanderers champions of the world, proposed a real competition between Europe's champions.

Real Madrid entered the first one.

Then came the part that surprised me.

In the Reims forward line that day in Paris was Raymond Kopa, the best French footballer of his generation.

A few days earlier, he had agreed to join Real Madrid. He was playing his last match for Reims against his next employers.

Then Madrid kept winning it. In 1957 they beat Fiorentina 2–0 in their own stadium. In 1958 they came from behind twice against Milan and won 3–2, in the first European Cup final to go to extra time.

In 1959 they met Reims again. This time Kopa was in white, lining up against his old club. Madrid won 2–0.

In 1960, at Hampden Park, they beat Eintracht Frankfurt 7–3. Di Stéfano scored three and Ferenc Puskás four.

Yet in the two seasons Madrid won their fourth and fifth European Cups, they did not win the Spanish league.

Barcelona did, both times. The second title came on goal average, after the two clubs finished level on 46 points.

And it was Barcelona, in the first round of the 1960–61 competition, who became the first team to knock Real Madrid out of the European Cup.

Everything around Di Stéfano changed. Coaches came and went. Kopa arrived and won the Ballon d'Or. Puskás came in time for the last two finals. By 1960 the man on the bench was Miguel Muñoz, who as captain had lifted the trophy in 1956.

Di Stéfano was the constant: a centre-forward who defended, organised and dictated the tempo from deep before finishing the move himself.

When UEFA gave him its first President's Award in 2008, Michel Platini reached for the vocabulary of the 1950s. As he put it: "A god of the stadium, a magician of the ball, a perfect football master."

Francisco Gento, the winger on the left, outlasted everyone. He won a sixth European Cup in 1966 and remains the only player to have won it six times.

Here's what stays with me.

A dynasty hung on a dispute over one transfer. Every other part of that team was replaced, and the machine kept running. The one part nobody could replace was the one Bernabéu refused to let go.
