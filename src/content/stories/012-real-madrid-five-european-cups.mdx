Ten minutes into the first European Cup final, in Paris in June 1956, Real Madrid were losing 2–0 to Stade de Reims.

They won 4–3.

They would not lose a European Cup final for five years. Alfredo Di Stéfano would score in every one.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Di Stéfano was nearly a Barcelona player. For a while, he was meant to be both.

The Argentine had left River Plate for Millonarios of Bogotá. In 1953 Barcelona dealt with River, Real Madrid with Millonarios. Each club believed it owned him.

The Spanish federation ruled that he should alternate seasons.

Barcelona's president accepted, and was so criticised he resigned. Within weeks Barcelona withdrew. Why is still argued over.

Madrid's president, Santiago Bernabéu, had seen him in a Millonarios friendly and decided on the spot.

Then came the part that surprised me.

In the Reims attack that day was Raymond Kopa, the best French footballer of his generation. Days earlier, he had agreed to join Real Madrid. He played his last Reims match against his next employers.

Madrid kept winning. Fiorentina in 1957. Milan in 1958, after extra time. Reims again in 1959, with Kopa now in white.

In 1960, at Hampden Park, they beat Eintracht Frankfurt 7–3. Di Stéfano scored three and Ferenc Puskás four.

Yet in Madrid's fourth and fifth winning seasons, Barcelona won the Spanish league. In 1960–61 they became the first team to knock Madrid out of the European Cup.

By 1960 the man on the bench was Miguel Muñoz, who as captain had lifted the trophy in 1956.

Di Stéfano was the constant: a centre-forward who defended, organised and finished.

Francisco Gento, the left winger, outlasted everyone. He won a sixth European Cup in 1966, the only player to win six.

Here's what stays with me. A dynasty hung on one disputed transfer. Every other part of that team was replaced, and the machine kept running. The one part nobody could replace was the one Bernabéu refused to let go.
