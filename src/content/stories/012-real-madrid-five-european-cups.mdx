Ten minutes into the first European Cup final, at the Parc des Princes in Paris on 13 June 1956, Real Madrid were losing 2–0. Michel Leblond had scored for Stade de Reims after six minutes and Jean Templin after ten, and the French champions, playing in their own capital, were threatening to turn a new competition into a home party. Somewhere in the Reims forward line was Raymond Kopa, the best French footballer of his generation. A few days earlier he had agreed to join Real Madrid. He was playing his last match for Reims against his next employers.

What happened in the next 70 minutes set the pattern for the next five years. Alfredo Di Stéfano pulled one back after 14 minutes. Héctor Rial equalised before the half-hour. Michel Hidalgo restored Reims' lead after 62 minutes, and within five minutes the defender Marquitos had levelled again. Rial scored the winner 11 minutes from time. Real Madrid 4, Reims 3, in front of a crowd that the club puts at 40,000 and other records at about 38,000. Miguel Muñoz, the captain, became the first man to lift the trophy that is now the Champions League.

Real Madrid would not lose a European Cup final for five years. They would not lose a European Cup tie until the autumn of 1960. And the man who scored first in Paris would score in every one of the five finals.

## The argument over one player

The team that won in Paris had been assembled around a single transfer, and that transfer had nearly gone to Barcelona.

Di Stéfano was an Argentine centre-forward who had left River Plate for Millonarios of Bogotá in 1949. River still considered him theirs, and in 1953 two Spanish clubs went after him by two different doors. Barcelona negotiated with River Plate and agreed a deal. Real Madrid negotiated with Millonarios and bought the rights Millonarios claimed. Each club believed it owned him.

The Spanish federation's solution was one of the oddest rulings in football history: Di Stéfano would play for Real Madrid in 1953–54 and 1955–56 and for Barcelona in the seasons in between. The Barcelona president, Enric Martí Carreto, signed up to it and was criticised so heavily at home that he announced his resignation on 22 September 1953. Within weeks Barcelona withdrew their claim, and Di Stéfano belonged to Real Madrid outright.

How that happened is still argued about. Barcelona's own history says FIFA regarded their claim as legally sound and that the federation, under pressure from the state and from Real Madrid, blocked the move. Madrid-based writers, among them Alfredo Relaño, who later helped Di Stéfano write his memoirs, reject the idea that the Franco regime stole the player. Both clubs agree on the outcome. That October, in his first clásico, Di Stéfano scored in a 5–0 win over Barcelona.

## Bernabéu's machine

The man who wanted him most was Santiago Bernabéu, the club's president, a man who thought in decades. He had first seen Di Stéfano when Millonarios played a friendly against Madrid and, by the accounts of people around him, decided on the spot that he wanted him.

Bernabéu had already built the stage. Real Madrid's new stadium in Chamartín opened on 14 December 1947, and after a first expansion took its capacity to about 125,000, the club's general assembly voted in January 1955 to give it his name. A stadium that size needed opponents that big, and in December 1954 the French sports paper L'Équipe supplied them. Its journalist Gabriel Hanot, irritated by English newspapers declaring Wolverhampton Wanderers champions of the world, proposed a real competition between Europe's champions. Club representatives met in Paris on 2 and 3 April 1955 to agree the rules, and on 21 June UEFA agreed to run it. Real Madrid entered the first edition.

What Bernabéu built in those years was less a team than a system for renewing one. The coaches changed: José Villalonga, who was 36 when he won the 1956 final and remains the youngest coach to win the competition, took the first two titles, and by 1960 the man on the bench was Muñoz, the 1956 captain, who became the first person to win the European Cup as both player and coach. The supporting cast changed too. Kopa arrived from Reims in 1956 and won the Ballon d'Or in 1958, the first Frenchman to do so. Ferenc Puskás came in 1958, a story told elsewhere in this series. Two things stayed the same: Di Stéfano at the centre, and Francisco Gento, a winger of astonishing speed, on the left.

> He was somebody I really respected, having watched him from the stands at the Bernabeu and then played against him.
> — Bobby Charlton, speaking to Manchester United's website on Di Stéfano's death, 7 July 2014

## Five finals

The second final was the easiest to arrange. UEFA gave the 1957 final to the Bernabéu itself, and Real Madrid beat Fiorentina 2–0 at home on 30 May, Di Stéfano scoring from the penalty spot after 70 minutes and Gento adding a second six minutes later.

The third was the hardest. In Brussels on 28 May 1958, against a Milan side including Juan Alberto Schiaffino, Madrid fell behind twice. Schiaffino scored after 59 minutes and Di Stéfano equalised; Grillo scored after 77 and Rial equalised two minutes later. It became the first European Cup final to go to extra time, and in the 107th minute Gento's low shot gave Madrid a 3–2 win.

The fourth was a rematch. On 3 June 1959 at the Neckarstadion in Stuttgart, Madrid met Reims again, and this time Kopa was in white, lining up against his old club and its centre-forward Just Fontaine. Mateos scored after two minutes and Di Stéfano after 47. 2–0.

The fifth was the one people remember: the 7–3 win over Eintracht Frankfurt at Hampden Park on 18 May 1960, with three goals from Di Stéfano and four from Puskás. Di Stéfano had now scored in five successive finals, seven goals in all, a record that only Puskás, two years later, would match.

There was a detail inside the triumph that is easy to miss. In the same two seasons that Madrid won their fourth and fifth European Cups, they did not win the Spanish league. Helenio Herrera's Barcelona took the 1958–59 title and the 1959–60 title too, the second on goal average after the two clubs finished level on 46 points. Madrid's dominance was a European fact long before it was a domestic one. And it was Barcelona, in the first round of the 1960–61 competition, who finally ended it, drawing 2–2 and winning 2–1, 4–3 on aggregate, to become the first team to knock Real Madrid out of the European Cup.

## The turn

The decision that explains the five years was Bernabéu's refusal to let Di Stéfano go in 1953, whatever the federation, Barcelona or anyone else thought. Every other piece of the machine was replaceable, and was replaced. The coaches came and went, Kopa came from Reims, Puskás arrived only for the last two finals. Di Stéfano was the constant: a centre-forward who defended, organised and dictated the tempo from deep before finishing the move himself. France Football named him its player of the year in 1957 and 1959, and in 1989 gave him a one-off Super Ballon d'Or as the best of all its past winners.

When UEFA gave him its first President's Award in Madrid in February 2008, Michel Platini found the vocabulary of the 1950s still fitted.

> A god of the stadium, a magician of the ball, a perfect football master.
> — Michel Platini, presenting the UEFA President's Award to Di Stéfano, Madrid, February 2008

Gento outlasted everyone. He was in the side that won the sixth European Cup in 1966 and remains the only player to have won the competition six times.

## What it tells us

Dynasties are built around one irreplaceable player and a structure that replaces everyone else. Real Madrid's first great team had both, and the irreplaceable part was secured by a president who treated a transfer dispute as a matter of the club's future rather than its accounts. It also tells us that the European Cup was, from its first final, a competition shaped by Madrid as much as it was won by them.
