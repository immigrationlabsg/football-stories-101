On 13 November 1999, Paul Scholes scored twice at Hampden Park. England beat Scotland 2–0 in the first leg of their Euro 2000 play-off.

On 13 November 2017, Gianluigi Buffon was in tears at San Siro. Italy had drawn 0–0 with Sweden and were out of the World Cup.

Same date. Same kind of night. Two opposite endings, 18 years apart.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

England lost the second leg.

Four days after Hampden, Scotland came to Wembley and won 1–0. It was not enough. England went through 2–1 on aggregate.

Both of England's goals in the tie were Scholes's. Both came on the same night in Glasgow, in the first half.

In a tie England won by a single goal, he had scored every one they needed.

Italy's tie went the other way, and it was settled just as early.

Sweden won the first leg 1–0 at home. That goal turned out to be the only one in 180 minutes.

At San Siro, Italy needed one goal. They never found it. Sweden were going to the World Cup instead.

Then came the part that surprised me.

The two teams finished their play-offs with the same defensive record. England conceded once. So did Italy.

The whole difference was at the other end. England scored twice. Italy did not score at all, not once in three hours of football.

And the price of that was enormous. Italy had not missed a World Cup since 1958. Sixty years of World Cups ended on a night when they did not concede a goal.

That same night, Buffon announced his retirement from international football.

Here's what stays with me.

A play-off is two matches, and you can lose one of them and still go through.

Two goals in one evening can carry a whole tie.

A clean sheet, on its own, can still send you home.

One night is remembered for two goals, the other for none.
