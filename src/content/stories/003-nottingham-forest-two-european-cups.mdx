On the stroke of half-time in Munich's Olympiastadion on 30 May 1979, John Robertson, a left winger who looked as if he had been dressed by a charity shop and moved as if the ball were on a string, worked the ball out on the left against Malmö and curled a cross to the far post. Arriving at full stretch was Trevor Francis, who three months earlier had become the first British footballer to cost £1 million. He met it with a diving header. Nottingham Forest 1, Malmö 0. It stayed that way, and a club that had been in the Second Division two years earlier were champions of Europe.

That sentence still reads like a misprint. It was built, in a little over four years, by two men who had already done something almost as unlikely 15 miles up the road at Derby.

## January 1975

When Brian Clough began work at the City Ground on 6 January 1975, Nottingham Forest were, by Daniel Taylor's account in I Believe in Miracles, 13th in the Second Division and five points clear of the relegation places. Clough had won the First Division with Derby County in 1972, walked out in 1973, then spent a short spell at Brighton and Hove Albion before leaving them for Leeds United, where he lasted 44 days. He was famous, out of work and, by the standards of the people who ran English football, dangerous.

His first 18 months at Forest were ordinary. The team finished 16th and then, in his first full season, eighth. What changed everything was the arrival in July 1976 of Peter Taylor, his former assistant at Derby, who had stayed on to manage Brighton when Clough went to Leeds. Taylor was the scout and the judge of character; Clough was the voice and the authority. Clough himself is widely quoted as saying that he was not equipped to manage successfully without Taylor, and that he was the shop window while Taylor was the goods in the back. Nobody has ever pinned down where he first said it, but the line was good enough for Taylor's publishers to put it on his memoir.

Taylor's eye went to work immediately. In 1976 Forest brought in the centre-half Larry Lloyd from Coventry City, and they found the forward Garry Birtles playing non-league football for Long Eaton United. In 1976–77 Forest finished third in the Second Division, which in those days was the last promotion place, a single point ahead of Bolton Wanderers and Blackpool. They did not even clinch it on the pitch. Their season was over when Bolton, who could have overtaken them, lost to the champions Wolverhampton Wanderers. By one account the Forest players heard the news as their holiday flight landed in Palma.

## The season nobody expected

Promoted clubs are supposed to survive. Forest won the league. Before the season Clough and Taylor made one signing, £150,000 on Kenny Burns, a Birmingham City forward with a reputation for trouble whom they intended to rebuild as a centre-back. In September they spent money in a way that told the rest of the division they were serious: a record fee for a goalkeeper on Peter Shilton from relegated Stoke City, and a much smaller one on the Scotland midfielder Archie Gemmill from Derby.

The title was won by seven points from Liverpool, the reigning European champions, and the gap was the smallest part of the achievement. From a 1–0 defeat at Leeds on 19 November 1977 to a 2–0 defeat at Anfield on 9 December 1978, Forest went 42 First Division matches without losing. Nobody beat that record until Arsenal in August 2004. They also won the League Cup that spring, after a 0–0 draw at Wembley, beating Liverpool 1–0 in the replay at Old Trafford with a Robertson penalty. Shilton was cup-tied, having played in the competition for Stoke, so Forest's goalkeeper for the whole run was an 18-year-old reserve called Chris Woods, whose League Cup games that season turned out to be his only senior matches for the club.

The core of the team had cost almost nothing. Robertson, Viv Anderson, Tony Woodcock and Ian Bowyer were already at the club when Clough arrived. What Clough and Taylor added was not so much talent as a way of seeing it. Burns, the problem forward, was voted Footballer of the Year as a centre-half in 1978. Robertson, whom Clough himself had once dismissed as scruffy, unfit and uninterested, became the player every move went through. Clough later called him a Picasso of our game, and one of the finest deliverers of a football he had ever seen.

## Europe, twice

As champions, Forest entered the 1978–79 European Cup, and the first-round draw paired them with Liverpool, the holders, who had won the previous two finals. Forest won the first leg 2–0 at the City Ground, with goals from Birtles and Colin Barrett, and drew 0–0 at Anfield. From there they beat AEK Athens 7–2 on aggregate and Grasshoppers of Zurich 5–2. The semi-final against Cologne was the tie that nearly ended it. On 11 April 1979 Forest were 2–0 down at home after 20 minutes, came back through Birtles, Bowyer and Robertson to lead 3–2, and then conceded a late equaliser to the Japanese forward Yasuhiko Okudera that most people treated as decisive. In Cologne a fortnight later, Bowyer scored the only goal of the second leg after 65 minutes.

In February 1979 Clough had signed Francis from Birmingham City. Clough later wrote that he set the fee at £999,999 so that being the first £1 million player would not go to Francis's head; with VAT and the transfer levy, the bill came to well over £1 million. Because he signed mid-season, Francis was not eligible for the earlier rounds, so the final in Munich was his first European Cup match.

A year later they did it again, and the second run was harder. They lost the first leg of the quarter-final at home to Dynamo Berlin and won the return 3–1 in East Berlin, with two goals from Francis. They beat Ajax 2–0 at home in the semi-final, Francis scoring the first, and held on to lose only 1–0 in Amsterdam. Then Francis injured his Achilles tendon and missed the final, his place going to the 18-year-old Gary Mills.

The final was played at the Santiago Bernabéu in Madrid on 28 May 1980 against Hamburg, whose team was built around Kevin Keegan. Forest's plan was unapologetic: sit deep, let Hamburg have the ball, trust Shilton, and wait for Robertson. After about 20 minutes (the reports differ by a few minutes) he cut inside the Hamburg full-back Manfred Kaltz and scored. For the rest of the night Hamburg attacked and Shilton saved everything they had. 1–0 again.

## The turn

The decision that explains it all was Clough's choice, in the summer of 1976, to bring Taylor back to his side. Together they had a way of looking at footballers that the rest of English football did not share: they bought players with flaws they thought they could fix, turned a troublesome forward into the best defender in the country and a winger written off as unfit into the side's creative heart, and they were right often enough to win the league with a promoted side.

The partnership did not last. Taylor retired from Forest in 1982, came back that November to manage Derby, and in 1983 took Robertson there. That transfer was the final straw, and the two men never spoke again. Taylor died in October 1990. Clough stayed at Forest until 1993, managing more than 1,100 matches in 18 years, and retired in the season the club was relegated. He died on 20 September 2004, aged 69. Francis died in July 2023, and Robertson on Christmas Day 2025, aged 72.

## What it tells us

Forest did not win two European Cups because of money. They won because two people with an unusual eye were allowed to judge players by what they could become rather than by what they had been, and because a squad that had been told it was ordinary chose to believe the one voice that said otherwise. It tells us that the gap between a mid-table Second Division club and the best team in Europe was, for a few years, mostly a question of who was in the dugout.
