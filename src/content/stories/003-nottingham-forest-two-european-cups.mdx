On the stroke of half-time in Munich on 30 May 1979, John Robertson worked the ball out on the left and curled a cross to the far post.

Trevor Francis arrived at full stretch and met it with a diving header.

Nottingham Forest 1, Malmö 0.

It stayed that way. A club that had been in the Second Division two years earlier were champions of Europe.

That sentence still reads like a misprint.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The missing piece was not a player.

Brian Clough started work at the City Ground on 6 January 1975 with Forest, by Daniel Taylor's account, 13th in the Second Division.

Clough was famous and out of work. He had won the league with Derby County, walked out, gone to Brighton and then lasted 44 days at Leeds United.

His first 18 months at Forest were ordinary. Sixteenth, then eighth.

In July 1976 Peter Taylor, his former assistant at Derby, came from Brighton to rejoin him.

Taylor was the scout and the judge of character. Clough was the voice.

Clough is widely quoted as saying he could not manage successfully without Taylor: he was the shop window, Taylor the goods in the back. Nobody has pinned down where he first said it.

Taylor's eye went to work at once. Forest found Garry Birtles playing non-league football for Long Eaton United.

In 1976–77 they finished third, the last promotion place, a single point ahead of Bolton and Blackpool.

They did not even clinch it on the pitch. Their season was already over when Bolton lost to Wolves. By one account, the players heard the news as their holiday flight landed in Palma.

Promoted clubs are supposed to survive.

Forest won the league.

They signed Kenny Burns, a Birmingham forward with a reputation for trouble, and turned him into a centre-half. He was voted Footballer of the Year.

They won the title by seven points from Liverpool, the European champions.

Then came the part that surprised me.

They also won the League Cup that spring, and their goalkeeper for the whole run was an 18-year-old reserve called Chris Woods.

Peter Shilton was cup-tied. Those League Cup games turned out to be Woods's only senior matches for the club.

Between November 1977 and December 1978 they went 42 league games unbeaten, a record nobody beat until Arsenal in 2004.

The core of the team had cost almost nothing. Robertson was already there when Clough arrived.

Clough himself had once dismissed him as scruffy, unfit and uninterested. He became the player every move went through.

As Clough later put it, Robertson was "a Picasso of our game".

In Europe the first-round draw gave them Liverpool, the holders. Forest won 2–0 at home and drew 0–0 at Anfield.

In the semi-final they drew 3–3 at home to Cologne and most people treated the tie as lost. Ian Bowyer scored the only goal in Germany.

Francis, signed for a fee Clough set at £999,999 so it would not go to his head, was ineligible for the earlier rounds.

The final was his first European Cup match. He won it with that header.

A year later they did it again.

Francis was injured and missed the final. Against Hamburg in Madrid, Forest sat deep, trusted Shilton and waited for Robertson.

After about 20 minutes, the reports differ by a few, Robertson cut inside the full-back Manfred Kaltz and scored.

1–0 again.

The partnership did not last. In 1983 Taylor, by then managing Derby, took Robertson there, and the two men never spoke again.

Here's what stays with me.

Forest did not win because of money. They won because two people were allowed to judge players by what they could become.

A scruffy winger, a troublesome forward, an 18-year-old reserve.

For a few years, the gap between mid-table in the Second Division and the best team in Europe was mostly a question of who sat in the dugout.
