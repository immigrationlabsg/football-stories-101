The clock in Munich's Olympiastadion showed 45 minutes on 30 May 1979 when John Robertson, a left winger who looked as if he had been dressed by a charity shop and moved as if the ball were on a string, drifted past two Malmö defenders near the corner flag. He did not cross early. He waited, shaped to go inside, and then curled the ball to the far post with his left foot. Arriving at full stretch, a yard from the goal line, was Trevor Francis, who three months earlier had become the first British footballer to cost £1 million. He met the cross with his head and tumbled into the shot-put circle behind the goal. Nottingham Forest 1, Malmö 0. It stayed that way, and a club from a city of 280,000 people, which had been in the Second Division two years earlier, were champions of Europe.

That sentence still reads like a misprint. It was built, in a little over four years, by two men who had already done something almost as unlikely 15 miles up the road.

## January 1975

When Brian Clough walked into the City Ground on 6 January 1975, Nottingham Forest were 13th in the Second Division and had the look of a club that had stopped expecting anything. Clough had won the First Division with Derby County in 1972, resigned in a fury in 1973, lasted 44 days at Leeds United in 1974 and then spent an unhappy few months at Brighton. He was 39, famous, unemployed and, by the standards of the people who ran English football, dangerous.

His first 18 months at Forest were ordinary. The team finished 16th and then eighth. What changed everything was the arrival in July 1976 of Peter Taylor, his former partner at Derby, who had stayed behind at Brighton when Clough left. Taylor was the scout and the judge of character; Clough was the voice and the authority. Neither man ever pretended the arrangement was equal in public, and neither ever worked as well without the other.

> I'm not equipped to manage successfully without Brian Clough. I am the shop window and he's the goods in the back.
> — Peter Taylor

Taylor's eye went to work immediately. He brought in the centre-half Larry Lloyd from Coventry, found the forward Garry Birtles playing for Long Eaton United for a fee of about £2,000, and persuaded Clough that Kenny Burns, a Birmingham City forward with a reputation for trouble, could be rebuilt as a centre-back. In 1976–77 Forest finished third in the Second Division, which in those days was the last promotion place. They won it by a single point over Bolton Wanderers, and they did not even win it on the pitch. Bolton lost their final match to Wolves while the Forest squad were on an end-of-season holiday in Mallorca. The players heard they had gone up in a bar.

## The season nobody expected

Promoted clubs are supposed to survive. Forest won the league. They began 1977–78 with a 3–1 win at Everton, and in September Clough and Taylor spent money in a way that told the rest of the division they were serious: £250,000, a record for a goalkeeper, on Peter Shilton from Stoke City, and a smaller fee on the Scotland midfielder Archie Gemmill from Derby. Shilton conceded 18 goals in 37 league games.

The title was won by seven points from Liverpool, the reigning European champions, and the gap was the smallest part of the achievement. From a defeat at Leeds on 19 November 1977 to a defeat at Liverpool on 9 December 1978, Forest went 42 First Division matches without losing. No English club had done it before, and none did it again until Arsenal in 2004. They also won the League Cup that spring, beating Liverpool 1–0 in a replay at Old Trafford with a Robertson penalty, after Shilton, cup-tied, had been replaced by an 18-year-old called Chris Woods who had never played a league game.

The core of the team had cost almost nothing. Robertson, Martin O'Neill, Viv Anderson, Tony Woodcock and Ian Bowyer were already at the club when Clough arrived. Frank Clark had come on a free transfer from Newcastle at 31. What Clough and Taylor added was not so much talent as a way of seeing it: Burns, the problem forward, was the Football Writers' Footballer of the Year as a centre-half in 1978, and Robertson, whom previous managers had treated as an overweight nuisance, became the player every move went through.

> John Robertson was a very unattractive young man. If one day I felt a bit off colour, I would sit next to him. I was bloody Errol Flynn in comparison. But give him a ball and a yard of grass and he was an artist, the Picasso of our game.
> — Brian Clough, Clough: The Autobiography, 1994

## Europe, twice

As champions, Forest entered the 1978–79 European Cup, and the draw for the first round put them against Liverpool, the holders, who had won the previous two finals. Forest won the first leg 2–0 at the City Ground, with goals from Birtles and Colin Barrett, and drew 0–0 at Anfield. From there they beat AEK Athens 7–2 on aggregate and Grasshoppers of Zurich 5–2. The semi-final against Cologne was the tie that nearly ended it: 2–0 down at home inside 20 minutes, Forest came back to 3–3, only for the Japanese forward Yasuhiko Okudera to score a late equaliser that most people treated as decisive. In Cologne, Bowyer headed the only goal of the second leg after 65 minutes.

In February 1979 Clough had signed Francis from Birmingham City. He announced the fee as £999,999, he said, so that the player would not get a swollen head about being a million-pound footballer; the taxes and levies took it past the mark anyway. Francis was not eligible for the earlier rounds, so the final in Munich was his European debut. His header from Robertson's cross was the only time he touched the ball in the Malmö penalty area all night.

A year later they did it again, and the second run was harder. They lost the first leg of the quarter-final at home to Dynamo Berlin and won the return 3–1 in East Berlin, with two goals from Francis and a Robertson penalty. They beat the Ajax of the day 2–0 at home in the semi-final and held on to lose only 1–0 in Amsterdam. Then, a month before the final, Francis ruptured an Achilles tendon against Crystal Palace and was out.

The final was played at the Santiago Bernabéu in Madrid on 28 May 1980 against Hamburg, whose team was built around Kevin Keegan, the twice European Footballer of the Year. Forest's plan was unapologetic: sit deep, let Hamburg have the ball, trust Shilton, and wait for Robertson. In the 20th minute he exchanged passes with Birtles on the edge of the area, cut inside on to his weaker right foot and hit a low shot inside the far post. For the remaining 70 minutes Hamburg attacked and Shilton, Burns and Lloyd held. 1–0 again.

## The turn

The decision that explains it all was Clough's choice, in the summer of 1976, to swallow his pride and ask Taylor to join him. Clough on his own had been a brilliant motivator with a patchy eye for a player. Taylor on his own had been a journeyman. Together they had a way of looking at footballers that the rest of English football did not share: they bought character flaws they thought they could fix and sold reputations they thought were inflated, and they were right often enough to win the league with a promoted side.

The partnership did not last. Taylor retired in 1982, then came back to manage Derby and signed Robertson from Forest without telling Clough, and the two men never spoke again. Taylor died in October 1990. Clough stayed at Forest until 1993, when the club was relegated and he retired. He died in September 2004, and the two cities he had taken to the title named a road between them after him. Francis died in July 2023. Robertson, Shilton, Birtles, Burns and Anderson are still regularly asked to explain how it happened, and none of them has ever managed to make it sound plausible.

## What it tells us

Forest did not win two European Cups because of money, because they spent less than most of the teams they beat. They won because two people with an unusual eye were allowed to judge players by what they could become rather than by what they had been, and because a squad that had been told it was ordinary chose to believe the one voice that said otherwise. It tells us that the gap between a mid-table Second Division club and the best team in Europe was, for a few years, mostly a question of who was in the dugout.
