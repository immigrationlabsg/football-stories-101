In July 2000, Florentino Pérez was standing for president of Real Madrid, and he was not expected to win.

So he made a promise. If elected, he would sign Luís Figo, Barcelona's best player.

If he failed, he said, he would pay the members' fees for a season himself.

He won. Eight days later, Figo was unveiled in Madrid.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Barcelona could not stop it. Figo's contract had a buy-out clause of 10 billion pesetas, and Madrid paid it. It was a world-record fee.

That year Figo also won the Ballon d'Or.

The man he beat, Lorenzo Sanz, had just won two Champions Leagues in three seasons. The Figo promise helped beat him.

Pérez kept buying. Zinedine Zidane came from Juventus in 2001, Ronaldo from Inter in 2002, David Beckham from Manchester United in 2003.

The plan had a name: Zidanes y Pavones. World stars would play alongside academy graduates such as Francisco Pavón, a young centre-back.

At first it worked. Madrid won the league in 2001, the Champions League in 2002 and the league again in 2003.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Within two days of that 2003 title, the club dismissed its coach, Vicente del Bosque.

That summer, after Beckham arrived, Claude Makélélé, the midfielder who won the ball back for the stars, left for Chelsea. He won the league there in 2005 and 2006.

Zidane saw the problem. As he put it: "Why put another layer of gold paint on the Bentley when you are losing the entire engine?"

By early 2006, Madrid had won no major trophy since that 2003 title.

In February, Arsenal won 1–0 at the Bernabéu, through a goal by Thierry Henry. Days later Madrid lost 2–1 at Mallorca and fell 10 points behind Barcelona.

On 27 February, Pérez resigned, saying the club needed a change of direction.

Here's what stays with me.

One promise can build a team in a single summer. Stars are easy to buy. The player who makes them look good is the one you notice only when he has gone.
