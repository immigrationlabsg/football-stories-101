In the tunnel in Lisbon, minutes before the European Cup final, Bertie Auld started to sing.

It was 25 May 1967. Across the tunnel stood Inter, champions of Europe in 1964 and 1965. Auld later remembered them looking immaculate, more like gods than footballers.

Celtic had never played in a European final.

Auld began the Celtic Song. Within a minute or two his team-mates had joined in, and the Italians looked baffled.

Ninety minutes later, the singers were the first British team to win the European Cup.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Every man in Celtic's starting eleven was born within about 30 miles of Celtic Park.

Of the 15-man squad, all but two were born within ten miles of the ground.

Ronnie Simpson, the goalkeeper, was born in Glasgow. Bobby Murdoch came from Rutherglen, just down the road. Billy McNeill, the captain, was born in Bellshill.

Tommy Gemmell, from Motherwell, was one exception, at roughly 11 miles.

The other was Bobby Lennox, from Saltcoats on the Ayrshire coast, about 30 miles away. Football365 once called him, affectionately, the side's foreigner.

Take Lennox out, and the champions of Europe were drawn from a bus ride.

The man who shaped them was a local too.

Jock Stein was born in Burnbank, near Hamilton, in 1922. He went down the pits as a teenager and played part-time while he worked as a miner.

In 1951 Celtic signed him from Llanelli Town in Wales.

When an ankle injury ended his playing days, Celtic let him coach the reserves and youth players.

There was a ceiling, though. Celtic was a club of Irish Catholic roots, and Stein was a Protestant.

He left to manage Dunfermline, then Hibernian. Meanwhile Celtic drifted. They had not won a major trophy since the League Cup of 1957.

But here's where it gets interesting.

In March 1965 Stein came back as manager, the first Protestant in the club's history.

It took him six weeks to win something. On 24 April Celtic beat Dunfermline 3–2 in the Scottish Cup final.

The next season they won the league, the first of nine titles in a row.

By the time they reached Lisbon, Celtic had already won the league, the Scottish Cup, the League Cup and the Glasgow Cup. The European Cup would make it five out of five.

That was why the singing came so naturally. As Auld explained, the players sang on the bus to every game. It was what came to him.

Jim Craig, Celtic's right-back, said many years later that the Italians' faces seemed to ask what on earth they had been drawn against.

Helenio Herrera's Inter defended deep and struck on the counter, the style Italians called catenaccio. And they arrived without Luis Suárez and Jair da Costa, both injured.

In the seventh minute Craig brought down Renato Cappellini, and Sandro Mazzola scored the penalty.

Then Inter did what Inter did. They dropped men behind the ball and dared Celtic to find a way through.

For an hour Celtic could not. Giuliano Sarti, the Inter goalkeeper, kept stopping them.

Stein's answer to the best defence in Europe was to send his full-backs forward.

In the 63rd minute Craig, the right-back, rolled the ball across the edge of the area. Gemmell, the left-back, met it first time. 1–1.

In the 84th minute Gemmell found Murdoch, whose long shot was deflected in by Stevie Chalmers.

Celtic 2, Inter 1.

As Stein put it afterwards: "We did it by playing football. Pure, beautiful, inventive football."

Supporters poured on to the pitch. The presentation stand was on the far side of the stadium from the dressing rooms, and in the chaos McNeill was separated from his team.

He climbed up and lifted the trophy alone. Years later he said the only disappointment of the day was that his team-mates were not beside him.

Here's what stays with me.

A team found almost entirely within a few miles of its own ground beat the champions of Europe. Their manager was a former miner who had once coached the club's reserves. Nobody has done it like that since, and it is hard to imagine anyone ever will.
