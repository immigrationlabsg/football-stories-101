In the tunnel of the Estádio Nacional in Lisbon on 25 May 1967, two teams stood side by side waiting to walk out for the European Cup final. On one side were Inter, champions of Europe in 1964 and 1965, players whom Bertie Auld later remembered as looking immaculate, more like gods than footballers. On the other side were Celtic, a team that had never played in a European final. Then Auld began to sing the Celtic Song. Within a minute or two the rest of his team-mates had joined in.

The Italians looked baffled. Jim Craig, Celtic's right-back, said many years later that the faces of the Inter players seemed to ask what on earth they had been drawn against. Auld's explanation was simple. The players sang on the bus to every game. It was what came to him. Ninety minutes later the singers were the first British team to win the European Cup.

## Eleven from round the corner

What makes the Lisbon Lions unlike any other European champions is not the score or the opponent but the map. Every man in the starting eleven was born within about 30 miles of Celtic Park, and most of them a great deal closer than that. Of the wider squad of 15, all but two were born within ten miles of the ground. Ronnie Simpson, the goalkeeper, was born in Glasgow. Bobby Murdoch came from Rutherglen, just down the road from Parkhead. Billy McNeill, the captain, was born in Bellshill in Lanarkshire. Tommy Gemmell, from Motherwell, was one of the two exceptions, at roughly 11 miles. The other was Bobby Lennox, born in Saltcoats on the Ayrshire coast, about 30 miles away. Football365 once called him, affectionately, the side's foreigner.

The precise version matters, because the story is usually told more loosely. The famous line is that the team was "born within 30 miles of Parkhead". That holds only because of Lennox: take him out and the radius shrinks to a bus ride. In an age of global scouting, it is hard to imagine a champion of Europe drawn from so small a patch of ground again.

This was not a team assembled from across a continent. It was a team from Glasgow and its surroundings, and the man who shaped it was a Lanarkshire man, like several of his players.

## The reserve-team coach

John Stein, known in football as Jock, was born in Burnbank, near Hamilton, on 5 October 1922. He went down the pits as a teenager and played part-time football while he worked as a miner. By 1950 he was a footballer of modest reputation at Llanelli Town in Wales, which is about as far from the centre of the game as a Scottish footballer could get. In 1951 Celtic brought him back to Scotland.

He became far more than a signing to fill a gap. Stein was in the team that won a league and Scottish Cup double in 1954. Then a persistent ankle injury ended his playing career, and Celtic gave him the job of coaching the reserve and youth players. For a few years he worked with the club's youngest footballers, learning the trade away from the spotlight.

There was a ceiling, though. Celtic was a club of Irish Catholic roots, and Stein was a Protestant. In 1960 he left to manage Dunfermline Athletic, and in 1964 he moved to Hibernian. Meanwhile his old club drifted. Celtic had not won a major trophy since the League Cup final of 1957. They lost the League Cup final of October 1964 to Rangers. In January 1965 the chairman, Robert Kelly, announced that Stein was coming back, and in March he took charge, the first Protestant manager in the club's history.

It took him six weeks to win something. On 24 April 1965 Celtic beat Dunfermline 3–2 in the Scottish Cup final at Hampden Park. Auld scored twice, and McNeill won it in the 81st minute with a header from a corner. It was the club's first senior trophy in seven and a half years. The next season Celtic won the league, the first of nine titles in a row.

## Lisbon, 25 May 1967

By the time Celtic reached Lisbon they had already won the Scottish League, the Scottish Cup, the League Cup and the Glasgow Cup. The European Cup would make it five out of five, a clean sweep of every competition they entered that season.

Inter were the opposite of Celtic in almost every way. Their coach, Helenio Herrera, had built two European Cup-winning sides on a fierce, deep defence and fast counter-attacks, the style Italian football called catenaccio. But they arrived in Portugal weakened. Luis Suárez, their Spanish playmaker, had been ruled out with injury, and the Brazilian winger Jair da Costa, who had scored the only goal of the 1965 final, was missing as well. Suárez said decades later that neither of them played that night.

The final began as if Herrera's script would hold. In the seventh minute Craig brought down Renato Cappellini in the penalty area, and Sandro Mazzola scored from the spot. Inter did what Inter did: they dropped men behind the ball and dared Celtic to find a way through.

For an hour Celtic could not. They attacked relentlessly, and the Inter goalkeeper Giuliano Sarti, outstanding all evening, kept stopping them. Then, in the 63rd minute, Craig made amends. He ran on to the ball on the right and rolled it across the edge of the penalty area, where Gemmell, a full-back who should, by Italian logic, have been nowhere near the place, met it first time with a fierce shot from the edge of the area. 1–1.

The winner came in the 84th minute. Gemmell was given space again on the left and played the ball to Murdoch, whose long-range shot was deflected past Sarti by Stevie Chalmers in front of goal. Celtic 2, Inter 1.

At the final whistle Celtic supporters poured on to the pitch. The presentation stand was on the opposite side of the stadium from the dressing rooms, and in the chaos McNeill was separated from his team-mates. He climbed up and lifted the trophy without them. Years later he said the only disappointment of the whole day was that they were not beside him to share it.

> There is not a prouder man on God's earth than me at this moment.
> — Jock Stein, Lisbon, 25 May 1967, as reported by The Scotsman

Bill Shankly, the Liverpool manager and Stein's friend, had travelled to watch. He found Stein afterwards and told him he was immortal. Accounts of the exact words differ, but the meaning has never been in dispute.

## The turn

The decision that explains Lisbon was Stein's choice to attack a team built to absorb attacks. Inter were the best defensive side in Europe, and the orthodox way to play them was to be patient and avoid conceding. Stein sent full-backs forward. The equaliser came from one full-back passing to the other, and the winner began with that same left-back, Gemmell, finding Murdoch for the shot Chalmers turned in. Celtic beat catenaccio not by out-thinking it but by out-numbering it, again and again, until something gave.

> We did it by playing football. Pure, beautiful, inventive football.
> — Jock Stein, after the final in Lisbon, 25 May 1967

The other half of the explanation is Stein himself: a former miner, a journeyman footballer and a former reserve-team coach who knew Celtic from the inside. He did not import a team. He took local footballers and asked them to play braver football than anyone in Europe. He died on 10 September 1985 in Cardiff, at a Scotland match, while managing his country.

## What it tells us

The Lisbon Lions are a reminder that the best teams are sometimes found rather than bought. Stein did not assemble a squad from across a continent; he looked at the players already in front of him and asked them to play with more courage than their opponents. That a side from one corner of Scotland could do it at all is the wonder. That nobody has done it since is the lesson.
