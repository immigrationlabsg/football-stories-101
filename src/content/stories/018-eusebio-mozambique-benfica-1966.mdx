In December 1960, Benfica flew an 18-year-old from Mozambique to Lisbon and gave him a codename: Ruth.

Then they hid him.

He was taken to a fishing town in the Algarve and kept out of sight in a hotel, with people watching over him.

The boy was Eusébio da Silva Ferreira. Five years later he won the Ballon d'Or.

So why would a club hide its new signing?

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Another club was sure he was already theirs.

Eusébio was born on 25 January 1942 in Mafalala, a poor district of Lourenço Marques, now Maputo. He grew up playing barefoot with balls made of rolled-up newspaper and old socks.

At 15 he joined Sporting Clube de Lourenço Marques. The name mattered. It was a nursery club of Sporting in Lisbon, and Sporting assumed that if the boy was any good, he would one day be theirs.

Word of him reached Benfica in a barber's shop.

José Carlos Bauer, a former Brazil international, saw Eusébio while touring Mozambique with a Brazilian club side. His own club could not afford him.

Some time later Bauer ran into his old coach Béla Guttmann, by now in charge of Benfica, at a barber's in Lisbon. He told him about the forward in Lourenço Marques.

Accounts differ on the order of these meetings. They agree that Guttmann moved fast.

Benfica reached an agreement with Eusébio's family, for a fee reported at 250 contos, while Sporting were still assuming he was theirs.

Sporting protested, and the clubs fought over him through the winter.

Benfica took no chances. The fishing town was Lagos, and accounts put his stay there at somewhere between a week and 12 days.

He was not registered until May 1961.

In June, at a tournament in Paris, Benfica were 4–0 down to Pelé's Santos when Guttmann sent him on. He scored a hat-trick in about 20 minutes. Santos still won.

Then came the part that surprised me.

A year later, before the 1962 European Cup final against Real Madrid in Amsterdam, the 20-year-old asked his team-mate Mário Coluna to introduce him to his idol, Alfredo Di Stéfano.

And to ask for his shirt after the match.

Ferenc Puskás scored three in the first half. Madrid led 3–2 at the interval. Then Coluna equalised and Eusébio scored twice in about five minutes. Benfica won 5–3.

Di Stéfano, still angry at the result, kept his promise. Eusébio later said he cared more about the shirt than the cup. He kept it for the rest of his life.

Then came England, and Portugal's first World Cup.

By the 25th minute of the quarter-final at Goodison Park, on 23 July 1966, Portugal were 3–0 down to North Korea, who had already beaten Italy.

Two minutes later Eusébio scored. He did not celebrate. He took the ball out of the net, ran it back to the centre circle and put it on the spot.

As he recalled in 2010, his team-mate António Simões kept saying: "As long as we don't go four goals down, we're still in with a chance."

Eusébio scored four goals in about half an hour. Portugal won 5–3.

In the semi-final against England, Nobby Stiles followed him everywhere. Eusébio's late penalty was not enough, England won 2–1, and he was in tears at the final whistle.

In Portugal it is still called the Jogo das Lágrimas, the game of tears.

He finished with nine goals, the most at the tournament.

Two years later, in the European Cup final at Wembley, Manchester United's goalkeeper Alex Stepney denied him with a save.

Eusébio stopped to applaud it.

When he died in January 2014, aged 71, Portugal declared three days of national mourning. His remains were later moved to the National Pantheon.

Here's what stays with me.

One of the greatest careers in football turned on a chat in a barber's shop and a week or so hidden in a fishing town. He belongs to Mozambique and to Portugal at once. And the boy who could take apart any defence still wanted his hero's shirt more than the trophy.
