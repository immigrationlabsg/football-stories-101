By the 25th minute of the World Cup quarter-final at Goodison Park in Liverpool on 23 July 1966, Portugal were 3–0 down to North Korea. The Koreans had scored in the first minute, then twice more in four minutes, and a crowd of about 40,000 that had come expecting a formality was watching a team from a country few of them could place on a map take apart one of the best sides in the tournament. North Korea had already beaten Italy.

Two minutes later Eusébio scored. He did not celebrate. He took the ball out of the net, ran it back to the centre circle and put it on the spot for the restart. Before half-time he had scored again, from a penalty. Early in the second half he equalised, and a few minutes after that he took another penalty and put Portugal ahead. José Augusto added a fifth. Portugal won 5–3. Eusébio had scored four goals in about half an hour.

One of his team-mates had a simple theory throughout.

> I remember very clearly what Simoes said when we were 3-0 down. He kept saying, ‘As long as we don’t go four goals down, we’re still in with a chance.’ And he was right.
> — Eusébio, speaking to the Associated Press in 2010, about António Simões

## Mafalala

He was born Eusébio da Silva Ferreira on 25 January 1942 in Mafalala, a poor district of Lourenço Marques, the capital of what was then Portuguese Mozambique and is now Maputo. His father, a railway worker from Angola, died when Eusébio was a boy, and his mother brought up the family. He played barefoot in the streets, with balls made of rolled-up newspaper and old socks, for a neighbourhood team that called itself Os Brasileiros, the Brazilians, after the side whose players they pretended to be.

At 15 he joined Sporting Clube de Lourenço Marques. The name mattered: it was a nursery club of Sporting in Lisbon, and Sporting assumed that if the boy turned out to be any good, he would one day be theirs. Several accounts say he had first tried to join the local affiliate of Benfica and been turned away.

He turned out to be very good indeed, and word reached Lisbon by an indirect route. José Carlos Bauer, a former Brazil international who had played under the Hungarian coach Béla Guttmann at São Paulo, saw Eusébio while touring Mozambique with a Brazilian club side. His club could not afford him. Some time afterwards Bauer ran into Guttmann, by now coach of Benfica, at a barber’s shop in Lisbon, and told him about the forward in Lourenço Marques. Accounts differ on the order of these meetings and on the name of Bauer’s team, but they agree on what Guttmann did next: he moved fast.

## Ruth

Benfica reached an agreement with Eusébio’s family, paying a fee reported at 250 contos, while Sporting were still assuming he was theirs. In December 1960 the 18-year-old was put on a plane to Lisbon. To keep the deal quiet, Benfica referred to him by a codename: Ruth.

Sporting protested that the transfer was illegitimate, and the two clubs fought over him through the winter. Benfica did not take chances. Eusébio was taken to Lagos, a fishing town in the Algarve, and kept out of sight in a hotel there, for somewhere between a week and 12 days depending on the account, with people watching over him. The dispute itself took months to settle, and he was not registered with Benfica until May 1961.

He did not need long to make up for it. In June 1961 Benfica played Pelé’s Santos at a tournament in Paris. Benfica were 4–0 down when Guttmann sent Eusébio on in the second half, and he scored a hat-trick in about 20 minutes. Santos still won. Nobody in Paris forgot the substitute.

## The turn

The moment that made Eusébio’s career was not a goal but a race between two clubs, and Benfica won it because they treated a teenager from the colonies as a matter of urgency rather than as something that could be collected later. Had Sporting secured him, he would still have scored goals. But he would have arrived at a club without Guttmann, and without the team that, a few months later, was the champion of Europe.

Benfica had won the European Cup for the first time that spring, beating Barcelona 3–2 in Bern on 31 May 1961, without him. A year later they were back in the final, in Amsterdam on 2 May 1962, against Real Madrid, the club that had won the first five editions. Before kick-off Eusébio, 20 years old, asked his team-mate Mário Coluna to introduce him to his idol, Alfredo Di Stéfano, and to ask for his shirt after the match.

Ferenc Puskás scored three goals in the first half, and Madrid led 3–2 at the interval. In the second half Coluna equalised and Eusébio scored twice in about five minutes, the first from a penalty. Benfica won 5–3. Di Stéfano, still angry at the result, kept his promise and handed over the shirt. Eusébio later said that he had cared more about the shirt than about the cup, and he kept it for the rest of his life.

Benfica reached the final again in 1963, at Wembley, where Eusébio scored first but Milan won 2–1 through two goals from José Altafini. At the end of 1965 France Football named him European Footballer of the Year, the Ballon d’Or, two years after Lev Yashin.

## England, 1966

Portugal had never played at a World Cup before 1966. In England they won all three group matches, against Hungary, Bulgaria and the holders, Brazil, whom they beat 3–1 at Goodison Park with two goals from Eusébio. Then came North Korea.

The semi-final, against England, had been scheduled for Goodison too, but it was moved to Wembley at short notice. On 26 July Bobby Charlton scored twice, Nobby Stiles followed Eusébio everywhere, and his penalty eight minutes from the end was not enough. England won 2–1. Eusébio was in tears at the final whistle, and in Portugal the match is still called the Jogo das Lágrimas, the game of tears.

Two days later, at Wembley again, he scored from the spot against the Soviet Union, beating Lev Yashin, who had become a friend, and Portugal won the third-place match 2–1. Eusébio finished with nine goals, the most at the tournament. English crowds, who had spent a month watching him, took to him in a way they rarely did with visiting players.

He spent most of his career at Benfica, winning 11 league titles by UEFA’s count, although the club’s own tally differs, and he was Europe’s leading league scorer in 1968 and again in 1973. Italian clubs tried to buy him in the mid-1960s. Accounts differ on what stopped them; the most often repeated is that Portugal’s dictator, António de Oliveira Salazar, would not let him go, and some of his relatives have said the deal failed when Italy closed its league to foreign players after the 1966 World Cup.

At Wembley in the 1968 European Cup final against Manchester United, he was denied by a save from the goalkeeper Alex Stepney and stopped to applaud it. United won 4–1. Charlton remembered it when Eusébio died.

> He was one of the finest players I ever had the privilege to play against.
> — Bobby Charlton, statement after Eusébio’s death, January 2014

Eusébio died at home on 5 January 2014, aged 71. Portugal declared three days of national mourning, and Parliament voted to move his remains to the National Pantheon, where they were taken in 2015.

## What it tells us

Talent travels only when someone moves it, and the route Eusébio took, from a street team in a colonial city to the summit of European football, ran through a chance conversation in a barber’s shop and a few weeks of hiding in the Algarve. He belongs to Mozambique and to Portugal at once, and the story of how he left one for the other is also a story about empire. What England saw in 1966 was the finished article; what made it possible was a race most of England never heard about.
