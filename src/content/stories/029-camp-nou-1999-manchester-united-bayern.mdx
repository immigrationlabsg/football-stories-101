When the 90 minutes ran out at Camp Nou on 26 May 1999, Bayern Munich were beating Manchester United 1–0.

They had led since the sixth minute. They had hit the woodwork twice. Their coach had already taken off the veteran Lothar Matthäus to help close the game out.

Three minutes later, United were champions of Europe.

Here’s what I didn’t know until recently.

Both United goals came from substitutes.

United had come to Barcelona chasing something no English club had ever won: the Treble. The Premier League and the FA Cup were already theirs. The European Cup would complete it.

They came without their captain. Roy Keane and Paul Scholes were both suspended, and UEFA’s report is blunt: without them, United could not take control of midfield. Peter Schmeichel, playing his last game for the club, wore the armband.

Mario Basler’s free kick put Bayern ahead after six minutes. Mehmet Scholl struck the woodwork. Late on, Carsten Jancker’s overhead kick crashed against the bar.

In the 67th minute Alex Ferguson sent on Teddy Sheringham for Jesper Blomqvist. With around ten minutes left (reports differ by a minute) Ole Gunnar Solskjær replaced Andy Cole.

Neither change seemed to matter. Bayern had the final won and could not finish it off. They waited for the whistle.

In the first minute of added time the ball dropped into the Bayern area. Stefan Effenberg’s clearance went astray, by Sheringham’s own account, and Ryan Giggs mis-hit a shot back towards goal.

Sheringham swept it in. He has since admitted he scuffed it.

1–1. Most people inside the stadium assumed extra time was coming.

Then came the part that surprised me.

The final was played on what would have been Sir Matt Busby’s 90th birthday.

Busby had taken United into the European Cup in 1956.

On 6 February 1958, his young team were flying home from a tie in Belgrade when their plane crashed at Munich, on its third attempt to take off. Twenty-three people died, eight of them United players. Busby was so badly hurt that he was given the last rites.

He came back and rebuilt. In 1968 his team beat Benfica 4–1 after extra time at Wembley, and Bobby Charlton, who had been pulled from the wreckage at Munich, scored twice.

For the next 31 years, no United side won it again.

Back in Barcelona, extra time never came.

Barely two minutes later David Beckham took a corner. Sheringham flicked it on with his head. Solskjær, arriving at close range, prodded it into the roof of the net.

Sheringham had been on for 24 minutes. He scored one and set up the other. Solskjær, on for about ten, needed one touch.

Effenberg said afterwards he had no words for something so sickening and so brutal. Schmeichel later said that at no point had he thought United had lost. Matthäus, years on, said the defeat still pained him.

As Ferguson put it to the camera that night: “Football, bloody hell.”

Afterwards, UEFA reports, he said he had made a promise not to let the memory of the Busby Babes down. Solskjær later told the club’s website he believed Busby had been watching.

A coincidence of dates proves nothing on its own. It still made the night impossible to remember as just another final.

Here’s what stays with me.

A final is not over when it looks over. It is over when the referee ends it. United’s winners came from the two most ordinary things in football, a ball into the box and a substitution. Bayern were better for 90 minutes. United were better for three, and three was enough.
