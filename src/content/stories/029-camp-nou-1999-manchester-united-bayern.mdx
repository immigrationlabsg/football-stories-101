When the 90 minutes ran out at Camp Nou in Barcelona on the night of 26 May 1999, Bayern Munich were beating Manchester United 1–0, and had been since the sixth minute. Bayern had hit the United woodwork twice. Their coach, Ottmar Hitzfeld, had already taken off the veteran defender Lothar Matthäus, in the 80th minute, to help close the game out. The crowd, which UEFA records as 90,245, was watching what looked like the last moments of a match settled in its first ten minutes.

Then United pushed forward one more time, and in the space of about three minutes the final, the season and a club’s long memory all turned over.

## A team with holes in it

United had come to Barcelona chasing something no English club had ever won: the Treble. They had already won the Premier League and the FA Cup. A win in Barcelona would add the European Cup, a trophy the club had won only once, in 1968, when Matt Busby’s side beat Benfica 4–1 after extra time at Wembley, ten years after the Munich air disaster.

They came without two of their most important players. Roy Keane, the captain, and Paul Scholes were both suspended. Peter Schmeichel, the goalkeeper, playing his last game for the club, took the armband. Without Keane and Scholes, United had to rearrange their midfield, and UEFA’s match report is blunt about the effect: missing the suspended pair, they simply could not take control of the middle of the pitch.

Bayern scored early. In the sixth minute Mario Basler’s free kick went in, and for most of the next 84 minutes the German side looked the more likely to score again. The manager, Alex Ferguson, later summed up the night from his side of the touchline in two short clauses: they hit the bar, they hit the post. Mehmet Scholl struck the woodwork, and late on Carsten Jancker’s overhead kick crashed against the United crossbar. Bayern had the final won and could not finish it off.

Ferguson changed things. In the 67th minute Teddy Sheringham came on for Jesper Blomqvist. With around ten minutes left (the reports differ by a minute) Ole Gunnar Solskjær replaced Andy Cole. Neither change seemed to matter. Bayern made one more substitution of their own, taking off Basler, and waited for the whistle. United needed a goal to take the final to extra time, and they had almost run out of minutes in which to find it.

## Stoppage time

It came in the first minute of added time. The ball dropped into the Bayern penalty area, Stefan Effenberg’s attempted clearance went astray, by Sheringham’s own later account, and it fell to Ryan Giggs, who mis-hit a shot back towards goal. Sheringham swept it in; he has since admitted he scuffed it. UEFA’s report has it in the 91st minute. United were level.

Most people inside the stadium assumed extra time was coming. It never did. Barely two minutes later United won a corner, and David Beckham took it. Sheringham flicked the ball on with his head. Solskjær, arriving at close range, prodded it into the roof of the net.

United had scored twice in stoppage time to turn defeat into victory. Effenberg, speaking afterwards, said he had no words for a moment so sickening and so brutal. Schmeichel, the man who had been on the losing side for 90 minutes and the winning side for three, later told FourFourTwo that at no point had he thought United had lost.

> Football, bloody hell.
> — Alex Ferguson to the camera after the final, as recorded by UEFA.com

## The turn

What decided the final was Ferguson’s two substitutions. Both men he sent on scored, and both goals came out of the most ordinary situation in football, a ball pumped into the penalty area in the last seconds. Sheringham, on for 24 minutes, scored one and set up the other. Solskjær, on for about ten, needed one touch. Bayern had played the better football for most of the night and had taken off an experienced defender to protect a lead. United, with the clock running down, did the opposite: they kept attacking and kept putting more attackers on the pitch.

There is a lesson in that about how substitutes are judged, and about how quickly the judgement can change. A change in the 67th minute and another with ten minutes left are, most nights, the kind of thing nobody remembers. On this night they were the whole story, because the men who came on were exactly where the ball arrived.

ESPN, comparing the night with Liverpool’s comeback from 3–0 down against Milan in Istanbul in 2005, has asked which of the two was the more epic final. UEFA’s own retrospective, 20 years later, asked in its headline whether 1999 was the most dramatic of all.

Matthäus, years afterwards, told FourFourTwo that the defeat still pained him.

> We were a lot better.
> — Lothar Matthäus, interviewed by FourFourTwo

On the scoreboard, though, it was United 2 Bayern 1, and the Treble was complete: the Premier League, the FA Cup and the European Cup in a single season, the first time any English club had done it.

## Busby’s birthday

There was one more detail that made the night feel arranged. Sir Matt Busby was born on 26 May 1909 and died in 1994. The final in Barcelona was played on what would have been his 90th birthday.

Busby had taken United into the European Cup in 1956, initially without the blessing of the Football League. His young team, the Busby Babes, were coming home from a European tie in Belgrade when their plane crashed on its third attempt to take off at Munich, where it had stopped to refuel, on 6 February 1958. Twenty-three people died, eight of them United players, and Busby himself was so badly hurt that he was given the last rites. While he recovered, his assistant Jimmy Murphy held the club together; 13 days after the crash a patched-up side beat Sheffield Wednesday 3–0 in the FA Cup, with the team sheet in the programme left blank.

Busby came back and rebuilt the club. On 29 May 1968 at Wembley his team beat Benfica 4–1 after extra time. Bobby Charlton, who had been pulled from the wreckage at Munich, scored twice; George Best scored; Brian Kidd scored on his 19th birthday. Bill Foulkes, another survivor of the crash, played at centre-half. Busby was knighted that year. For the next 31 years, no United side managed to win it again.

Ferguson, UEFA reports, said after the final in Barcelona that he had made a promise not to let the memory of the Busby Babes down. Solskjær, years later, told Manchester United’s own website that he believed Busby had been watching. The club has told the story of that night many times since, always in the same terms: two goals in injury time, the Treble clinched, and Sir Matt’s birthday. A coincidence of dates proves nothing on its own. But for a club whose identity had been shaped by Munich and by Wembley in 1968, it meant that the night in Barcelona was never going to be remembered as just another final.

The night belongs to the substitutes, but it also belongs to a goalkeeper. Schmeichel had captained the side in his final game for the club, and United won the European Cup on the night he left.

## What it tells us

A final is not over when it looks over; it is over when the referee ends it. United’s winning goals came from the two most ordinary things in the game, a ball into the box and a substitution, tried again and again until they worked. Bayern were better for 90 minutes. United were better for three, and three minutes is enough.
