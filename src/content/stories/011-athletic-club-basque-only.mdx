On Thursday 11 April 2024 a flat river barge called the Gabarra moved slowly up the estuary of the Nervión, from Getxo at the river mouth towards the city hall of Bilbao, about 13 kilometres away. On its deck stood the players of Athletic Club, holding the Copa del Rey they had won five days earlier. On the banks, on the bridges and on every balcony in between, the city had turned red and white. Nobody agreed on how many people were there. Agency reporters counted hundreds of thousands; local estimates ran to a million. Everyone agreed on the number that mattered: it was 40 years since the boat had last been out.

The Gabarra only sails when Athletic win something big, and for four decades they had not. What made the day stranger, and to many people in Bilbao sweeter, was who was on the boat. Every player in the squad had been born or brought up in the Basque Country, or had learned his football at a Basque club. That is not an accident of recruitment. It is the rule the club has lived by for more than a century, in a sport that has spent the same century learning to buy its players from anywhere.

## Shirts from Southampton

Bilbao learned its football from the British, and in its first decade Athletic picked English players as a matter of course.

Even the colours came from England, at least in the version the club tells. In late 1909 a member named Juan Elorduy was sent to England to buy kits in the blue and white of Blackburn Rovers. He could not find enough of them, and came home instead with red-and-white striped shirts from Southampton. Athletic first wore them on 9 January 1910. The spare shirts went to the club's Madrid branch, the side that grew into Atlético Madrid, which is why the two clubs still share the stripes. Some historians doubt the Southampton detail and point instead to a friendly in November 1909 and to Sunderland's striped kit as the model. The club's own history keeps the Southampton story.

The rule about players emerged in the same years, and it emerged from a row. In the 1911 Copa del Rey, rivals including Real Sociedad protested that Athletic were fielding English players who were not eligible. The federation rejected the complaint, Real Sociedad withdrew in protest, and Athletic won the cup without the disputed men. For the following year's competition the federation required all players to be Spanish citizens. Accounts differ on exactly when Athletic went further than the federation and settled on local players only; sources give dates from 1911 to 1919. What is not disputed is that it has never been written into the club's rules. It is a custom, and it has held.

Supporters have a saying for it: "Con cantera y afición, no hace falta importación." With homegrown players and our own fans, there is no need for imports.

## A rule that should not work

The modern version of the rule covers the seven historic Basque provinces, four in Spain including Navarre and three across the border in France. A player qualifies if he was born there, grew up there, or was trained at a Basque club. The rule has never applied to coaches. The most celebrated of them was an Englishman, Fred Pentland, who won the cup in 1923, his first season, and was known in Spain as El Bombín, the bowler hat. He wore it on the touchline, and after a trophy his players took it from him and smashed it, a ritual that became a tradition.

By any modern logic the rule should have sunk the club. Athletic draw on one small corner of Europe and compete against clubs that can sign from the whole planet. Yet Athletic have played in every season of La Liga since it began in 1929 and have never been relegated. Only Real Madrid and Barcelona share that record. They have won the league eight times, the last two in 1983 and 1984 under Javier Clemente, when they also completed the league and cup double.

The rule has produced its own giants. Telmo Zarra, a centre-forward, scored 251 league goals for Athletic between 1940 and 1955 and was the league's top scorer six times. His total stood as the Spanish record for almost six decades, until Lionel Messi passed it for Barcelona on 22 November 2014.

It has also produced arguments about who counts. When Bixente Lizarazu, born and raised on the French side of the border at Hendaye, joined from Bordeaux in 1996, he became the first Frenchman to play for the club. He stayed one season before moving to Bayern Munich. Borderline cases have been argued over ever since, and each one has stretched the definition a little without breaking it.

## The boat that came out of the museum

The Gabarra itself was an improvisation. When Athletic won the league in 1983, their first title in 27 years, a club director felt that the usual appearance on the city hall balcony would not do, and proposed taking the team up the estuary on a working barge instead. It sailed again in 1984 after the double. Then it waited, and was retired to the city's maritime museum while the club reached final after final and lost them all.

On the eve of the 2024 final, the coach, Ernesto Valverde, was asked about the club's record in finals.

> We always have to try to win, but we have played five finals this century and we have lost all of them.
> — Ernesto Valverde, pre-final news conference, 5 April 2024, reported by AFP

The final against Mallorca at La Cartuja in Seville on 6 April 2024 looked for a long time like a sixth. Dani Rodríguez put Mallorca ahead in the 21st minute. Oihan Sancet equalised five minutes after half-time, and nobody scored again in 120 minutes. In the shoot-out the goalkeeper Julen Agirrezabala saved from Manu Morlanes, Nemanja Radonjić missed, and Alex Berenguer struck Athletic's fourth penalty to win it 4–2.

> This title is like no other.
> — Ernesto Valverde, press conference at La Cartuja, Seville, 6 April 2024, published by the Spanish football federation

Nico Williams was one of the stars of that team. His older brother, Iñaki, was its symbol.

## The turn

The decision that explains Athletic is the one the club keeps making without ever writing it down: to define "our own" by where a footballer was formed, not by where his family came from. That choice is what put Iñaki Williams on the barge.

His parents left Ghana and crossed the Sahara to reach Spain; his father has described walking the desert barefoot. A Catholic priest in Bilbao, Iñaki Mardones, met the couple, helped them find a home and saw them through the birth of their first son, who was named after him. Iñaki Williams was born in Bilbao, grew up in Navarre and came through Athletic's academy. Ghana asked him to play for them; for years he said that he was born in Bilbao and felt Basque, before finally choosing Ghana in 2022.

On 20 April 2016 he came on against Atlético Madrid at San Mamés. He did not miss another La Liga match for almost seven years. When a physical problem kept him out of a trip to Celta Vigo on 29 January 2023, the run had reached 251 consecutive league games, a record for the competition.

A rule that began, more than a century earlier, as a way of keeping foreign professionals out has become a way of letting a city's newest families in. The club did not change the rule to make that happen. The city changed, and the rule followed it.

## What it tells us

Constraints can be a strategy. Athletic cannot buy their way out of trouble, so they spend their effort on finding and keeping players nobody else can take, and on giving them a reason to stay. It also tells us that identity in football is less about blood than about belonging: the boat on the Nervión carries whoever the city has made.
