On 31 May 1992 the Yugoslavia squad were in Leksand, a small Swedish town, ten days from the start of the European Championship.

Then they broke camp and went home.

The United Nations had imposed sanctions the day before. UEFA confirmed the team was out.

Their coach, Ivica Osim, had already resigned, his family trapped in the siege of Sarajevo.

Their place went to the runners-up in their qualifying group.

Denmark.

You probably know the legend. The Danes were called back from the beach and won the whole thing on ice cream and goodwill.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The beach part is mostly a myth, and the players have spent 30 years correcting it.

As Peter Schmeichel put it to UEFA.com: “The myth is that we were all on the beach.”

The Danish league season had finished only about two weeks before the opening match. The national team already had a friendly arranged against the CIS, the short-lived side that replaced the Soviet Union. They played it on 3 June and drew 1–1.

They were match fit. What they were not, Schmeichel admitted, was mentally ready for a tournament.

Some of them were close to the beach, though.

Brian Laudrup came home from a friendly to find his wife had taken a call from the Danish FA. His first answer was no. He was coming back from a serious knee injury and did not think he could last 90 minutes.

Then he started ringing team-mates, several of whom were already on holiday.

The coach, Richard Møller Nielsen, has been widely reported as saying he had been about to start work on a new kitchen.

And Møller Nielsen was not popular. His cautious methods had driven away Denmark’s best player. Michael Laudrup refused to play under him, and watched the tournament from a distance.

The group stage went as everyone expected. A 0–0 draw with England. A 1–0 defeat by Sweden, the hosts. One point, no goals.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Kim Vilfort, a midfielder, had a young daughter, Line, who was being treated for leukaemia. After the second game her condition worsened, and he went home to be with her.

Without him, Denmark beat France 2–1 and reached the semi-finals.

His family urged him to go back.

In Gothenburg the semi-final against the Netherlands, the holders, finished 2–2 after extra time. Vilfort took a penalty in the shoot-out and scored. So did every other Dane.

Marco van Basten stepped up for the Dutch, and Schmeichel saved it.

It was the only kick of the ten that was missed.

The final, back in Gothenburg on 26 June, was against a newly unified Germany, the heavy favourites. John Jensen scored from long range after 18 minutes. Schmeichel kept Germany out.

With 12 minutes left, Vilfort scored the second.

The man who had left the squad to sit at his daughter’s bedside had made it 2–0. Denmark, who had not qualified, were champions of Europe.

There was no fairy-tale ending for him. Line died a few weeks after the tournament.

One more footnote, and it changed the game for good.

Denmark had spent long spells passing the ball back to Schmeichel, who could pick it up, wait and start again. One analysis found their goalkeeper received more passes per 90 minutes than any other at the finals.

From the summer of 1992, keepers could no longer handle a deliberate back-pass from a team-mate’s foot.

The rule had been under discussion since a dull 1990 World Cup, so Denmark were not the only reason. But theirs is the final most often cited, and Schmeichel has claimed the credit himself.

Here's what stays with me.

The truer story is better than the legend. Not a beach, but ten days, a team unloved by its own star, and a father who kept leaving and kept coming back. Nobody expected anything of them, and that turns out to be its own kind of freedom.
