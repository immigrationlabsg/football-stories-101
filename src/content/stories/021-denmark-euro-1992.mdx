On 31 May 1992 the Yugoslavia squad were in Leksand, a small town in the middle of Sweden, preparing for a European Championship that was due to start in ten days. They had not planned to be there. Their first base had been in Ystad, on the south coast, and Swedish police had asked them to move because it was close to a centre for refugees from Kosovo. Their coach was no longer with them either. Ivica Osim had resigned on 23 May with his own family trapped in the siege of Sarajevo. The day before, 30 May, the United Nations Security Council had passed Resolution 757, imposing sanctions on what was left of Yugoslavia. On the 31st, UEFA confirmed that the team would be excluded from the tournament. The players broke camp and went home.

It was not only a football decision. FIFA's measures went further than UEFA's, shutting Yugoslav teams out of all international competition, including qualifying for the 1994 World Cup. The squad that went home from Leksand had beaten Denmark to first place in their qualifying group, and it has been called the best team never to win the European Championship. It never got the chance to try.

Their place, and their fixtures, went to the team that had finished second behind them in qualifying. That team was Denmark.

## Not quite the beach

The story everyone knows is that the Danes were recalled from their summer holidays, sand still between their toes, and won the thing on ice creams and goodwill. It is a lovely story. It is not quite what happened, and the men who were there have spent 30 years correcting it.

> The myth is that we were all on the beach.
> — Peter Schmeichel, interview with UEFA.com

What is documented is more ordinary and more interesting. Yugoslavia's participation had been in doubt for weeks as the war in the Balkans worsened, and Denmark, as group runners-up, were the obvious replacement. The Danish league season had finished only about two weeks before the tournament's opening match, and the national team already had a friendly arranged against the CIS, the short-lived team that had replaced the Soviet Union. That match was played on 3 June 1992, three days after the decision, and finished 1–1. Schmeichel's point was that the players based in Denmark and those abroad had not long finished their seasons when they were told they were in. They were match fit. What they were not, he admitted later, was mentally prepared for a tournament.

> There are a lot of stories about us being on the beach.
> — Peter Schmeichel to Sky Sports, as reported by Goal

Some of them were, though, or near enough. Brian Laudrup told UEFA.com that he came back from a friendly to find his wife had taken a call from the Danish FA: he was going to the European Championship. His first reaction was to say no. He was coming back from a serious knee injury and did not think he could last 90 minutes. Then he started ringing his team-mates, several of whom were already on holiday and had to report within days. Laudrup recalled that they had about a week to prepare for their first match, against England. The coach, Richard Møller Nielsen, has been widely reported as saying he had been about to start work on a new kitchen.

So the precise version is this: Denmark were not plucked off a beach, but they were assembled at ten days' notice, out of a domestic season that had just ended and a set of summer plans that had just been cancelled. Nobody, least of all the Danes, expected them to get out of the group.

## A team that did not like its coach

There was another reason for low expectations. Møller Nielsen was not popular. His cautious, defensive methods had driven away Denmark's best player. Michael Laudrup had refused to play under him. For a while his younger brother Brian had refused too, before changing his mind in the spring of 1992. Michael did not. He watched the tournament from a distance and returned to the national team only after it was over.

The group stage did nothing to change anyone's mind. Denmark drew 0–0 with England and lost 1–0 to the hosts, Sweden. They had one point and had not scored, and they needed to beat France to have any chance of going through.

That was also the moment the tournament became personal for one of the squad. Kim Vilfort, a midfielder, had a young daughter, Line, who was being treated for leukaemia. After the first two group games her condition worsened and Vilfort went home to be with her. He missed the France match. Denmark won it 2–1, with goals from Henrik Larsen after eight minutes and the substitute Lars Elstrup after 78, and went through to the semi-finals.

## The turn

The semi-final against the Netherlands in Gothenburg was the match that turned a nice story into a serious one. The Dutch had Marco van Basten, Frank Rijkaard and Dennis Bergkamp, and were expected to reach the final. Larsen scored twice in the first half-hour, after five minutes and after 33. Bergkamp and Rijkaard replied for the Netherlands, and extra time settled nothing. It went to penalties at 2–2.

Vilfort was back. His family had urged him to return to the squad, and he took one of the Danish penalties and scored. So did every other Danish taker. For the Netherlands, Van Basten stepped up, and Schmeichel saved it. It was the only kick of the ten that was missed. Kim Christofte scored the decisive one, and Denmark had won 5–4 on penalties.

That save is the hinge of the whole tournament. Up to that point Denmark had been a team surviving on defending and luck. After it, they were a team that had beaten the holders, and they began to believe the story that everyone else was now telling about them.

## Gothenburg, 26 June 1992

The final at the Ullevi in Gothenburg was against a newly unified Germany, who were the heavy favourites. In front of 37,800 people, with the Swiss referee Bruno Galler in charge, Denmark went ahead after 18 minutes when the midfielder John Jensen hit a long-range shot past Bodo Illgner. Germany pushed for the equaliser and Schmeichel kept them out.

With 12 minutes left Vilfort, the man who had left the squad to sit at his daughter's bedside, scored the second. It was 2–0, and it stayed that way. Denmark, who had not qualified, were champions of Europe.

The story has no fairy-tale ending for the man who scored the second goal. Line Vilfort died a few weeks after the tournament.

There is one more footnote, and it changed the game. Denmark had spent long periods of the tournament passing the ball back to Schmeichel, who could pick it up, wait, and start again. One analysis of the tournament found that Denmark's goalkeeper received more passes per 90 minutes than any other keeper at the finals. Within weeks the law changed: from the summer of 1992 a goalkeeper could no longer handle a deliberate back-pass from a team-mate's foot. The rule had been under discussion since a dull World Cup in 1990, but Denmark's final is the match most often cited, and Schmeichel himself has claimed that he was the reason for it.

## What it tells us

Expectation is a weight, and Denmark played Euro 92 without carrying any. The best version of their story is not the beach, which did not quite happen, but the ten days, which did: a team assembled in a hurry, unloved by its own star, held together by a goalkeeper and by a midfielder who kept leaving to sit at a hospital bed and kept coming back. It tells us that in tournament football the gap between the best team and a good team that nobody fears can be closed in three weeks.
