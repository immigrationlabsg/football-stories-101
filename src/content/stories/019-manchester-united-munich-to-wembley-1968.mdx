Thirteen days after the crash, Manchester United printed a match programme with a blank space where the team should have been.

That is the detail I cannot shake.

The day after a 3–3 draw with Red Star in Belgrade, United’s plane put down at Munich to refuel. It was snowing. On the third attempt to take off, it ran off the end of the runway and broke apart.

Of the 44 people on board, 23 died.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

United were only in Europe because their manager, Matt Busby, had insisted on it.

A year earlier the football authorities had kept the English champions, Chelsea, out of the new competition. In 1956 United were told much the same.

Busby went in anyway, initially without the blessing of the Football League.

Old Trafford had no adequate floodlights, so the home leg against Anderlecht was played at Maine Road, Manchester City’s ground. United won 10–0.

The draw in Belgrade on 5 February 1958 put the Busby Babes into the semi-finals of the European Cup. It also put them on a snowbound runway in Munich.

The crew had already abandoned two attempts to take off. On the third, the aircraft failed to reach take-off speed.

Eight of the dead were players: Geoff Bent, Roger Byrne, Eddie Colman, Duncan Edwards, Mark Jones, David Pegg, Tommy Taylor and Liam Whelan. Three were club staff. Eight were journalists, among them Frank Swift, the former Manchester City goalkeeper.

Edwards was 21. He died in hospital 15 days after the crash.

The goalkeeper, Harry Gregg, survived. When the plane came to rest he climbed out, then went back into the burning wreckage.

By accounts that include his own later recollection, he heard a baby crying and brought out a 20-month-old girl. Then he went back for her badly injured, pregnant mother.

He dragged out two unconscious team-mates, Bobby Charlton and Dennis Viollet.

Busby was so badly hurt that the last rites were read over him. He lived.

His assistant, Jimmy Murphy, who had not been on the plane, held the club together.

Then came the part that surprised me.

On 19 February United played Sheffield Wednesday in the FA Cup. Of the players on duty in Belgrade, only Gregg and Bill Foulkes took the field.

Foulkes led the team out, in front of 59,848.

Shay Brennan, a youth-team full-back playing on the wing, scored twice, once direct from a corner. United won 3–0.

Murphy’s patched-up side went all the way to the FA Cup final.

Busby came back and started again. It took five years to win a trophy, and ten to reach a European Cup final.

In 1968 United met Real Madrid in the semi-final, the club that had stopped the Babes in 1957. At half-time in the Bernabéu they were 3–1 down on the night.

In the closing quarter of an hour, Foulkes, a 36-year-old centre-half who had survived Munich, wandered upfield.

George Best cut the ball back. Foulkes struck it into the corner. United were through, 4–3 on aggregate.

The final was at Wembley on 29 May 1968, against Benfica. Charlton headed United in front.

In extra time Best scored. Brian Kidd, on his 19th birthday, scored the third. Charlton added the fourth.

United won 4–1.

Two men from the plane were on the pitch, Charlton and Foulkes. A third, Busby, was their manager.

Busby said afterwards, in words reported with slightly different phrasing over the years, that the moment Charlton took the cup had cleansed him of the guilt he felt about going into Europe.

As Charlton later put it to UEFA.com: “It was a marvellous night because it put things right in a way.”

Here's what stays with me.

The ambition that put United on that runway is the same ambition that gave them something to set against the loss. A trophy does not redeem a disaster, and the survivors never pretended it did. But a blank team sheet still gets filled, by people who decide to keep going.
