On 5 February 1958, in Belgrade, Manchester United drew 3–3 with Red Star and went through to the semi-finals of the European Cup, 5–4 on aggregate. The next day their plane, a propeller-driven British European Airways Elizabethan registered G-ALZU, put down at Munich to refuel, because it could not reach England in one hop. It was snowing. The crew abandoned two attempts to take off. On the third, the aircraft failed to reach take-off speed, ran off the end of the runway and broke apart.

Of the 44 people on board, 23 died. The club’s own memorial lists them. Eight were players: Geoff Bent, Roger Byrne, Eddie Colman, Duncan Edwards, Mark Jones, David Pegg, Tommy Taylor and Liam Whelan. Three were club staff: the secretary Walter Crickmer, the trainer Tom Curry and the coach Bert Whalley. Eight were journalists: Alf Clarke, Donny Davies, George Follows, Tom Jackson, Archie Ledbrooke, Henry Rose, Eric Thompson and Frank Swift, the former Manchester City goalkeeper. The other four were Captain Ken Rayment of the flight crew, the crew member Tom Cable, the travel agent Bela Miklos and Willie Satinoff, a supporter and friend of the manager, Matt Busby. Edwards, 21 years old, died in the Rechts der Isar hospital 15 days after the crash.

Investigators eventually concluded that slush on the runway had slowed the aircraft too much for it to get airborne.

## The decision of 1956

The team that died at Munich was the best in England. United had won the First Division in 1955–56 and again in 1956–57, with a side so young that the newspapers called them the Busby Babes. Byrne, the captain, had lifted both trophies.

They were in Europe because Busby had insisted on it. The European Cup began in 1955–56, and the English champions that season, Chelsea, did not take part: the football authorities, wary of a continental tournament that might crowd out the domestic game, kept them out. A year later United, as champions, were told much the same. Busby went in anyway, initially without the blessing of the Football League. Old Trafford had no adequate floodlights, so after a 2–0 win in Brussels the home leg against Anderlecht was played at Maine Road, Manchester City’s ground. United won it 10–0. They beat Borussia Dortmund and Athletic Bilbao before meeting Real Madrid, the holders, in the 1957 semi-final. They lost 3–1 at the Bernabéu and could only draw 2–2 in Manchester.

## Thirteen days

Harry Gregg, the goalkeeper, was among the survivors. When the plane came to rest he climbed out, then went back into the burning wreckage. By accounts that include his own later recollection, he heard a baby crying and brought out a 20-month-old girl, then returned for her badly injured, pregnant mother, Vera Lukić (the spelling differs between accounts). He dragged out two unconscious team-mates, Bobby Charlton and Dennis Viollet. He was remembered for it as the reluctant hero of Munich, and he died in 2020, aged 87.

Busby was so badly hurt that the last rites were read over him in the Rechts der Isar hospital in Munich. He lived, and spent months recovering. The man who held the club together was his assistant, Jimmy Murphy, who had not been on the plane. Murphy was also manager of Wales, and on 5 February, the day of the Belgrade match, he had been in Cardiff, where Wales beat Israel to qualify for the 1958 World Cup.

On 19 February, 13 days after the crash, United played Sheffield Wednesday in the fifth round of the FA Cup at Old Trafford. In the match programme, the space where the United team should have been was left blank. Of the players who had been on duty in Belgrade, only Gregg and Bill Foulkes took the field, and Foulkes led the team out. A crowd of 59,848 watched Shay Brennan, a youth-team full-back playing on the wing, score twice, once direct from a corner, and Alex Dawson add a third. United won 3–0.

Murphy’s patched-up side got all the way to Wembley, where Bolton Wanderers beat them 2–0 with two goals from Nat Lofthouse. In May they met Milan in the European Cup semi-final, won 2–1 at Old Trafford and lost 4–0 in the San Siro.

## Rebuilding

Busby came back, and he started again. The rebuilding took five years to deliver a trophy: the FA Cup in 1963, 3–1 against Leicester City at Wembley, two goals from David Herd and one from Denis Law. United were league champions in 1965, edging Leeds United on goal difference, and again in 1967, with a forward line of Law, Charlton and a teenage Belfast winger, George Best, that came to be called the United Trinity. In between, in 1966, they reached the semi-finals of the European Cup and went out. Charlton, the young man Gregg had pulled from the wreckage, was now the team’s centre.

The 1967 title meant one more European Cup campaign. In the semi-final United met Real Madrid, the club that had stopped the Babes in 1957. They won the first leg 1–0 at Old Trafford, and at half-time in the Bernabéu on 15 May 1968 they were 3–1 down on the night. David Sadler pulled one back. Then, in the closing quarter of an hour, Foulkes, a 36-year-old centre-half who had survived Munich, wandered upfield. Best beat the left-back and cut the ball back, and it ran to Foulkes, who struck it into the corner. It finished 3–3 and United were through 4–3 on aggregate.

## The turn

The decision that explains this story, the tragedy and the triumph both, is the one Busby made in 1956. He chose to take his club into Europe when the people who ran English football would rather he had not. It was the reason the Babes were in Belgrade, and so on a snowbound runway in Munich. It was also why, for the next ten years, the European Cup was the one prize that could make sense of what had happened.

Busby understood that himself. After Wembley he said, in words reported with slightly different phrasing over the years, that the moment Charlton took the cup had cleansed him, easing the guilt he felt about going into Europe, and that it was his justification.

The final was played at Wembley on 29 May 1968, against Benfica. Charlton headed United in front early in the second half. Jaime Graça equalised with about ten minutes left, and late in normal time Alex Stepney saved a shot from Eusébio that would have won it for Benfica. In extra time Best scored within three minutes. Brian Kidd, playing on his 19th birthday, scored the third. Charlton added the fourth. United won 4–1.

Two men who had been on the plane were on the pitch: Charlton, who scored twice, and Foulkes. A third, Busby, was their manager. He was knighted that year for rebuilding the club.

> It was a marvellous night because it put things right in a way.
> — Bobby Charlton on the 1968 final, speaking to UEFA.com

Charlton said in the same interview that Munich remained the defining event of his life, and in his autobiography he wrote that the crash was always there, a factor that could never be discounted. Winning in 1968 did not change that. It put something next to it.

## What it tells us

The same ambition that put United on a runway in Munich is what eventually gave the club something to set against the loss. A disaster does not get redeemed by a trophy, and the people who survived Munich never pretended it did. What 1968 shows is how long a club can carry a single purpose, and how much of that carrying is done by a few individuals, a manager, an assistant and the players who walked out of the wreckage, who decide to keep going.
