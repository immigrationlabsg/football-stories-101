Just after five o’clock on the afternoon of 4 May 1949, a Fiat G.212 of Avio Linee Italiane came down through thick fog towards Turin. It was carrying the Torino football team home from Lisbon. The hill of Superga rises above the city, with a basilica on its summit, and in the fog the crew did not see it. The aircraft struck the retaining wall behind the basilica. All 31 people on board were killed: 18 players, the club’s coaches and officials, journalists and four crew. The 18 included every member of the first team bar one.

The team that died on that hillside was not merely a good side. It was the team Italians still call Il Grande Torino. It had won five consecutive Serie A titles: in 1942–43, and then, after two seasons in which the war stopped the championship, every year from 1945–46 to 1948–49, the last of them confirmed only after the crash. It was also the backbone of the national side. In a few seconds, in the fog, Italian football lost almost an entire generation of its finest players.

## A promise kept in Lisbon

The trip that ended at Superga was a friendly, played as a gesture. Benfica’s captain, Francisco Ferreira, was approaching retirement, and he had become friends with Torino’s captain, Valentino Mazzola. Ferreira had won from Mazzola a promise that the Grande Torino would come to Lisbon for his farewell match. On 3 May 1949 they kept it, at the Estádio Nacional. Torino lost 4–3. It did not matter. Benfica still remember the game. Their club website describes the two teams as connected forever by what happened the next day.

The next afternoon the team flew home. By the time they reached Turin the weather was poor and the fog was thick. Accounts of the final minutes differ in their detail, and some suggest a faulty altimeter, but they agree on the essentials: the crew had no clear view of the ground, and the pilot appears to have misjudged where the hill was.

Among the dead were the two men who ran the team. Leslie Lievesley, an Englishman, was the coach that season. Beside him was Ernő Egri Erbstein, the Hungarian who had shaped the side, first as coach and later as technical director. Erbstein was a Hungarian Jew. During the war, back in Hungary, he was sent to a Nazi forced labour camp near Budapest. He escaped it, survived, and came back to Turin to build the best team in the country. Having outlived the Holocaust, he died on a hillside in Piedmont.

## The men who were not on board

Some of the most haunting parts of the story concern the people who should have been on the plane and were not.

Sauro Tomà, a defender, stayed in Turin with a knee injury, a damaged meniscus, and missed the trip. The club’s second-choice goalkeeper, Renato Gandolfi, was also left behind. His place went to the third-choice keeper, Dino Ballarin, after Dino’s brother Aldo, a Torino defender, argued for him to be taken. Both Ballarin brothers died at Superga.

One of the men who stayed behind became one of the greatest players of the next decade. László Kubala, a Hungarian forward who had fled the post-war upheaval in his own country, had recently joined Torino and was due to play for them in Lisbon. His wife and son had only just reached Turin, the boy was ill, and Kubala stayed with him. He went on to become a legend at Barcelona.

Vittorio Pozzo, the former Italy coach, who had built his post-war national team around Torino’s players, was invited to travel and declined. The club gave the seat to a journalist, Cavallero, who was killed. The radio commentator Nicolò Carosio also did not take the flight; most accounts say he stayed at home for his son’s confirmation, though at least one gives illness instead.

It fell to Pozzo to identify the bodies. He had brought almost the whole Torino side into the national team, and he knew them as well as anyone outside the club.

## The team that died

How good were they? One afternoon gives a measure. On 11 May 1947 Italy played Hungary in a friendly in Turin. Ten of the eleven Italian players were from Torino. The only exception was the goalkeeper, Lucidio Sentimenti of Juventus. Italy won 3–2, with two goals from Guglielmo Gabetto and a winner from Ezio Loik in the last minute. Hungary’s goals came from Ferenc Szusza and from a penalty by a young Ferenc Puskás. On that afternoon in Turin, one club’s players were enough to beat them.

At the centre of everything was the captain, Mazzola, who was also the fulcrum of the national team under Pozzo. At the Stadio Filadelfia, Torino’s home ground, Mazzola presided over a ritual that the club’s supporters still talk about. When a match was going badly, a supporter in the stand named Oreste Bolmida, a railway station master at Porta Nuova who was never a player, coach or director of the club, would raise a trumpet and sound three blasts. Mazzola would roll up the sleeves of his maroon shirt. What followed was known as the quarto d’ora granata, the maroon quarter of an hour, in which Torino were expected to overwhelm whoever was in front of them.

They usually did. From 17 January 1943 until the crash, Torino did not lose a match at home. The sources count the run differently, depending on which matches they include, but every version of it ends in the same place: not with a defeat, but with Superga.

## The turn

There was no single error of judgement in the story of the Grande Torino, which is part of what makes it unbearable. The decisive choice was a generous one: to break off from a league campaign that was nearly won and fly to Portugal to keep a captain’s promise to another club’s captain. Every small decision around it, a meniscus injury here, a sick child there, a son’s confirmation, a brother’s persuasion, a coach’s polite refusal, sorted the living from the dead without anyone knowing it. The fog over Superga did the rest.

What followed showed how much the team had meant. The coffins lay in state at the Palazzo Madama in the centre of Turin, and on 6 May a grieving crowd filed past them. Nobody could count the mourners, and the estimates still disagree: UEFA says more than 800,000 people passed through the Palazzo Madama over two days, other accounts speak of hundreds of thousands lining the streets for the funeral, and Il Post puts the number who came to the city at close to a million. A few days after the crash, the journalist Indro Montanelli offered the city a consolation in the Corriere della Sera.

> Heroes are always immortal in the eyes of those who believe in them. And so the boys will believe that Torino is not dead: it is only “away”.
> — Indro Montanelli, Corriere della Sera, May 1949 (translated from Italian)

The league title still had to be decided, and Italian football found a way to do it with dignity. Torino were declared champions with four matches still to play; at least one account says three. The federation accepted the club’s proposal to play the remaining fixtures with its youth team, which was itself the reigning youth champion of Italy. Torino’s opponents in those games fielded their youth teams too.

Valentino Mazzola’s elder son, Sandro, born in Turin in 1942, was six when his father died. He grew up to join Inter, and spent his whole playing career there, from 1960 to 1977. He scored twice in the 1964 European Cup final, when Inter beat Real Madrid 3–1 in Vienna, won the competition again the following year, won four Serie A titles and the 1968 European Championship with Italy. He never played for Torino. He died on 19 September 2026, aged 83.

## What it tells us

Some great teams fade, and some are broken up by age or money. The Grande Torino did neither: it was stopped, whole and at its peak, by a hill in the fog. That is why it remains a measuring stick in Italian football, a team frozen at its best, and why the people who missed that flight spent the rest of their lives explaining that they had been lucky.
