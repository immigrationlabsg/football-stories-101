Just after five o’clock on the afternoon of 4 May 1949, a plane came down through thick fog towards Turin.

It was carrying the Torino football team home from Lisbon.

The hill of Superga rises above the city, with a basilica on its summit. In the fog, the crew did not see it. The aircraft struck the retaining wall behind the basilica.

All 31 people on board were killed. Eighteen of them were players.

Among them was every member of the first team bar one.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

They had flown to Lisbon to keep a promise.

Benfica’s captain, Francisco Ferreira, was approaching retirement. He had become friends with Torino’s captain, Valentino Mazzola, and had won from him a promise that the Grande Torino would come to Lisbon for his farewell match.

On 3 May 1949 they kept it. Torino lost 4–3.

It did not matter. Benfica still describe the two clubs as connected forever by what happened the next day.

And this was no ordinary side to send on a friendly. This was Il Grande Torino. Five consecutive Serie A titles, the last confirmed only after the crash. The backbone of the national side.

On 11 May 1947, Italy beat Hungary 3–2 in Turin with ten Torino players on the pitch. Only the goalkeeper came from another club.

At their home ground, the Filadelfia, there had been a ritual. When a match was going badly, a supporter named Oreste Bolmida, a railway station master, would raise a trumpet and sound three blasts.

Mazzola would roll up his sleeves.

What followed was called the quarto d’ora granata, the maroon quarter of an hour. From January 1943 until the crash, Torino did not lose a match at home.

Among the dead was Ernő Egri Erbstein, the Hungarian who had shaped the side. Erbstein was Jewish. During the war he was sent to a Nazi forced labour camp near Budapest, escaped, survived, and came back to Turin to build the best team in the country.

Having outlived the Holocaust, he died on a hillside in Piedmont.

Then came the part that surprised me.

So much of the story is about who was not on the plane.

Sauro Tomà, a defender, stayed in Turin with a damaged knee.

The second-choice goalkeeper was left behind too. His place went to the third choice, Dino Ballarin, after Dino’s brother Aldo, a Torino defender, argued for him to be taken.

Both Ballarin brothers died at Superga.

László Kubala, a Hungarian forward, had recently joined Torino and was due to play in Lisbon. His wife and son had only just reached Turin. The boy was ill, and Kubala stayed with him.

He went on to become a legend at Barcelona.

Vittorio Pozzo, the former Italy coach, was invited to travel and declined. His seat went to a journalist, who was killed.

It then fell to Pozzo to identify the bodies. He had brought almost the whole Torino side into the national team. He knew them as well as anyone outside the club.

The coffins lay in state at the Palazzo Madama. Nobody could count the mourners, and the estimates still disagree, from hundreds of thousands to close to a million.

A few days later, the journalist Indro Montanelli wrote in the Corriere della Sera that the boys would believe Torino was not dead, that “it is only ‘away’”.

The season still had to be finished. Torino were declared champions with four matches left, and played them with their youth team.

Their opponents fielded youth teams too.

Mazzola’s elder son, Sandro, was six when his father died. He grew up to play his whole career for Inter and scored twice in a European Cup final.

He never played for Torino.

Here's what stays with me.

Nobody made a reckless choice. The decisive one was generous: a captain keeping his word to a friend. Everything else, a knee, a sick child, a brother’s persuasion, a polite refusal, sorted the living from the dead without anyone knowing it.
