Six minutes into the second half at the Estadio Azteca in Mexico City, on 22 June 1986, the England midfielder Steve Hodge tried to send the ball back towards his own goalkeeper. It came off his foot at the wrong angle and rose into the air above the penalty spot. Diego Maradona, who had kept running into the area, arrived underneath it at the same moment as Peter Shilton. Shilton was much the taller of the two and came out with his arms raised. Maradona realised, as he later admitted, that he was not going to beat him with his head. So he raised his fist alongside it and punched the ball over the goalkeeper and into the net.

The Tunisian referee, Ali Bin Nasser, did not see it clearly. He looked across to his linesman, the Bulgarian Bogdan Dochev, who did not raise his flag. Bin Nasser pointed to the centre circle. Argentina led 1–0 in a World Cup quarter-final against England, four years after the two countries had been at war, in front of 114,580 people.

Four minutes later Maradona scored again, and the second goal was so good that it has spent four decades arguing with the first for the right to define him.

## Who saw what

England's players protested. They had seen the hand; so, on the television replays, had everybody watching around the world. The two officials told different stories for the rest of their lives. Bin Nasser said in later interviews that his view had been blocked, that he waited for a signal from Dochev that never came, and that under the instructions of the time he was bound to defer to the official with the better view. Dochev, who died in 2017, put it the other way round.

> Although I felt immediately there was something irregular, back in that time FIFA didn't allow the assistants to discuss the decisions with the referee.
> — Bogdan Dochev, as quoted by The Guardian

The phrase that made the goal famous has a stranger history than most people realise. The story is that after the match Maradona told reporters that the goal had been scored "un poco con la cabeza de Maradona y otro poco con la mano de Dios", a little with the head of Maradona and a little with the hand of God. Encyclopaedias repeat it. But as the Argentine newspaper La Nación has pointed out, there is no film or audio recording of him saying it. According to La Nación, no Argentine television crew recorded it, and the line does not appear in Clarín, La Nación or the other Buenos Aires papers the following day. It spread through the Mexican press. Later in 1986, in a newspaper column of his own in Tiempo Argentino, Maradona wrote that it was completely false that he had said the goal was scored with the hand of God. Whether the line was his, or a reporter's that he never disowned strongly enough, nobody has been able to settle. It became his anyway.

What is not in doubt is the hand. In his autobiography, Yo soy el Diego, published in 2000, he wrote that he could now say what he could not say at the time, and that it had been the hand of Diego. In August 2005, on his own television chat show, La Noche del 10, he said it again, plainly: he had punched the ball because he knew he could not head it past Shilton. He added that he did not regret it for a second. Shilton, asked for a response, said the apology, if that is what it was, had come too late.

## Eleven seconds

The second goal began with Maradona receiving the ball in Argentina's own half. He turned, and then he ran. Over roughly 60 yards and about 11 seconds he went past five England players and then took the ball around Shilton before putting it into the net.

On Víctor Hugo Morales's radio commentary for Argentine listeners, the run produced one of the most famous pieces of broadcasting in Spanish. Morales later explained that he had been reaching for an image he had used before, of Maradona as a kite that flew wherever nobody expected.

> Barrilete cósmico… ¿de qué planeta viniste?
> (Cosmic kite… what planet did you come from?)
> — Víctor Hugo Morales, radio commentary, 22 June 1986

Two details from the people closest to it make the goal even better. Argentina's forward Jorge Valdano later said that Maradona told him afterwards he had been looking for the right moment to pass to him the whole way. He never found it, so he kept going.

The other detail goes back six years. On 13 May 1980 Maradona had played at Wembley in a friendly and done something very similar: he ran through the England defence, took on the goalkeeper Ray Clemence, and shot wide instead of going round him. Afterwards his younger brother Hugo, known in the family as El Turco, told him he should have taken the ball round the keeper. Maradona said later that he had argued back at the time. In 1986, faced with Shilton, he remembered, and went round him. In 2002 the goal was voted FIFA's Goal of the Century in an online poll.

## The turn

The decision that shaped everything was made in a fraction of a second under a dropping ball, and it was made by a player who, by his own account, knew exactly what he was doing. Maradona did not reach for the ball by accident. He judged that he would lose the header, and he cheated. The officials' failure to see it was the accident; the intent was his.

That is why the two goals cannot be separated. The first is remembered because it was a lie the referee believed. The second is remembered because it was the truest thing he ever did on a football pitch. Four minutes apart, against the same opponents, in the same shirt, they gave the world both Maradonas at once.

England did come back into it. With nine minutes left Gary Lineker headed in a cross from John Barnes to make it 2–1, and England pressed until the end, but Argentina held on and went on to win the World Cup.

He also never pretended that the match was only about football. The Falklands War had ended four years earlier, and in his autobiography he wrote about what beating England had meant to Argentines in terms that went far beyond a quarter-final.

> It was like beating a country, not a football team.
> — Diego Maradona, Yo soy el Diego (2000), English translation

As the players left the pitch, Steve Hodge, whose miscued ball had set up the first goal, asked Maradona for his shirt. They swapped in the tunnel. Hodge kept it for decades and lent it for about 20 years to the National Football Museum in Manchester. In May 2022 it sold at Sotheby's for £7,142,500, at the time an auction record for a piece of sports memorabilia. Even that was argued over: members of Maradona's family, including his daughter Dalma, said publicly that the shirt was the one he had worn in the first half, not the second, and Sotheby's replied that its photo-matching showed it was worn after the interval, when both goals were scored. That November, the match ball, which Bin Nasser had kept, also went to auction.

## What it tells us

Greatness and guilt can sit in the same 90 minutes, and in the same man. The Azteca quarter-final endures because it refuses to let anyone choose: the cheat and the genius are the same player, four minutes apart. It also reminds us that the most famous words in the story may never have been spoken at all, which is a useful thing to know about any legend.
