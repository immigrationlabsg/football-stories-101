Six minutes into the second half at the Estadio Azteca, on 22 June 1986, an England midfielder miscued a back-pass.

The ball rose above the penalty spot. Diego Maradona and Peter Shilton went up for it together.

Shilton was much the taller. So Maradona raised his fist and punched the ball into the net.

Argentina led England 1–0 in a World Cup quarter-final, four years after the two countries had been at war, in front of 114,580 people.

Four minutes later Maradona scored again. And the two goals have been arguing over him ever since.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The famous line may never have been said.

The story goes that Maradona told reporters the goal was scored a little with his head and a little with the hand of God. Encyclopaedias repeat it.

But as the Argentine newspaper La Nación has pointed out, there is no film or audio of him saying it. According to La Nación, it was not in the Buenos Aires papers the next day. It spread through the Mexican press.

Later in 1986, in a newspaper column of his own, Maradona wrote that it was completely false that he had said it.

Whether the line was his, or a reporter’s, nobody has settled.

It became his anyway.

The hand itself is not in doubt. In his autobiography in 2000 he called it the hand of Diego. In 2005, on his own television show, he said plainly that he had punched it because he could not head it past Shilton, and that he did not regret it for a second.

Shilton said the apology, if that is what it was, had come too late.

The officials told different stories for the rest of their lives. The Tunisian referee, Ali Bin Nasser, said his view had been blocked and he waited for a signal that never came. His Bulgarian linesman, Bogdan Dochev, put it the other way round.

Then came the part that surprised me.

The second goal began in Argentina’s own half. Over roughly 60 yards and about 11 seconds, Maradona went past five England players and then round Shilton.

Jorge Valdano, his team-mate, later said Maradona told him he had been looking for the right moment to pass to him the whole way.

He never found it. So he kept going.

And the run had been rehearsed, in a way, six years earlier.

At Wembley in 1980, Maradona ran through the England defence in a friendly, took on the goalkeeper Ray Clemence and shot wide.

Afterwards his younger brother Hugo told him he should have taken the ball round the keeper. Maradona argued back.

In 1986, faced with Shilton, he remembered. He went round him.

On Argentine radio, Víctor Hugo Morales reached for an image he had used before, Maradona as a kite that flew wherever nobody expected. As Morales put it on air: “Barrilete cósmico… ¿de qué planeta viniste?” Cosmic kite, what planet did you come from?

In 2002 it was voted FIFA’s Goal of the Century in an online poll.

Gary Lineker headed one back with nine minutes left. Argentina held on, 2–1.

As the players left the pitch, Steve Hodge, whose miscued ball had set up the first goal, asked Maradona for his shirt. They swapped in the tunnel.

Hodge lent it for about 20 years to the National Football Museum in Manchester. In May 2022 it sold at Sotheby’s for £7,142,500, at the time an auction record for sports memorabilia.

Even that was argued over. Maradona’s family said it was his first-half shirt. Sotheby’s said its photo-matching showed it was worn after the interval, when both goals were scored.

Here's what stays with me.

The cheat and the genius are the same man, four minutes apart, and nobody gets to choose one. And the most famous words in the story may never have been spoken at all, which is worth remembering about any legend.
