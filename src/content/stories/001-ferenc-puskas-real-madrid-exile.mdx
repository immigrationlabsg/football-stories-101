On the evening of 22 November 1956, Ferenc Puskás walked out at San Mamés in Bilbao wearing the red and white of Budapest Honvéd for a European Cup tie that no longer felt like football. Eighteen days earlier Soviet tanks had rolled back into Budapest to end the Hungarian uprising. The Honvéd players had left the country before the fighting reached its climax, and now they were playing a first-round first leg against Athletic Bilbao while their families waited at home for news. Honvéd lost 3–2. Nobody in the dressing room thought the score was the important result of the night.

The return leg could not be played in Budapest. It was moved to the Heysel Stadium in Brussels on 20 December 1956 and finished 3–3, which put Honvéd out 6–5 on aggregate. Then came the decision that defined the rest of Puskás's life. Instead of returning, the squad went on tour, through Italy, Spain and on to Brazil, against the wishes of the Hungarian federation and FIFA. When the tour ended, most of the players went home. Puskás, Sándor Kocsis and Zoltán Czibor did not.

He was 29, the captain of the best national team in the world, and he had just made himself an exile.

## The Galloping Major

To understand what Puskás gave up, start with what he had built. He made his debut for Kispest, his neighbourhood club in south Budapest, as a 16-year-old in 1943. When the club was taken over by the army in 1949 and renamed Honvéd, Puskás was given a nominal rank, and the English press later turned that into a nickname: the Galloping Major. He was short and thick-set, with a left foot that did almost everything and a right foot that, by his own cheerful admission, was mainly for standing on.

Between 1950 and 1954 Hungary did not lose a match. They won Olympic gold in Helsinki in 1952, and on 25 November 1953 they went to Wembley and beat England 6–3, the first time a side from outside the British Isles had won there. Puskás scored twice. The second of them, a drag-back that left the England captain Billy Wright tackling thin air before a shot into the roof of the net, is still the goal most people picture when they hear his name.

> He went past Wright like a fire engine going to the wrong fire.
> — Geoffrey Green, The Times, 26 November 1953

In the return match in Budapest six months later Hungary won 7–1. England's players had never seen anything like it. Bobby Robson, then a young professional watching at Wembley, remembered it decades later as the day English football discovered it had been overtaken.

> We saw a style of play, a system of play that we had never seen before. None of these players meant anything to us. We didn't know about Puskás. All these fantastic players, they were men from Mars as far as we were concerned.
> — Bobby Robson, recalling 25 November 1953

Then came the 1954 World Cup in Switzerland, and the afternoon that still hurts in Hungary. Puskás had been kicked on the ankle by Werner Liebrich in an 8–3 group-stage win over West Germany and missed the quarter-final and semi-final. He insisted on playing the final in Bern on 4 July 1954, and for eight minutes it looked like the right call: he scored the opening goal and Hungary led 2–0. West Germany came back to win 3–2. In the 88th minute Puskás put the ball in the net for what would have been the equaliser, and the Welsh linesman Mervyn Griffiths flagged for offside. Hungarian football never fully recovered from that day. Two years later, Puskás was gone.

## Thirty-one, heavy and unwanted

FIFA responded to the players who stayed abroad with a suspension. For Puskás it meant no competitive football for the best part of two years. He drifted through Austria and Italy, putting on weight, playing exhibition matches, fielding offers from clubs that could not get his registration cleared. Inter Milan looked, and looked away. By the summer of 1958 he was 31, out of condition, and in the view of most of European football, finished.

The man who disagreed was Emil Österreicher, who had been Honvéd's technical director and was now scouting for Real Madrid. He persuaded Santiago Bernabéu to take a chance. Madrid already had Alfredo Di Stéfano, Francisco Gento and Raymond Kopa, and had won the first three European Cups. They did not need a fat Hungarian who had not played a serious match since 1956. They signed him anyway.

What happened next is the reason the story is told. Puskás trained the weight off, settled in beside Di Stéfano rather than competing with him, and started scoring at a rate that made the previous two years look like a rumour. In his first season he scored 21 league goals. In his second, 1959–60, he was La Liga's top scorer with 26. He won that award, the Pichichi, four times in five seasons, and Real Madrid won the league five years running from 1961 to 1965. In eight seasons in Spain he scored 156 goals in 180 league matches. He had arrived at an age when most forwards of his era had already retired.

The Spanish press called him Cañoncito Pum, the little cannon. The nickname was affectionate, and it was accurate. He did not beat defenders with pace. He beat them with a first touch that put the ball exactly where his left foot wanted it, and then he hit it before anyone had worked out that he was going to shoot.

## Hampden Park, 18 May 1960

Real Madrid had won the 1959 European Cup final against Reims without him; he was injured and watched from the stand. A year later, in front of 127,621 people at Hampden Park in Glasgow, he played the final that fixed his place in the game's memory. The opponents were Eintracht Frankfurt, who had put 12 goals past Rangers over two semi-final legs and took the lead after 18 minutes. Di Stéfano equalised, Di Stéfano scored again, and then Puskás took over: a close-range finish just before half-time, a penalty, a turn and shot from a tight angle, and a header. Four goals in a European Cup final. Di Stéfano completed his hat-trick, and Real Madrid won 7–3. Many of the Scots in the ground stayed behind to applaud a team that was not theirs.

Puskás played two more finals. In Amsterdam in 1962 he scored a hat-trick and still finished on the losing side, 5–3 to a Benfica team with a young Eusébio. In Vienna in 1964 Madrid lost 3–1 to Inter. By the time the club won the trophy again in Brussels in 1966, he was 39 and no longer in the side, though his name was on the roll of honour for a third time. He remains the only player to score a hat-trick in two European Cup finals.

There was one more twist. Having been told he would never play for Hungary again, he took Spanish citizenship and won four caps for Spain, including three at the 1962 World Cup in Chile. He did not score. His international goals stayed where they were: 84 in 85 matches for Hungary, a ratio almost nobody in the modern game has matched.

## The turn

The decision that explains the outcome was made in Brussels and Milan in the winter of 1956, and it was not really a decision about football. Puskás chose not to go back to a country whose uprising had just been crushed, knowing it meant a ban, the loss of his captaincy and, as far as anyone could tell, the end of his career. Kocsis and Czibor made the same choice and both went to Barcelona, where they were good rather than great. What made Puskás different was less the talent, which all three had, than a stubbornness about his own body. He was the one who got fit again.

He did go home eventually. He coached around the world after retiring, most memorably taking Panathinaikos of Athens to the 1971 European Cup final at Wembley, where they lost 2–0 to the Ajax of Johan Cruyff. He visited Hungary for the first time in 25 years in 1981, returned for good in the early 1990s, and briefly managed the national team in 1993. Budapest's national stadium was renamed for him in 2002. When he died on 17 November 2006, aged 79, he was given a state funeral and buried under the dome of St Stephen's Basilica. The country that had banned him from its football for a generation buried him like a king.

## What it tells us

Age and condition are facts, but they are not verdicts. Puskás was written off at 31 by people looking at a photograph rather than at a player, and the club that ignored the photograph won five league titles with him. It is also a reminder of how much of football history is really political history. The greatest Hungarian team there has ever been was dismantled not by opponents but by tanks, and the goals Puskás scored in white were, in a sense, goals that Hungary lost.
