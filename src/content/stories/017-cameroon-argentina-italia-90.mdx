Around the hour mark of the opening match of Italia 90, Cameroon went down to ten men.

It was 8 June 1990, at San Siro in Milan. Their opponents were Argentina, the world champions, captained by Diego Maradona.

The script was obvious. Cameroon would sit deep, hang on and hope.

About six minutes later, François Omam-Biyik rose in the Argentine box and headed the ball down towards goal.

Nery Pumpido got his hands to it. The ball squirmed through them and into the net.

Cameroon won 1–0, and finished with nine men.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Cameroon's whole summer had begun with a phone call to an island in the Indian Ocean.

Roger Milla had retired from international football. He was living on Réunion, the French territory east of Madagascar, playing for a local club, JS Saint-Pierroise.

He turned 38 on 20 May 1990, 19 days before the opening match.

Then Paul Biya, the president of Cameroon, rang him personally and asked him to come back.

Cameroon's head coach, the little-known Soviet Valeri Nepomniachi, did not want him. Accounts agree that the president's word settled it.

A head of state had overruled his national coach over a 38-year-old who had stepped away from international football. In most stories like that, the president is proved wrong.

First, though, came Milan.

Inside the first ten minutes Benjamin Massing went through the back of Maradona and was booked.

The score was still 0–0 when Claudio Caniggia surged forward, André Kana-Biyik brought him down, and the French referee Michel Vautrot sent him off.

The goal followed in the 67th minute. It was not a powerful header.

But here's where it gets interesting.

The strangest thing about that night was the crowd.

More than 73,000 people filled San Siro. FIFA's figure is 79,780, other records give 73,780. The Milanese had no intention of supporting Argentina.

Maradona played his club football for Napoli, in the south. Milan showed him no affection. The Argentine anthem was booed before kick-off, and whenever Argentina had the ball, the noise was for Cameroon.

Cameroon gave them plenty to cheer, not always politely. In the closing minutes Caniggia broke again, and Massing stopped him with another heavy challenge.

Second yellow. Nine men.

The holders had Maradona and Caniggia and a two-man advantage. They still could not find a way through.

Maradona aimed his bitterness at the stands, not at the team that had beaten him. As he put it afterwards, in an English version of his Spanish: "My only pleasure this afternoon was in discovering that the Italians in Milan have stopped being racists: today, for the first time, they supported the Africans."

And the 38-year-old the president had recalled did not wait long.

Against Romania in Bari, Milla came off the bench and scored both goals in a 2–1 win.

Cameroon then lost 4–0 to the Soviet Union and still finished top of their group.

Against Colombia in Naples it was goalless after 90 minutes. Milla came on and scored twice in extra time. The second came after he took the ball off the Colombian goalkeeper, René Higuita, who had wandered far off his line.

Four goals at the tournament. Every one of them as a substitute. And a dance the whole tournament remembered.

That took Cameroon to a quarter-final against England, the first time an African team had reached the last eight of a World Cup.

In Naples, England won 3–2 after extra time, Gary Lineker scoring twice from the penalty spot.

But nobody was treating Cameroon as a curiosity any more.

Here's what stays with me.

The moment that explains that night is not the goal. It is the choice, with ten men and a draw already worth celebrating, to go forward anyway. A team built around a recalled 38-year-old and a coach nobody knew simply refused the part written for it.
