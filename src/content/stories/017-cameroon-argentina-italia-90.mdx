On the evening of 8 June 1990, around the hour mark of the opening match of the World Cup, Claudio Caniggia surged forward at San Siro in Milan and André Kana-Biyik brought him down. The French referee, Michel Vautrot, sent the Cameroon player off. Argentina, the world champions, captained by Diego Maradona, were level at 0–0 against a team few people in the stadium knew much about, and now that team had ten men.

The logical next act was obvious. Cameroon would sit deep, hang on, and hope. Instead, six or seven minutes later, they were in the Argentine half again. The ball was lifted into the box, and François Omam-Biyik rose to head it down towards goal. It was not a powerful header. Nery Pumpido, Argentina's goalkeeper, got his hands to it, and the ball squirmed through them and into the net. Cameroon 1, Argentina 0, in the 67th minute.

It is worth pausing on how unlikely that sequence was. A team reduced to ten men is supposed to lose its shape, not find a goal. A world-class goalkeeper is supposed to hold a header that arrives without much pace. And the opening match of a World Cup, staged for the holders as a kind of coronation, is supposed to go to script. Two red cards and a fumble later, it was one of the most famous opening matches the World Cup has ever had.

## A crowd that changed sides

The game had felt strange from the start. A crowd of more than 73,000 filled San Siro (FIFA's figure is 79,780; other records give 73,780), and the Milanese had no intention of supporting Argentina. Maradona played his club football for Napoli, in the south, and the Milan crowd showed him no affection. The Argentine anthem was booed and whistled before kick-off. When Argentina had the ball, the noise was for Cameroon.

Cameroon gave the crowd something to cheer straight away, though not always in the spirit of the game. Inside the first ten minutes Benjamin Massing went through the back of Maradona and was booked. It set the tone. Cameroon played with an aggression that unsettled the champions, and by one player's account the battle had started before the teams even reached the pitch.

> When we entered the shared warm-up area, the South Americans seemed a little haughty. As soon as we launched into our battle cries, I saw the fear in their eyes.
> — André Kana-Biyik, recalling the warm-up, in an interview published by FIFA

Cameroon were not only defending. At one point a shot from Cyrille Makanaky was scrambled off the line by a frantic Argentine defence. But for an hour neither side scored, and then came Caniggia's run, Kana-Biyik's dismissal, and the goal.

## Nine men

Cameroon spent the last 20-odd minutes defending a lead with ten men against a side with Maradona and Caniggia in it, and in the closing minutes they gave themselves an even harder job. Caniggia broke again, and Massing, already on a yellow card, stopped him with another heavy challenge. The second booking meant a second red, and there was no room for argument: Massing had already been warned in the opening minutes for a challenge on Maradona. For the final moments Cameroon had nine men on the field.

What followed was survival football of the most basic kind, a team two men short trying to keep the ball away from its own goal for as long as the clock allowed. Argentina had Maradona, Caniggia and the knowledge that they were the better-known team, and none of it was enough to find a way through.

They held on. When Vautrot blew for full time, Cameroon had beaten the holders, in the opening match, in front of a crowd that had adopted them as its own.

Maradona's response was bitter and sharp, and he aimed it at the stands rather than at the team that had beaten him.

> My only pleasure this afternoon was in discovering that the Italians in Milan have stopped being racists: today, for the first time, they supported the Africans.
> — Diego Maradona, speaking to reporters after the match, 8 June 1990 (translated from Spanish; English versions vary slightly)

## The man from Réunion

The defeat was the headline, but the story of Cameroon's summer had begun months earlier, with a telephone call to an island in the Indian Ocean.

Roger Milla had retired from international football. He was living on Réunion, the French territory east of Madagascar, and playing for a local club, JS Saint-Pierroise. Réunion is a very long way from the centre of world football, and it was not a place where anyone expected to find a World Cup forward. He turned 38 on 20 May 1990, 19 days before the opening match. Then Paul Biya, the president of Cameroon, rang him personally and asked him to come back.

The request was not universally welcome. Cameroon's head coach was Valeri Nepomniachi, a little-known Soviet coach. Accounts agree that Milla's recall went against the coach's wishes, and that the president's word settled it. Milla went to Italy.

It is hard to think of a cleaner example of politics reaching into a dressing room. A head of state had overruled his national coach on a squad place, for a 38-year-old who had stepped away from the international game. In most stories of that kind, the president is proved wrong.

What happened next made him the face of the tournament. Against Romania in Bari, he came off the bench and scored both goals in a 2–1 win. Cameroon then lost 4–0 to the Soviet Union and still finished top of their group. In the round of 16 against Colombia in Naples, the game was goalless after 90 minutes. Milla came on and scored twice in extra time, the second after he dispossessed Colombia's goalkeeper, René Higuita, who was caught far off his line. Colombia pulled one back, but Cameroon won 2–1. Milla had scored four goals at the tournament, all of them as a substitute, and he celebrated with a dance that the whole tournament remembered.

That took Cameroon to a quarter-final against England in Naples on 1 July, the first time an African team had reached the last eight of a World Cup. Emmanuel Kundé and Eugène Ekéké scored for Cameroon, but England won 3–2 after extra time, Gary Lineker scoring twice from the penalty spot.

## The turn

The moment that explains the evening in Milan is not the goal but what came just before it. When Kana-Biyik was sent off, Cameroon had every reason to retreat and play for a draw that would already have counted as a triumph. They did not. Within about six minutes, with ten men, they were pushing forward into the Argentine half, and from one of those attacks came the only goal.

That refusal to play the part written for them ran through the whole tournament: a squad with a Soviet coach of modest reputation and a 38-year-old striker summoned from an Indian Ocean island by the head of state, who between them beat the world champions, topped their group and took England to extra time in a quarter-final.

The Argentina match also changed how Cameroon were seen by everyone they met afterwards. Opponents could no longer treat them as a curiosity; the side that had beaten the world champions with nine men had to be taken seriously by everyone else in the draw. By the time they reached the quarter-final, they were no longer the outsiders in the tournament story. They were the story.

## What it tells us

Reputations are fragile on opening nights, when nobody has found their rhythm and the favourites have the most to lose. Cameroon arrived at Italia 90 as a curiosity and left as the team that changed how the world looked at African football. The lasting image is not the champions losing but nine men refusing to accept that they were supposed to.
