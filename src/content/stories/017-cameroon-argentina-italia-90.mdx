Around the hour mark of the opening match of Italia 90, Cameroon went down to ten men.

It was 8 June 1990, at San Siro. Their opponents were Argentina, the world champions, captained by Diego Maradona.

About six minutes later, François Omam-Biyik rose and headed the ball towards goal. It squirmed through Nery Pumpido's hands.

Cameroon won 1–0, and finished with nine men.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Cameroon's summer had begun with a phone call to an island in the Indian Ocean.

Roger Milla, aged 38, had retired from international football. He was living on Réunion, playing for a local club.

Then Cameroon's president, Paul Biya, rang him personally.

The head coach, the little-known Soviet Valeri Nepomniachi, did not want him. The president's word settled it.

André Kana-Biyik brought down Claudio Caniggia and was sent off. The goal followed in the 67th minute.

But here's where it gets interesting.

The crowd was on Cameroon's side.

Maradona played his club football for Napoli, in the south. Milan showed him no affection. The Argentine anthem was booed, and whenever Argentina had the ball, the noise was for Cameroon.

In the closing minutes Benjamin Massing, booked early for a foul on Maradona, stopped Caniggia with another heavy challenge.

Second yellow. Nine men.

Argentina had two extra men. They still could not find a way through.

Against Romania, Milla came off the bench and scored both goals in a 2–1 win. Against Colombia he came on and scored twice in extra time.

Four goals at the tournament, every one as a substitute.

That took Cameroon to a quarter-final against England, the first African team to reach the last eight of a World Cup. England won 3–2 after extra time.

Here's what stays with me.

The moment that explains that night is not the goal. It is the choice, with ten men, to go forward anyway.

A team built around a recalled 38-year-old simply refused the part written for it.
