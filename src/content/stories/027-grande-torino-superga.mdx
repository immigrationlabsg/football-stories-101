Just after five o’clock on 4 May 1949, a plane came down through thick fog towards Turin.

It was bringing Torino home from Lisbon.

In the fog, the crew did not see the hill of Superga. The aircraft struck the retaining wall behind its basilica.

All 31 people on board were killed. Eighteen of them were players.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

They had flown to Lisbon to keep a promise.

Benfica’s captain, Francisco Ferreira, was retiring. He had won from Torino’s captain, Valentino Mazzola, a promise that the Grande Torino would come to his farewell match.

They kept it on 3 May, and lost 4–3.

This was no ordinary side. Five consecutive Serie A titles, the last confirmed only after the crash.

On 11 May 1947, Italy beat Hungary 3–2 with ten Torino players on the pitch.

Among the dead was Ernő Egri Erbstein, the Hungarian who had shaped the side. Erbstein was Jewish. During the war he escaped a Nazi forced labour camp, survived, and came back to Turin.

Then came the part that surprised me.

So much turned on who was not on the plane.

Sauro Tomà, a defender, stayed in Turin with a damaged knee.

The third-choice goalkeeper, Dino Ballarin, went because his brother Aldo, a Torino defender, argued for him to be taken.

Both Ballarin brothers died at Superga.

László Kubala had recently joined Torino and was due to play in Lisbon. His son was ill, and Kubala stayed with him.

Vittorio Pozzo, the former Italy coach, declined an invitation to travel. His seat went to a journalist, who was killed.

It then fell to Pozzo to identify the bodies.

Torino were declared champions with four matches left, and played them with their youth team.

Their opponents fielded youth teams too.

Here's what stays with me.

Nobody made a reckless choice. The decisive one was generous: a captain keeping his word to a friend. Everything else, a knee, a sick child, a brother’s persuasion, a polite refusal, sorted the living from the dead.
