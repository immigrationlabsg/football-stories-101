Near the end of an FA Cup fourth-round tie at Elland Road on 25 January 1975, Leeds United were awarded a penalty against a team from the Southern League. Leeds were a First Division side, and the man who placed the ball was Peter Lorimer. In the Wimbledon goal stood Dickie Guy. He saved it. The match finished 0–0, and Guy, and the club most of England had never thought about, found themselves famous across the country. Guy was no passing hero. Between 1967 and 1978 he made nearly 600 first-team appearances for Wimbledon, at one point 275 in a row, and he was the constant in the side that turned a Southern League club into a Football League one.

Wimbledon lost the replay, staged at Selhurst Park, 1–0, and the only goal was a shot that went in off the knee of one of their defenders. His name was Dave Bassett. Thirteen years later he would be the man who had built the team that won the FA Cup, and another Wimbledon goalkeeper would be saving another penalty, this time at Wembley.

## Plough Lane to the Football League

The Leeds tie was the high point of a run that had already beaten a First Division side. In the third round Wimbledon had gone to Turf Moor and beaten Burnley 1–0. They were, at the time, chasing the Southern League title, and they won it three seasons in a row. In June 1977, at the Football League's annual meeting, they were elected to the Fourth Division in place of Workington.

They did not play their way into the League; they were voted in, and an existing member, Workington, was voted out to make room. Three titles in a row, and a cup run that had taken a First Division scalp at Burnley and held Leeds United to a draw at Elland Road, had made their case for them.

What followed was not a smooth climb. In their first few seasons Wimbledon went up and down between the bottom two divisions. Bassett, who had played for the club from 1974 to 1978, became manager in 1981, and the team he built won three promotions in four seasons.

In 1982–83, only the second season in which English league football awarded three points for a win, Wimbledon won the Fourth Division with 98 points, a Football League record for a season played under three points for a win. In 1983–84 they finished second in the Third Division and went up again. In 1985–86 they finished third in the Second Division, taking the last promotion place, and a 1–0 win away at Huddersfield Town carried them into the First Division. Nine years after leaving the Southern League they were in the top flight, four seasons after they had been in the bottom tier.

## The First Division

They lost their first top-flight match, away at Manchester City. Then they won four in a row, and by the start of September 1986 Wimbledon were top of the First Division. Sources disagree by a day on exactly when, the 1st or the 2nd. The stay at the top did not last, but neither did the team fall away. Wimbledon finished their first season in the top division in sixth place.

That summer Bassett resigned, and Bobby Gould took over. He inherited a squad that had finished sixth, and a team that the rest of the First Division no longer took lightly.

The road to Wembley in 1987–88 was built on the qualities Bassett had drilled into them. Wimbledon beat West Bromwich Albion 4–1 at home in the third round, won 2–1 at Mansfield Town, 3–1 at Newcastle United, and 2–1 at home to Watford in the quarter-final. In the semi-final at White Hart Lane they beat Luton Town 2–1, with a John Fashanu penalty and a goal from Dennis Wise.

Their opponents in the final were Liverpool, newly crowned league champions.

## The turn

The moment that decided the 1988 final, and which explains how Wimbledon won it, came from preparation rather than nerve. Their captain and goalkeeper, Dave Beasant, had spent the season watching John Aldridge take penalties on television. He noticed, as he later explained, that Aldridge used a stutter in his run-up and that goalkeepers tended to commit themselves when he paused. In the days before Wembley he had also practised facing penalties from Fashanu. Beasant decided that if he held his position through the stutter, Aldridge would put the ball to his left.

It was the logic of the whole club in miniature. Wimbledon could not match Liverpool's players man for man. What they could do was refuse to be surprised. A penalty is the one moment in football when a goalkeeper knows exactly who will strike the ball and from where, and Beasant had turned that into an advantage weeks before the match was played. The save that followed looked like instinct. It was homework.

The same habit runs through the whole climb. A Southern League side does not beat Burnley away and hold Leeds by accident, and a Fourth Division side does not reach the top flight in four seasons on enthusiasm alone. Bassett's teams and then Gould's were built to make the most of what they had, and to take nothing about their opponents on trust.

## Wembley, 14 May 1988

The final was played in front of a crowd of nearly 100,000. Wimbledon took the lead shortly before half-time: Wise curled in a free kick from the left and Lawrie Sanchez met it with a looping header that dropped into the net. Most records give the minute as the 37th; Liverpool's own match archive gives the 36th.

In the second half Liverpool were given a penalty when Clive Goodyear challenged Aldridge in the area. Aldridge stuttered in his run, as Beasant had predicted, and struck the ball to Beasant's left. Beasant was already going that way. In his own later telling, he flung himself to his left and pushed the ball away. No goalkeeper had ever saved a penalty in an FA Cup final before.

Liverpool did not score. When the final whistle went, the BBC commentator John Motson summed up the afternoon in a single sentence. His line carried the nickname into the newspaper reports and broadcasts that followed, and it has stayed attached to that team ever since.

> The Crazy Gang have beaten the Culture Club.
> — John Motson, BBC television commentary, 14 May 1988

Beasant climbed the steps to receive the trophy as Wimbledon's captain. Sanchez, asked three decades later whether the result had been a shock to the team, said it had not been, and that they had gone to Wembley expecting to win.

> Liverpool held no fear for us.
> — Lawrie Sanchez, speaking to The Irish Post, May 2018

Eleven years earlier Wimbledon had been a non-league club. Thirteen years earlier their goalkeeper had made the save that first put them on the national stage, and the ball that knocked them out had gone in off the knee of their future manager. Now a Wimbledon goalkeeper's save had won them the Cup. Guy's save at Elland Road had earned a replay and a place in the club's legend. Beasant's at Wembley protected the lead that brought the trophy. Between them lay the whole of Wimbledon's journey, from a non-league ground to the Football League, through all four divisions and up the Wembley steps.

## What it tells us

Wimbledon's rise is usually told as a story of attitude, and the attitude was real. But the detail that won the Cup was a goalkeeper doing his homework on one opponent's run-up. Clubs without money can still choose to know more than the opposition, and to make their limits into a method. Wimbledon did both, and for one afternoon it was enough to beat the best team in England.
