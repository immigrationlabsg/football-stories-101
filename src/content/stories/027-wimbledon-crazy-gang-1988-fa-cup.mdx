In the second half of the 1988 FA Cup final, Wimbledon were 1–0 up against Liverpool, the newly crowned league champions.

Then Clive Goodyear challenged John Aldridge in the area. Penalty.

No goalkeeper had ever saved a penalty in an FA Cup final.

Aldridge stuttered in his run-up and struck it to the goalkeeper's left. Dave Beasant was already going that way, and pushed it away.

Thirteen years earlier, Wimbledon had been a Southern League club.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The save looked like instinct. It was homework.

Beasant, Wimbledon's captain and goalkeeper, had spent the season watching Aldridge take penalties on television. He noticed a stutter in the run-up, and that goalkeepers tended to commit themselves when Aldridge paused. In the days before the final he had practised facing penalties from John Fashanu.

His conclusion: hold your ground through the stutter, and Aldridge will go to your left.

And it was not the first famous penalty save in Wimbledon's story.

Near the end of an FA Cup tie at Elland Road in January 1975, Leeds United were given a penalty against the Southern League side. Peter Lorimer placed the ball. Wimbledon's goalkeeper, Dickie Guy, saved it.

The match finished 0–0, and a club most of England had never thought about was suddenly famous.

Wimbledon lost the replay 1–0. The only goal went in off the knee of one of their own defenders.

His name was Dave Bassett. Remember him.

Wimbledon won the Southern League three seasons in a row, and in June 1977 they were voted into the Fourth Division in place of Workington.

They did not play their way in. They were elected.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Bassett became manager in 1981. In 1982–83, only the second season of three points for a win, Wimbledon won the Fourth Division with 98 points, a Football League record under that system. They went up again the next season.

In 1985–86 a 1–0 win at Huddersfield took them into the First Division.

Nine years after leaving the Southern League, they were in the top flight.

By early September 1986 they were top of it. Sources differ by a day on exactly when.

They finished their first top-flight season sixth. Bassett resigned that summer, and Bobby Gould took over.

A year later they were at Wembley. On 14 May 1988, in front of nearly 100,000, Dennis Wise curled in a free kick shortly before half-time and Lawrie Sanchez looped a header into the net. Most records say the 37th minute; Liverpool's own archive says the 36th.

After Beasant's save, Liverpool did not score. At the final whistle, as John Motson put it on BBC television: "The Crazy Gang have beaten the Culture Club."

Beasant climbed the steps to receive the Cup as captain.

Thirteen years separated the two penalty saves. Guy's at Elland Road earned a replay and a place in the club's legend. Beasant's at Wembley protected the lead that won the trophy.

And the defender whose knee had knocked Wimbledon out in 1975 was the man who had built the team that won it. Bassett had played for the club, then managed it to three promotions in four seasons.

Here's what stays with me.

The attitude was real, but attitude did not save that penalty. What saved it was a goalkeeper who knew more than his opponent. Clubs without money can still choose to do the homework. For one afternoon, it was enough to beat the best team in England.
