On 4 November 2001, Michael Owen scored twice for Liverpool against Manchester United at Anfield. David Beckham scored United's goal. Liverpool won 3–1.

Owen scored the first, and a John Arne Riise free kick made it 2–0 by half-time.

Beckham, in United's number 7, pulled one back five minutes after half-time. Within a minute, Owen had scored again.

Same afternoon. Same pitch. Opposite ends of the result.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Six weeks later, the two men were rivals again, this time in the votes for the player of the year.

In France Football's Ballon d'Or, Owen won with 176 points. He was the first English winner since Kevin Keegan in 1979.

Beckham finished fourth, with 102.

FIFA's World Player of the Year, voted for by national team coaches, came out very differently.

Beckham got more first-place votes than anyone. Thirty coaches put him top, three more than picked the eventual winner, Luís Figo.

He still finished second. Figo had more second-place votes, 35 to 23, and won by 12 points, 250 to 238.

So in the same December, one panel made Owen the best player in Europe, and the coaches picked Beckham first more often than any other player in the world, without giving him the award.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Their paths kept crossing.

In 2004 Owen left Liverpool for Real Madrid, where Beckham had arrived a year earlier.

In 2009, with his contract at relegated Newcastle over, Owen joined Manchester United on a free transfer.

United gave him the number 7. It was the shirt Beckham had worn at Anfield that afternoon.

And the striker who had scored twice against United won the only league title of his career with them, in 2011.

Here's what stays with me.

One afternoon put them on opposite sides.

Two votes in the same December could not agree on which of them had the better year.

And in the end, Owen wore Beckham's number.
