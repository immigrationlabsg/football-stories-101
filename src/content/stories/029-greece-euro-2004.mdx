Before the summer of 2004, Greece had played six matches at major finals.

They had won none of them.

They had scored one goal.

That summer they became champions of Europe.

Here’s what I didn’t know until recently.

The man who changed that was a German. He did not start with tactics. He started with rules.

As Otto Rehhagel put it before the semi-final: “The Greek players were very talented but they had to understand that a team group has rules.”

It did not work at once. Qualifying began with two defeats.

Then Greece won six in a row without conceding, and finished above Spain.

The tournament opened in Porto on 12 June, against Portugal and a 19-year-old called Cristiano Ronaldo.

Giorgos Karagounis scored after seven minutes. Angelos Basinas added a penalty after Ronaldo brought down a Greek defender. Greece won 2–1.

They needed it. After a draw with Spain and a defeat by Russia, they went through on goals scored.

Then came the part that surprised me.

All three knockout matches were won 1–0. All three goals were headers.

Against France, the holders, the captain Theodoros Zagorakis crossed and Angelos Charisteas headed in.

Against the Czech Republic, Traianos Dellas met a corner at the end of the first period of extra time. Under the silver-goal rule, the match ended there.

That sent Greece back to Lisbon, and back to Portugal.

In the 57th minute of the final, Basinas swung in a corner and Charisteas rose above Costinha to head it in. According to UEFA’s report, it was Greece’s only effort on target all night.

It was enough.

Greece conceded four goals, all in the group stage. Zagorakis was named player of the tournament.

Here’s what stays with me.

Greece were not lucky once. They were organised six times. A team of modest reputations did the same unglamorous things for every minute, and beat the hosts twice and the holders once.
