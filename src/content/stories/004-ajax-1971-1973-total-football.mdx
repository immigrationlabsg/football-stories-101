Five minutes into the European Cup final at Wembley on 2 June 1971, Piet Keizer swung a cross in from the left and Dick van Dijk, a forward most of the crowd had never heard of, scored. Ajax Amsterdam led 1–0. On the Greek bench sat the Panathinaikos coach, Ferenc Puskás, who had scored four goals in a European Cup final on a Scottish pitch 11 years earlier and now watched a team do to his side what Real Madrid had once done to everyone. A second goal arrived late on, from Arie Haan, who had come on at half-time. Ajax 2, Panathinaikos 0.

The captain who received the trophy was the Yugoslav defender Velibor Vasović. The player everyone remembered had not scored and did not need to. Johan Cruyff had spent 90 minutes appearing wherever the game was about to happen, and the rest of European football had started to wonder how he knew. In the stands, on crutches, was Ruud Krol, the defender who had broken his leg that spring and missed the night of his career.

## A club near the bottom

The dynasty began with a rescue. On 22 January 1965 Ajax, embroiled in a fight against relegation, appointed as coach a former centre-forward of theirs, Rinus Michels, who had been working as a gymnastics teacher at a school for deaf children. He replaced the Englishman Vic Buckingham. Ajax finished that season 13th in a 16-team league. The following year they were champions, and they won the title again in 1967 and 1968, adding the Dutch cup in 1967.

Michels did two things that were unusual for the time. He made the players fitter than anybody they faced, and he insisted that fitness was not an end but a means: it bought the energy to press high up the pitch and to keep a defensive line so far forward that opponents were repeatedly caught offside. His best-known remark is usually shortened to "football is war". The versions recorded at the time are a little longer and a little more revealing: football, he said, is something like war, and the side that behaves too properly is lost.

The first time the rest of Europe noticed was 7 December 1966, when Liverpool came to Amsterdam's Olympic Stadium for a European Cup second-round first leg and lost 5–1 in fog so thick that spectators sometimes knew a goal had been scored only from the players' celebrations. Bill Shankly later wrote that the match should never have started. The second leg finished 2–2, and Ajax went through 7–3. In 1969 Ajax reached the final itself and were taught a lesson, losing 4–1 to Milan in Madrid, with Pierino Prati scoring three. In 1970 the European Cup went to the Netherlands for the first time, but it was Feyenoord of Rotterdam, under Ernst Happel, who won it, beating Celtic 2–1 in Milan. Ajax's answer was to win everything else.

## Three finals, no goals conceded

The side Michels took to Wembley in 1971 had an architecture that is now taught in coaching courses, and it showed how flexible his players already were. With Krol injured, Heinz Stuy was in goal behind a back four of Wim Suurbier, Barry Hulshoff, Vasović and the 19-year-old Johan Neeskens, a midfielder by instinct who ran further and tackled harder than anyone. Nico Rijnders and Gerrie Mühren were in midfield; Sjaak Swart and Keizer were the wingers; Van Dijk led the line; and Cruyff was nominally a forward and in practice whatever the moment required. At half-time Michels sent on Horst Blankenburg and Haan.

What made the system work was not the formation but the permission. When a full-back went forward, a midfielder dropped into his space; when Cruyff drifted to the wing, someone else became the striker. The pitch was treated as a problem of space rather than of positions. Hulshoff later recalled that the players talked about space constantly, and that Cruyff was forever telling his team-mates where to run, where to stand and when to stay still.

Michels left for Barcelona after the 1971 final. The man the Ajax board hired to replace him was a quietly spoken Romanian, Ștefan Kovács, whom nobody in Amsterdam had expected. Kovács made a decision that looks easy in hindsight and was not: he loosened the discipline and let the players run the system themselves. The result was the best club season anyone had seen.

In 1971–72 Ajax won the league, won the Dutch cup, and won the European Cup for the second time, beating Inter 2–0 in the final at De Kuip in Rotterdam on 31 May 1972 with both goals from Cruyff: a tap-in two minutes after half-time, after an Inter player had cut out Hulshoff's pass, and a header from a corner 14 minutes from the end. In September they won the Intercontinental Cup against Independiente of Argentina, 1–1 in Avellaneda and 3–0 in Amsterdam. In January 1973 they beat Rangers 6–3 on aggregate in what is now remembered as the first European Super Cup, although UEFA refused to endorse it at the time, because Rangers were serving a UEFA ban, and the matches went ahead unofficially as part of Rangers' centenary. In the spring of 1973 Ajax beat Bayern Munich 4–0 in a quarter-final first leg in Amsterdam, beat Real Madrid 3–1 on aggregate in the semi-final, winning 1–0 at the Bernabéu, and on 30 May 1973 in Belgrade beat Juventus 1–0 with a header from Johnny Rep after four minutes. Three consecutive European Cups, three finals, no goal conceded in any of them. Only Real Madrid, with five in a row from 1956, had won three straight before them.

## The vote

Kovács left in the summer of 1973; Romanian rules limited how long he could work abroad. Under his successor, George Knobel, the squad held a vote for captain before the new season. Cruyff, who had held the armband, expected to keep it. The players chose Keizer instead. Cruyff had already been talking to Barcelona, and when the Ajax chairman offered him a better contract and the armband back, he still wanted to go. Within weeks he had followed Michels to Barcelona for a fee reported at about six million guilders, a world record at the time. Nobody has ever settled how much the vote decided and how much it merely confirmed, but the sequence is not in doubt. Neeskens joined him in 1974. Ajax did not win the European Cup again until 1995.

Cruyff spent the rest of his life insisting that the way Ajax had played was a matter of thinking rather than of money or talent, and he had the results to make the argument stick: a World Cup final with the Netherlands in 1974, the Spanish league title with Barcelona in his first season as a player, and later, as a coach, the Barcelona team that won the club's first European Cup at Wembley in 1992 and laid the foundations for the Barcelona sides that followed. The line most often attributed to him, though nobody has traced where he first said it, is that playing football is very simple, but playing simple football is the hardest thing there is.

## The turn

The decision that made the dynasty was Michels's, in 1965, to treat conditioning not as a virtue in itself but as the price of a style. Everything that followed, the pressing, the offside line, the interchange of positions, depended on 11 players who could run for 90 minutes at a tempo their opponents could not sustain. The decision that ended it was the players' own, eight years later, in a vote about status rather than football. Michels died in 2005, Cruyff in March 2016 and Neeskens in October 2024.

## What it tells us

Dynasties are built on ideas and dismantled by people. Ajax's three European Cups came from a way of thinking about space that one coach imposed and a second coach was wise enough to leave alone, and it fell apart not because anyone found a tactical answer but because a dressing room made a decision about status. The idea, though, outlived the team: it travelled with Michels and Cruyff to Barcelona, and it is still visible, half a century on, in the way the best sides in the world defend by attacking.
