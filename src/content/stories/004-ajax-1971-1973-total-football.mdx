In January 1965 Ajax were fighting relegation, and they turned to a gymnastics teacher.

Rinus Michels had been teaching at a school for deaf children.

The following year Ajax were champions.

By 1973 they had won three European Cups in a row.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The genius of the system was not the formation. It was the permission.

Michels made his players fitter than anyone they faced. But fitness was never the point. It was the price of a style.

It bought the energy to press high and defend far up the pitch.

When a full-back went forward, a midfielder dropped into his space. When Johan Cruyff drifted wide, someone else became the striker.

The pitch was a problem of space, not of positions.

In 1969 they lost a European Cup final 4–1 to Milan. In 1971, at Wembley, they won one.

Then Michels left for Barcelona.

But here's where it gets interesting.

The board replaced him with a quietly spoken Romanian, Ștefan Kovács.

Kovács loosened the discipline and let the players run the system themselves.

In 1971–72 Ajax won the league, the Dutch cup and the European Cup, beating Inter 2–0 with two goals from Cruyff.

In 1973 they beat Juventus 1–0 in Belgrade, with a Johnny Rep header after four minutes.

Three European Cups in a row. Not one goal conceded in the finals.

And the dynasty was not ended by an opponent. It was ended by a vote.

In the summer of 1973 the squad voted for a captain. Cruyff had expected to keep the armband.

The players chose Piet Keizer.

Within weeks Cruyff had joined Michels in Barcelona for a then world-record fee. Johan Neeskens followed in 1974.

Ajax did not win the European Cup again until 1995.

Here's what stays with me.

Dynasties are built on ideas and dismantled by people.

One coach imposed the idea, and a second was wise enough to leave it alone.

And the idea outlived the team. It is still visible in the way the best sides defend by attacking.
