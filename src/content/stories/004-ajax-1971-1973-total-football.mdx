In January 1965 Ajax were fighting relegation, and they turned to a gymnastics teacher.

His name was Rinus Michels. He was a former Ajax centre-forward, and he had been teaching at a school for deaf children.

Ajax finished that season 13th in a 16-team league.

The following year they were champions.

By 1973 they had won three European Cups in a row, without conceding a goal in any of the finals.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The genius of the system was not the formation. It was the permission.

Michels began with something unusual for the time. He made his players fitter than anyone they faced.

But fitness was never the point. It was the price of a style.

It bought the energy to press high and to hold a defensive line so far forward that opponents kept getting caught offside.

When a full-back went forward, a midfielder dropped into his space. When Johan Cruyff drifted wide, someone else became the striker.

The pitch was treated as a problem of space, not of positions.

Barry Hulshoff later recalled that the players talked about space constantly, and that Cruyff was forever telling team-mates where to run, where to stand and when to stay still.

Europe first noticed on 7 December 1966. Liverpool came to Amsterdam's Olympic Stadium and lost 5–1, in fog so thick that spectators sometimes knew a goal had gone in only from the players' celebrations.

The first European Cup final came in 1969, and Ajax were taught a lesson: 4–1 to Milan.

Then the lesson was learned.

On 2 June 1971 at Wembley, Piet Keizer crossed from the left after five minutes and Dick van Dijk scored.

On the Panathinaikos bench sat their coach, Ferenc Puskás. For Ajax, the 19-year-old Johan Neeskens, a midfielder by instinct, played in the back four.

In the stands, on crutches, sat Ruud Krol. He had broken his leg that spring and missed the night of his career.

Ajax won 2–0.

Then Michels left for Barcelona.

But here's where it gets interesting.

The board replaced him with a quietly spoken Romanian, Ștefan Kovács, whom nobody in Amsterdam had expected.

Kovács made a decision that looks easy now and was not. He loosened the discipline and let the players run the system themselves.

The result was the best club season anyone had seen.

In 1971–72 Ajax won the league, the Dutch cup and the European Cup, beating Inter 2–0 in Rotterdam with two goals from Cruyff.

In September they added the Intercontinental Cup.

In 1973 they beat Bayern Munich 4–0 in a quarter-final first leg, won 1–0 at the Bernabéu, and beat Juventus 1–0 in Belgrade with a Johnny Rep header after four minutes.

Three European Cups in a row. Three finals. Not one goal conceded in any of them.

And the dynasty was not ended by an opponent. It was ended by a vote.

In the summer of 1973, under a new coach, the squad voted for a captain. Cruyff had held the armband and expected to keep it.

The players chose Keizer.

Cruyff had already been talking to Barcelona. The Ajax chairman offered him a better contract and the armband back, and he still wanted to go.

Within weeks he had joined Michels in Barcelona for a then world-record fee.

Nobody has ever settled how much the vote decided and how much it only confirmed.

But the sequence is not in doubt.

Neeskens followed in 1974.

Ajax did not win the European Cup again until 1995.

Here's what stays with me.

Dynasties are built on ideas and dismantled by people.

One coach imposed the idea, and a second was wise enough to leave it alone.

And the idea outlived the team. It travelled to Barcelona, and it is still visible in the way the best sides defend by attacking.
