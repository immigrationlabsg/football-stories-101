Inside the first minute in Istanbul, on 25 May 2005, Paolo Maldini volleyed in a free-kick from Andrea Pirlo.

UEFA's own records disagree by a second, 51 or 52. Either way, no Champions League final has had an earlier goal.

It got worse. Hernán Crespo scored on 39 minutes and again on 44.

At half-time Milan led Liverpool 3–0.

The final looked over.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

What happened in the next 15 minutes, in the dressing room, was not a speech.

It was a muddle about a substitution.

The room was quiet. Rafael Benítez later told ESPN that the usual minute or two of everybody talking simply did not happen.

Jamie Carragher admitted he did not think the game could be turned round.

But Benítez had already decided, during the first half, that the shape was wrong.

Kaká was running through the space in front of the back four, and Steven Gerrard was being dragged back to deal with him.

The fix was Dietmar Hamann. Bring him on to sit on Kaká, push Gerrard forward, switch to three centre-backs.

By his own account, Benítez had sent his fitness coach to get Hamann ready before the half had even finished.

The player due to make way was the left-back, Djimi Traoré.

Then the physio reported that the right-back, Steve Finnan, could not carry on.

So Finnan came off instead, and Traoré was sent back out.

In the confusion, Liverpool nearly sent out 12 men.

Benítez has recalled that he considered putting the striker Djibril Cissé on at right-back, until somebody pointed out that would make 12.

Meanwhile, through the walls, the players could hear thousands of travelling supporters singing "You'll Never Walk Alone" to a team 3–0 down.

Luis García has said that was when they began to believe.

The first goal came on 54 minutes. John Arne Riise crossed, and Gerrard, now much higher up the pitch, headed it in.

Two minutes later Vladimír Šmicer, on only because Harry Kewell was injured, scored from outside the area. It was the last match he ever played for Liverpool.

On the hour Gerrard was brought down. Penalty.

But here's where it gets interesting.

Xabi Alonso took it, and he later said he had never taken a penalty as a professional.

Benítez had given him the job before the final, because Gerrard had missed one against Tottenham.

Dida saved it. Alonso scored the rebound.

Three goals in six minutes. 3–3.

The rest of the night belonged to Milan.

Traoré, the left-back who was supposed to be sitting in the dressing room, cleared a shot from Andriy Shevchenko off the line.

In the 117th minute Shevchenko had two attempts from point-blank range in a second. Jerzy Dudek blocked both.

Before the shoot-out, Carragher ran to Dudek and jumped on his back. Remember Bruce Grobbelaar, he told him, and his wobbly legs in the 1984 final.

Dudek's first instinct was to ask for quiet. As he later recalled it: "Jamie, let me study the penalties first, we've only got two minutes - please, let me concentrate now."

He did both in the end. He raised his arms and moved along his line.

He saved from Pirlo. Then, with Milan needing to score, he saved from Shevchenko.

Liverpool won 3–2 on penalties.

Asked years later how much credit Carragher deserved, Dudek settled on 50/50.

Here's what stays with me.

This is remembered as a triumph of spirit, and it was.

But it rested on a tactical change that was right, a substitution that went wrong in a useful way, and a goalkeeper who took half the advice he was given.

Great nights are often less a plan than a run of lucky corrections.
