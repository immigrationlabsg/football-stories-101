Inside the first minute at the Atatürk Olympic Stadium in Istanbul, on the night of 25 May 2005, Andrea Pirlo swung a free-kick into the Liverpool penalty area and Paolo Maldini, the Milan captain, met it on the volley. Jerzy Dudek did not move. UEFA's own records disagree by a second about the timing, 51 or 52 seconds, but on the central fact they agree: no Champions League final has ever had an earlier goal.

It got worse. Harry Kewell, Liverpool's Australian winger, went off injured after 23 minutes and was replaced by Vladimír Šmicer. Hernán Crespo scored on 39 minutes and again on 44. At half-time Milan, with Kaká running through the space in front of Liverpool's back four as if it had been reserved for him, led 3–0 in a European Cup final, and the final looked over.

What happened in the next 15 minutes, in a dressing room under the stand, is the part of the story most people never hear. It was not a speech. It was a muddle about a substitution.

## Twelve men

The room was quiet. Rafael Benítez, the Liverpool manager, told ESPN two decades later that it was very difficult to walk in and see his players with their heads down, and that the usual minute or two of everybody talking simply did not happen. Dudek, in UEFA's oral history of the night, remembered some players being very angry and others very sad. Jamie Carragher, the centre-back, admitted he was deflated and did not think the game could be turned round. Xabi Alonso, the Spanish midfielder, recalled the room splitting between those who said they had to fight and those who kept asking how they could have been so bad.

But Benítez had decided during the first half, long before the whistle, that the shape was wrong, and the fix was already in motion. Kaká was finding room between the lines and Steven Gerrard was being dragged back to deal with him. He wanted to bring on Dietmar Hamann, the German holding midfielder, to sit on Kaká and release Gerrard further forward, and to switch to three central defenders. He later told the club's website that he had sent his fitness coach, Pako Ayestarán, to get Hamann ready before the half had even finished.

The player who was supposed to make way was Djimi Traoré, the left-back. Traoré has since told FourFourTwo that he had been withdrawn as part of the reshuffle. Then the physio reported that Steve Finnan, the right-back, could not carry on. Finnan came off instead, and Traoré was sent back out.

In the confusion there was a moment when Liverpool risked something even stranger. Benítez has recalled that he considered moving the striker Djibril Cissé, one of his substitutes, to right-back, until somebody pointed out that sending Cissé on as well would give Liverpool 12 men. Hamann, warming up, had his own version of the same panic: he has said he thought for a moment that the team were about to start the second half with one too many.

Outside, something else was going on. Several Liverpool players, among them Luis García, have described sitting in the dressing room and hearing thousands of travelling supporters singing "You'll Never Walk Alone" at a team that was 3–0 down. García has said it was the point at which the players began to believe.

Benítez, asked years later what turned the game, has said the important thing at half-time was what the team did rather than anything that was said: the extra man in midfield, the change of shape, and a simple message that one goal would put them back in it.

## Six minutes

The first goal came on 54 minutes. John Arne Riise crossed and Gerrard, now playing much higher up the pitch, rose to head it past Dida. Liverpool had their one goal.

Two minutes later Šmicer, the substitute who had only come on because Kewell was hurt, hit a shot from outside the penalty area that beat Dida. It was the last match Šmicer ever played for Liverpool.

Then, on the hour, Gerrard broke into the area and was brought down by Gennaro Gattuso. Penalty. Alonso picked up the ball. He later told FourFourTwo that he had never taken a penalty as a professional, and that Benítez had told him before the final that the job was his, because Gerrard had missed one against Tottenham earlier in the season. Dida saved it. The ball came back to Alonso and he scored at the second attempt.

From 54 to 60 minutes, Liverpool had scored three times. It was 3–3.

## Holding on

The rest of the night belonged to Milan. The formation had been rebuilt on the hoof, and the Italian side, recovering from the shock, kept coming. With the game level Traoré, the left-back who was supposed to be sitting in the dressing room, cleared a Shevchenko shot off the line.

The moment that settled it came in the 117th minute of extra time. Andriy Shevchenko, Milan's most dangerous forward, had two attempts from point-blank range in the space of a second, and Dudek blocked both. Luis García later called it a miracle, the kind of miracle they needed to win that game. Shevchenko has since called it inexplicable.

> We dominated the game and I had a double chance that Dudek inexplicably saved. Football is beautiful for that reason. Never take anything for granted.
> — Andriy Shevchenko, interviewed by FourFourTwo

## The turn

The shoot-out was where Liverpool's history caught up with them. In the 1984 European Cup final in Rome, Liverpool had beaten Roma on penalties after their goalkeeper, Bruce Grobbelaar, wobbled his knees on the line in a routine that became known as the spaghetti legs. Before the kicks in Istanbul, Carragher ran to Dudek and jumped on his back, telling him to do the same: put the takers off, remember Grobbelaar.

Dudek's first instinct was to ask for quiet.

> Jamie, let me study the penalties first, we've only got two minutes - please, let me concentrate now.
> — Jerzy Dudek, recalling the shoot-out to Liverpool FC's website

He did both in the end. Dudek has said he raised his arms and moved along his line to make the goal look smaller, a version of Grobbelaar's routine that was his own. Asked years later how much credit Carragher deserved, Dudek settled on 50/50. Serginho, Milan's first taker, shot over the bar. Hamann scored. Dudek saved from Pirlo. Cissé scored. Jon Dahl Tomasson scored for Milan, then Dida saved from Riise, who had crossed for the first goal and was the only Liverpool taker to miss. Kaká scored. Šmicer, in the final minutes of his Liverpool career, scored. That left Shevchenko needing to score to keep Milan alive, and Dudek saved it. Liverpool had won 3–2 on penalties.

The real hinge, though, was the one nobody planned. Benítez wanted Hamann on and Traoré off; the physio's report on Finnan reversed half of that decision, and the half that survived was the important one. Hamann's arrival smothered Kaká, freed Gerrard and gave Liverpool enough of the ball in that six-minute spell to believe. Traoré, reprieved by accident, went on to clear one off the line. Asked years later how it happened, Dudek admitted that he did not really know how they did it. The honest answer is that the plan, the accident and the singing all happened at once.

## What it tells us

Half-time is short, and the decisions made in it are rarely as calm as they look later. Liverpool's comeback is remembered as a triumph of spirit, but it was built on a tactical change that was right, a substitution that went wrong in a useful way, and a goalkeeper who listened to half the advice he was given. Great nights in football are often less a plan than a sequence of lucky corrections.
