Thirteen days after the crash, Manchester United's match programme left a blank where the team should have been.

That is the detail I cannot shake.

The day after a 3–3 draw with Red Star in Belgrade, United’s plane stopped at Munich to refuel. On its third take-off attempt, it ran off the runway.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

United were only in Europe because their manager, Matt Busby, insisted.

A year earlier the authorities had kept Chelsea, the English champions, out. In 1956 United were told the same. Busby went in anyway.

Of the 44 people on board, 23 died. Eight were players, among them Duncan Edwards, aged 21.

The goalkeeper, Harry Gregg, survived, then went back into the burning wreckage.

By accounts including his own, he brought out a 20-month-old girl, then her badly injured, pregnant mother. He dragged out Bobby Charlton, unconscious.

Busby was so badly hurt that the last rites were read over him. He lived.

His assistant, Jimmy Murphy, held the club together.

Then came the part that surprised me.

On 19 February United played Sheffield Wednesday in the FA Cup. Of the side in Belgrade, only Gregg and Bill Foulkes took the field.

In front of 59,848, Shay Brennan, a youth-team full-back, scored twice. United won 3–0.

It took Busby ten years to reach a European Cup final.

In the 1968 semi-final at Real Madrid, Foulkes, aged 36, wandered upfield. George Best cut the ball back. Foulkes struck it into the corner.

In the Wembley final against Benfica, Charlton headed United in front.

In extra time Best, Brian Kidd on his 19th birthday and Charlton again made it 4–1.

Two men from the plane were on the pitch. A third, Busby, was their manager.

Here's what stays with me.

The ambition that put United on that runway also gave them something to set against the loss. A trophy does not redeem a disaster.

But a blank team sheet still gets filled, by people who decide to keep going.
