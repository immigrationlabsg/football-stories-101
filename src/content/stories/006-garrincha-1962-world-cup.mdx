On 2 June 1962, in Brazil's second match of the World Cup, Pelé felt something tear at the top of his leg.

His tournament was over.

The man who replaced him as Brazil's decisive player was a winger with a crooked spine and bent legs of unequal length.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

FIFA's own history of him says doctors told his mother he would never walk.

He was born Manuel Francisco dos Santos. His sister called him Garrincha, after a small brown wren.

His right leg bent inwards and his left outwards.

At his trial for Botafogo in 1953 he beat Nílton Santos, already a Brazil international, so easily that Santos urged the club to sign him.

In Chile, without Pelé, Garrincha took over.

In the quarter-final against England he scored twice. Brazil won 3–1.

In the semi-final against the hosts, Garrincha scored twice more. Brazil won 4–2.

But Chile had fouled him repeatedly, and late on he kicked out.

He was sent off.

Then came the part that surprised me.

In 1962 a red card did not mean an automatic ban. A FIFA committee decided each case.

The only official who had seen the kick, a Uruguayan linesman, did not appear before it. By the accounts that survive, he had already left Chile.

Without his evidence, Garrincha was not suspended.

The man sent off in a World Cup semi-final was free to play in the final.

He played it with a high fever, dosed with aspirin. Brazil won 3–1 anyway.

Garrincha finished joint top scorer with four goals.

The rest of his life was hard and short. He drank heavily, and in 1983 he died of cirrhosis, aged 49.

His gravestone calls him the Joy of the People.

Here's what stays with me.

Football likes its geniuses to look like athletes, and Garrincha never did.

His greatest tournament happened only because the best player in the world was hurt.

And it was nearly cut short by a committee and an absent linesman.
