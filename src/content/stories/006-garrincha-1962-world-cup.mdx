On 2 June 1962, in Brazil's second match of the World Cup, Pelé tried a shot from distance and felt something tear at the top of his leg.

His tournament was over.

Brazil were the world champions, and they had just lost their best player.

The man who replaced him as Brazil's decisive player was a winger, aged 28, with a crooked spine and bent legs of unequal length.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

FIFA's own history of him says doctors told his mother he would never walk.

He was born Manuel Francisco dos Santos in Pau Grande, a mill village in the hills of Rio de Janeiro state.

His sister Rosa called him Garrincha, after a small brown wren, because he was small for his age.

By the time he was four, nobody called him anything else.

His right leg bent inwards and his left outwards. One was about six centimetres longer than the other, though accounts disagree on which.

At his trial for Botafogo in 1953 he was set against Nílton Santos, already a Brazil international.

Garrincha beat him so easily that Santos urged the club to sign him rather than ever face him in a match.

On his debut, with Botafogo 2–0 down, he scored a hat-trick.

Pelé later wrote that the two of them played 40 matches together for Brazil and never lost one.

And in Chile, without Pelé, Garrincha took over.

In the quarter-final against England on 10 June, he headed Brazil in front from a corner.

Early in the second half he struck a free-kick England could not hold, and Vavá scored the rebound.

Then he bent one in from around 30 yards. Brazil won 3–1.

Then came the part that surprised me.

That match is just as famous for a dog.

A stray ran onto the pitch and evaded several players, Garrincha among them, until Jimmy Greaves of England went after it.

As Greaves recalled: "I got down on my hands and knees, being a dog lover anyway, and called it over."

The dog relieved itself down the front of his shirt. There was no change of kit, so Greaves played the rest of the match in it.

The dog went home with Brazil. In one version Garrincha adopted it. In another, the squad raffled it and he won it.

Brazilian reports say it was named Bi, for the second world title.

Three days later Brazil played the hosts in the semi-final in Santiago.

Garrincha scored after nine minutes with his weaker left foot, then headed a second. Brazil won 4–2.

But Chile had fouled him repeatedly, and late on he kicked out at the midfielder Eladio Rojas.

He was sent off.

The next morning Santiago's El Mercurio asked in its headline what planet he had come from.

In 1962 a red card did not mean an automatic ban. A FIFA committee decided each case.

The only official who had seen the kick, the Uruguayan linesman Esteban Marino, did not appear before it. By the accounts that survive, he had already left Chile.

Without his evidence, Garrincha was not suspended. Sources differ on whether he was cleared or warned.

The man sent off in a World Cup semi-final was free to play in the final.

He played it with a high fever, dosed with aspirin, and FIFA's account calls him ill and off form.

Brazil won 3–1 anyway.

Garrincha finished joint top scorer with four goals, and FIFA's records name him player of the tournament.

The rest of his life was hard and short. He drank heavily, and on 20 January 1983 he died of cirrhosis, aged 49.

His gravestone, near the village where he grew up, calls him the Joy of the People.

Here's what stays with me.

Football likes its geniuses to look like athletes, and Garrincha never did.

His greatest tournament happened only because the best player in the world was hurt.

And it was nearly cut short by a committee and an absent linesman.
