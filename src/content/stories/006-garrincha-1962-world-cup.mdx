On 2 June 1962, at the Estadio Sausalito in Viña del Mar, Pelé tried a shot from distance in Brazil's second group match against Czechoslovakia and felt something tear at the top of his leg. The match finished 0–0. The injury, described in different accounts as a torn thigh muscle and a strained groin, ended his tournament.

Brazil were the world champions, and they had just lost the player who, as a teenager, had helped win them the trophy in Sweden four years earlier. What happened next is the strange part. The man who replaced him as Brazil's decisive player was not his deputy, Amarildo, though Amarildo did well. It was a winger, aged 28, with a crooked spine and bent legs of unequal length, who had been told as a child that he might never walk normally.

## The little bird

He was born Manuel Francisco dos Santos on 28 October 1933 in Pau Grande, a mill village in the hills of Rio de Janeiro state. His sister Rosa called him Garrincha, the name of a small brown wren, because he was small for his age, and by the time he was four nobody called him anything else.

His body was a puzzle. His spine was curved, his right leg bent inwards and his left outwards, and one leg was about six centimetres longer than the other; accounts disagree on which. FIFA's own history of him says doctors told his mother he would never walk. He grew up playing in the village instead, and was close to 20 when he went for a trial at Botafogo in 1953.

At the trial he was set against Nílton Santos, already a Brazil international and one of the best full-backs in the country. Garrincha beat him so easily that Santos urged the club to sign him rather than ever face him in a match. Botafogo did, despite their doubts about his body. On his debut, against Bonsucesso, Botafogo were 2–0 down after half an hour and Garrincha scored a hat-trick.

He reached the World Cup in 1958, but only in the third group match, against the Soviet Union in Gothenburg. Pelé, then a teenager, came into the side on the same day. Garrincha hit the woodwork in the opening minutes and Vavá scored soon after; Brazil won 2–0 and went on to win the tournament. On 20 January 2018, the 35th anniversary of Garrincha's death, Pelé wrote that the two of them had played 40 matches together for Brazil and never lost one.

## Chile without Pelé

In 1962 the coach, Aymoré Moreira, put Amarildo in Pelé's place, and against Spain on 6 June it was Amarildo who scored twice in the last 20 minutes to turn a 1–0 deficit into a 2–1 win. Brazil were through. From the quarter-final onwards, the tournament belonged to the man on the right wing.

England met Brazil in Viña del Mar on 10 June, and knew exactly whom they had to stop. After 31 minutes Garrincha rose at a corner and headed Brazil in front. Gerry Hitchens equalised before half-time. Early in the second half Garrincha struck a free-kick that England could not hold, and Vavá scored from the rebound. Then Garrincha bent a shot in from around 30 yards, and Brazil won 3–1.

The match is also remembered for a stray dog that ran onto the pitch and evaded several players, Garrincha among them, until Jimmy Greaves of England went after it.

> I got down on my hands and knees, being a dog lover anyway, and called it over.
> — Jimmy Greaves, recalling the 1962 quarter-final, as quoted by FIFA

The dog relieved itself down the front of his shirt. Teams had no change of kit, so Greaves played the rest of the match in it. The dog went home with Brazil: in one version Garrincha adopted it, in another the squad raffled it and he won it. Brazilian reports say it was named Bi, for the bicampeonato, the second world title. Greaves told the story for the rest of his life, and liked to say that in Brazil he was known as Garrincha's dog catcher.

Three days later, on 13 June, Brazil played the hosts in the semi-final at the Estadio Nacional in Santiago, in front of more than 76,000 people. Garrincha opened the scoring after nine minutes with a shot from his weaker left foot, then headed a second. Vavá scored twice after half-time and Brazil won 4–2. But Chile had fouled Garrincha repeatedly, and late in the match he retaliated, kicking out at the midfielder Eladio Rojas. He was sent off.

The next morning Santiago's El Mercurio, writing about the man who had just knocked Chile out of their own World Cup, asked in its headline where he had come from.

> Garrincha, ¿de qué planeta viniste?
> — Headline, El Mercurio, Santiago, the morning after the semi-final, as reproduced by FIFA ("Garrincha, what planet are you from?")

## The turn

In 1962 a red card did not carry an automatic suspension. A FIFA disciplinary committee decided each case, and it met before the final to decide whether Brazil's best player would miss it.

The official who had seen the kick was the Uruguayan linesman, Esteban Marino. He did not appear before the committee; by the accounts that have come down, he had already left Chile. Without his evidence the committee did not suspend Garrincha. Sources differ on whether he was let off entirely or given a warning, and claims about how Brazil arranged the outcome have been made over the years without being established. What is certain is the result: the player who had just been sent off in a World Cup semi-final was free to play in the final.

That decision is the hinge of the tournament. Brazil had already lost one great player in Chile. A suspension would have taken the second, and with him the player who had scored four of Brazil's seven goals in the two knockout matches.

As it turned out, Garrincha was not the hero of the final. He went into it on 17 June in Santiago running a high fever, dosed with aspirin, and FIFA's account of the match describes him as ill and off form. Czechoslovakia took the lead after about 15 minutes through Josef Masopust, and Amarildo equalised two minutes later. In the second half Zito headed Brazil in front after the Czechoslovak goalkeeper came for a cross and missed it, and Vavá made it 3–1 after another goalkeeping error. Brazil had won the World Cup for the second time in a row, before a crowd of about 69,000.

Garrincha finished with four goals, level with five others at the top of the scoring list, and FIFA's records name him the player of the tournament. The team had won without Pelé, and nobody in Chile was in any doubt about who had carried it.

## After Chile

He never reached that level again. He played in the 1966 World Cup in England, and on 15 July at Goodison Park, without Pelé, Brazil lost 3–1 to Hungary. It was the only match Brazil ever lost with Garrincha on the pitch.

The rest of his life was hard and short. He drank heavily. In April 1969 he was driving on the highway between Rio and São Paulo when the car collided with a lorry, and the mother of his wife, the samba singer Elza Soares, was killed. On 20 January 1983 he died of cirrhosis of the liver, aged 49. His coffin was taken from the Maracanã back to Pau Grande, with thousands lining the route. His gravestone, in a small cemetery near the village, describes him as the Joy of the People.

## What it tells us

Football likes its geniuses to look like athletes, and Garrincha never did. His best tournament came only because the greatest player in the world was injured, and it was nearly cut short by a hearing in a committee room. A World Cup can turn on an absent linesman as easily as on a dribble, and in 1962 it turned on both.
