One of the most famous shirts in Argentina wears the colours of Sweden.

Blue and gold. Boca Juniors have worn them since 1907, and the club has a story to explain why.

It is a wonderful story. It may even be true.

Boca was founded on 3 April 1905, at the house of Esteban Baglietto in La Boca, the dockside district of Buenos Aires.

The founders were teenagers, the sons of Italian immigrants, most of them from Genoa.

The district gave the club the first half of its name. The second half, Juniors, was English, because in Buenos Aires at the time English was the language of football and of prestige.

The colours took longer.

The first shirts were white with thin black stripes, sewn by Manuela Farenga, from one of the founding families. The boys avoided red because it belonged to Alumni.

Other designs followed, including sky blue. None lasted.

Here is the story as it is usually told.

In 1906 Boca found themselves wearing shirts very like those of another young club, Nottingham de Almagro. They agreed to settle it on the pitch. The winner would keep the colours.

Boca lost.

Nobody could agree on new colours. Then Juan Rafael Brichetto, who in the club's account worked at the port, had an idea worthy of a neighbourhood that lived by the water.

Boca would take the colours of the flag on the next ship to sail into the harbour.

The next ship was Swedish. Blue, with a yellow cross.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Almost every part of that story comes in more than one version.

The opponent is usually Nottingham de Almagro, but some tellings make it Racing Club. The match is dated 1906 or 1907. Brichetto is a port worker in some accounts, the club's president in others.

Even the ship has two names. Flag historians, drawing on research by the Argentine vexillologist Luis Havas, call it the Drottning Sophia. Other accounts name the Princessan Ingeborg.

Then came the part that surprised me.

The Mexican sports site Mediotiempo, looking into the legend, reported that it began to gain ground only in 1934.

Before then, and above all in 1907 itself, it appears in no newspaper, no club minutes, no book and no magazine.

No record from the time says a ship had anything to do with it.

What is documented is shorter. The club's centenary book dates the decision to an assembly on 5 April 1907, at which Brichetto proposed blue and gold.

The first shirts had a yellow sash running diagonally across the chest. The horizontal band came in 1912 or 1913.

Some historians point out that Swedish ships are recorded entering Buenos Aires in the first months of 1907. That makes the story possible, even plausible.

It does not make it true.

So the honest version runs like this. In 1907 Boca adopted the colours of the Swedish flag. Nearly everyone says a ship was the reason. Nobody has produced a document from 1907 that says so.

And yet the story fits the club almost too well.

A club founded by the children of immigrants, in a port where almost everything had arrived by sea, says its colours arrived by sea too. By chance. From a country none of the founders came from.

The colours travelled. In 1925 Boca became the first Argentine club to tour Europe, playing 19 matches in Spain, Germany and France and winning 15.

And the Swedish connection has never gone away. On 3 April 2024, the club's 119th birthday, Boca released a navy third shirt marked with yellow crosses.

More than a century after a ship may or may not have sailed past La Boca, the Swedish flag finally ended up on the shirt in a form anyone could recognise.

Here's what stays with me.

Origin stories are rarely written down when they happen. They are told later, by people who already know how things turned out. Boca's ship may never be proved, but it says something true about the club: it was made by people who came from somewhere else, and it has never minded saying so.
