One of the most famous shirts in Argentina wears the colours of Sweden.

Blue and gold. Boca Juniors have worn them since 1907.

A ship sailed into the harbour at La Boca, the story goes, and the club took the colours of its flag.

It is a wonderful story. It may even be true.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

No record from 1907 says a ship had anything to do with it.

Boca was founded on 3 April 1905 in La Boca, the dockside district of Buenos Aires. The founders were teenage sons of Italian immigrants.

Here is the story as it is usually told.

In 1906 Boca found themselves in shirts very like those of Nottingham de Almagro. They settled it on the pitch. The winner kept the colours.

Boca lost.

Then Juan Rafael Brichetto had an idea. Boca would take the colours of the flag on the next ship into the harbour.

The next ship was Swedish. Blue, with a yellow cross.

Now the versions. Some tellings make the opponent Racing Club. Even the ship has two names: the Drottning Sophia or the Princessan Ingeborg.

Then came the part that surprised me.

The Mexican site Mediotiempo reported that the legend began to gain ground only in 1934.

In 1907 itself, it appears in no newspaper, no club minutes, no book and no magazine.

The club's centenary book dates the decision to an assembly on 5 April 1907, where Brichetto proposed blue and gold.

Some historians note Swedish ships entering Buenos Aires in early 1907. That makes the story plausible.

It does not make it true.

And yet it fits the club almost too well. A club founded by the children of immigrants, in a port where almost everything arrived by sea, says its colours arrived by sea too.

Here's what stays with me.

Origin stories are rarely written down when they happen. They are told later, by people who know how things turned out.

Boca's ship may never be proved. But it says something true about a club made by people from somewhere else.
