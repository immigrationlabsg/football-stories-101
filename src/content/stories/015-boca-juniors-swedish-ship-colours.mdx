On 3 April 1905 a group of teenagers met at the house of Esteban Baglietto in La Boca, the dockside district of Buenos Aires where the Riachuelo river meets the River Plate. They were the sons of Italian immigrants, most of them from Genoa, and they wanted a football club. So many people turned up that the meeting moved outside, to Plaza Solís, and it was there, on a square in a neighbourhood of port workers and tenement houses, that they settled on a name. The district gave them the first half. The second half, Juniors, was English, because in Buenos Aires at the turn of the century English was the language of football and of prestige.

Among those usually named as founders are Baglietto, Alfredo Scarpatti, Santiago Sana and two brothers, Juan and Teodoro Farenga. Lists differ a little from account to account, which is a fair warning for what follows. The club they started would become one of the most famous in the world, and its blue shirt with a gold band one of the most recognisable in football. How the club came to wear it is the best-known story Boca tell about themselves. It is also, in its most famous details, a story nobody wrote down at the time.

## The shirts before the shirt

Boca did not start in blue and gold. The club’s own history says the first shirts were white with thin black stripes, sewn by Manuela Farenga, from one of the founding families, and that the boys avoided red because it belonged to Alumni. Over the next two years they tried other designs, including a sky-blue shirt and one with thin blue stripes. A further claim, that the very first Boca shirt was pink, is repeated in some histories and doubted by journalists and historians; there is no solid evidence for it.

None of these early kits lasted, and the reason, in the most often repeated version, was a clash.

## The ship

This is the story as it is usually told. In 1906 Boca found themselves wearing shirts very like those of another young club, Nottingham de Almagro. The two clubs agreed to settle the matter on the pitch: the winner would keep the colours, and the loser would have to change. Boca lost.

The club needed new colours, and nobody could agree on any. Then Juan Rafael Brichetto, who in the club’s own account worked at the port, came up with a solution worthy of a neighbourhood that lived by the water: Boca would take the colours of the flag of the next ship to sail into the harbour. The next ship was Swedish. Its flag was blue with a yellow cross, and Boca have worn blue and gold ever since.

It is a wonderful story, and almost every part of it comes in more than one version. The opponent in the decisive match is usually Nottingham de Almagro, but some tellings make it Racing Club. The year of the match is given as 1906 or 1907. Brichetto is described in some accounts as a port worker, in others as the club’s president, and in some as both. The ship has been given at least two names. Flag historians, drawing on research by the Argentine vexillologist Luis Havas, identify it as the Drottning Sophia, a freighter said to have been sailing from Copenhagen and to have passed through the port on 23 March 1907. Other accounts name a different Swedish vessel, the Princessan Ingeborg. A Swedish maritime museum holds a record for a Drottning Sophia, but it describes a coastal steamer built in Stockholm in 1875 and says nothing about South America, and it is not clear that it is the same ship.

## What is documented

Strip away the details that cannot be confirmed and what remains is still a good story, only a shorter one.

The year is solid. Boca’s colours were not chosen when the club was founded in 1905; they were adopted in 1907. The club’s centenary book, published in 2004, dates the decision to an assembly on 5 April 1907, at which Brichetto proposed blue and gold. The first blue-and-gold shirts had a yellow sash running diagonally across the chest. The horizontal band that the world knows came a few years later; accounts put the change in 1912 or 1913.

The ship is another matter. The Mexican sports site Mediotiempo, looking into the legend, reported that it began to gain ground only in 1934. Before then, and above all in 1907 itself, it appears in no newspaper, no club minutes, no book and no magazine. There is, in other words, no record from the time of the decision that a ship had anything to do with it.

On the other side of the argument, some historians point out that Swedish ships are recorded entering the port of Buenos Aires in the first months of 1907. That makes the story possible, even plausible: a Swedish flag on the Riachuelo would not have been a strange sight. It does not make it true. A Swedish ship in port is evidence that Swedish ships came to Buenos Aires, not evidence that one of them chose a football club’s colours.

So the honest version runs like this. In 1907, two years after its founding, Boca Juniors adopted blue and gold, proposed by Brichetto. Those are the colours of the Swedish flag. The club’s own history, and nearly everyone who tells it, says that is not a coincidence, and that a ship was the reason. No one has produced a document from 1907 that says so, and the name of the ship depends on whom you ask.

## The turn

The decision that mattered was not the ship but the club’s willingness to let the story stand. Whatever Brichetto had in mind in 1907, by the time Boca were famous the ship had become part of how the club explained itself, and it fitted perfectly. A club founded by the children of immigrants, in a port district where almost everything and everyone had arrived by sea, says its colours arrived by sea too, by chance, from a country none of the founders came from. Boca had borrowed its name from the English, as plenty of Buenos Aires clubs did. Its colours, the story says, came from whatever sailed up the river.

The colours did not stay local for long. In 1925 Boca became the first Argentine club to tour Europe, playing 19 matches in Spain, Germany and France between March and June and winning 15 of them, with one draw and three defeats. The domestic league that year could not be completed with Boca away, and the Argentine association declared them Campeón de Honor, champions of honour. On 25 May 1940 the club opened the stadium the world knows as La Bombonera, the chocolate box, a nickname said to come from its architect, Viktor Sulcic, who saw the shape of his design in a box of chocolates. The fans, meanwhile, took a name from the founders rather than the ship: Xeneizes, the Genoese, from the word for Genoese in the dialect of Liguria.

The Swedish connection has never gone away. On 3 April 2024, the club’s 119th birthday, Boca released a navy third shirt marked with yellow crosses in a nod to the Swedish flag. The club’s main sponsor, since 2023, has been Betsson, a betting company of Swedish origin. More than a century after a ship may or may not have sailed past La Boca, the Swedish flag finally ended up on the shirt in a form anyone could recognise.

## What it tells us

Club origin stories are rarely recorded when they happen; they are written down later, by people who already know how things turned out. Boca’s colours are documented, and the reason for them is a legend that may well rest on something real but cannot be shown to. The story survives not because it has been proved but because it explains something true about the club: it was made by people who came from somewhere else, and it has never minded saying so.
