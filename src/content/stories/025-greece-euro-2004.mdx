Before the summer of 2004, Greece had played six matches at major finals.

They had won none of them.

They had scored one goal.

At the 1994 World Cup in the United States they lost all three games, conceded ten and scored none. Ten years later they were champions of Europe.

Nobody even agrees on how unlikely that was. UEFA put them at 80 to 1, Planet Football at 150 to 1, The Irish Times at 250 to 1.

Here’s what I didn’t know until recently.

The man who changed that was a German. And he did not start with tactics. He started with rules.

Otto Rehhagel had spent his whole career in the Bundesliga, and he had a habit of improbable titles. In 1997–98 he took Kaiserslautern, newly promoted, to the German championship, two points ahead of Bayern Munich.

In Greece, Angelos Charisteas later described a long effort to make players who thought of their clubs first think of the team. As Rehhagel put it before the semi-final: “The Greek players were very talented but they had to understand that a team group has rules.”

It did not work at once. His first match in charge was a 5–1 defeat in Finland.

Qualifying for Euro 2004 began with two more defeats, 2–0 at home to Spain and 2–0 in Ukraine.

Then Greece won six in a row without conceding a goal, including 1–0 in Zaragoza, and finished above Spain.

Even so, Planet Football’s verdict was simple: Greece were not expected to get out of a group that contained the hosts.

The tournament opened in Porto on 12 June, against those hosts. Portugal had Luís Figo and a 19-year-old called Cristiano Ronaldo.

Giorgos Karagounis scored after seven minutes, the first goal of Euro 2004. Angelos Basinas added a penalty after Ronaldo brought down a Greek defender. Greece won 2–1.

They needed every bit of that result. A 1–1 draw with Spain and a 2–1 defeat by Russia left them level on points with Spain. Greece went through on goals scored, four to two.

Then came the part that surprised me.

All three knockout matches were won 1–0. All three goals were headers. Two came from corners.

Against France, the holders, the captain Theodoros Zagorakis crossed and Charisteas headed in.

Against the Czech Republic in Porto, Greece kept them out for 90 minutes and then for the first period of extra time. In its last moments Traianos Dellas, a centre-back, met a corner at the near post. Under the silver-goal rule then in force, the match ended there.

That sent Greece back to Lisbon, and back to Portugal.

In the 57th minute of the final on 4 July, Basinas swung in a corner and Charisteas rose above Costinha to head it in. According to UEFA’s report, it was Greece’s only effort on target all night.

It was enough.

Opponents with far more famous players knew exactly what was coming. They still could not stop it.

Greece scored seven goals in Portugal and conceded four, every one of them in the group stage. Zagorakis was named player of the tournament. Rehhagel, UEFA records, is the only coach to win the European Championship with a country other than his own.

And Portugal had been beaten twice by the same side, in the first match of their own tournament and the last.

Here’s what stays with me.

Greece were not lucky once. They were organised six times. A team of modest reputations agreed to do the same unglamorous things for every minute, and it beat the hosts twice and the holders once. The odds depend on who you ask; the result never does.
