In the 57th minute of the European Championship final at the Estádio da Luz in Lisbon, on 4 July 2004, the Greek right-back Giourkas Seitaridis ran down the wing and won a corner. Angelos Basinas swung it in. Angelos Charisteas, a striker from Werder Bremen, rose above the Portugal midfielder Costinha and headed it in from close range. According to UEFA’s report it was Greece’s only effort on target all night. It was enough. For the remaining half-hour and more Portugal came forward, and the defensive discipline that had carried Greece through the tournament held, as it had through every knockout round. The German referee, Markus Merk, blew for full time, and around 15,000 Greeks among a sold-out crowd of 62,865 began celebrating the first major trophy their country had ever won.

How unlikely that was depends on whose price you believe. Sources disagree on the pre-tournament odds: UEFA and Al Jazeera’s 2004 report put Greece at 80 to 1, Planet Football at 150 to 1, and The Irish Times at 250 to 1. What nobody disputes is the record they brought with them. Greece had reached two major finals before, the European Championship of 1980 and the World Cup of 1994. In six matches they had drawn one, lost five and scored a single goal. In the United States in 1994 they lost all three games, conceded ten and scored none. Planet Football’s retrospective puts it simply: before the tournament, Greece were not even expected to get out of a group that contained the hosts.

## A German in Athens

Otto Rehhagel took charge in August 2001. He was a German who had spent his whole career in the Bundesliga, where UEFA notes he was known as the child of the Bundesliga for his 1,000 top-flight games as a player and coach, and he had a record of improbable titles. In 1997–98 he had taken Kaiserslautern, newly promoted after relegation in 1996, to the German championship, two points ahead of Bayern Munich, whom he had coached before moving to Kaiserslautern. ESPN calls it the only time a promoted team has won the Bundesliga.

None of that helped at first. His first match in charge of Greece was a 5–1 defeat in Finland. Charisteas, recalling it years later for FourFourTwo, described what came next as a long effort to make a group of players who thought of their clubs first think of the team.

Rehhagel himself put it more bluntly, speaking before the semi-final in Portugal.

> The Greek players were very talented but they had to understand that a team group has rules. Once I got them to accept this, then they started achieving.
> — Otto Rehhagel, quoted by Paddy Agnew in The Irish Times, 1 July 2004

Qualifying for Euro 2004 began badly. Greece lost 2–0 at home to Spain and 2–0 away to Ukraine. Then they won their next six matches without conceding a goal, including 1–0 against Spain in Zaragoza, with Giannakopoulos scoring. They finished top of the group and sent Spain into the play-offs. For a team with no history at major tournaments, it was a hint of what was coming: they did not need many goals if the other side scored none.

## Porto, Faro, Lisbon

The tournament opened on 12 June at the Estádio do Dragão in Porto, with the hosts. Portugal had Luís Figo and a young winger, Cristiano Ronaldo, then 19. Greece scored after seven minutes, through Giorgos Karagounis, the first goal of Euro 2004. Six minutes into the second half Ronaldo, tracking back, brought down a Greek defender, the referee Pierluigi Collina gave a penalty, and Basinas converted it. Ronaldo pulled one back in stoppage time. Greece won 2–1.

That one result changed the arithmetic of the group, and Greece needed all of it. They drew 1–1 with Spain, Charisteas scoring, and then lost 2–1 to Russia in Faro, where they were 2–0 down after 17 minutes. Zisis Vryzas’s goal just before half-time looked like a consolation. It was not. In the other match Portugal beat Spain 1–0, and Greece and Spain finished level on four points. Greece went through on goals scored, four to Spain’s two, and Vryzas’s goal was part of the difference.

In the quarter-final in Lisbon on 25 June, they met France, the holders. In the 65th minute Theodoros Zagorakis, the captain, crossed and Charisteas headed in. France could not find a way back. Greece won 1–0.

## The turn

The decision that explains Greece’s summer is the one Rehhagel described in the words The Irish Times reported: before he worried about tactics, he insisted that his players accept the team’s rules. Every other piece followed from that. Greece were not built to dominate games. They were built so that no one in the side would stop doing his job, and so that a single goal, often from a set piece, would usually do. In six matches in Portugal they scored seven goals and conceded four, all of those in the group stage. The three knockout matches were all won 1–0, and all three goals were headers: one from a captain’s cross, two from corners. Opponents with more famous players knew exactly what was coming and still could not stop it, because stopping it meant beating a whole team that had agreed to do the same unglamorous things for 90 minutes and more.

The semi-final against the Czech Republic in Porto on 1 July showed the method at its most extreme. Greece kept them out for 90 minutes and then for the first period of extra time. In its last moments Vassilios Tsiartas took a corner and the centre-back Traianos Dellas got to it at the near post and headed it in. Dellas, a tall central defender who played in every match, was the heart of the defence that had let in so little. Under the silver-goal rule then in force, a team that led at the end of the first period of extra time had won, and so the match ended there. It finished 1–0.

That brought Greece back to Lisbon and to Portugal, the team they had beaten in the opening match. UEFA later noted that the wheel had come full circle: three weeks after winning 2–1 in Porto, Rehhagel’s outsiders won the final 1–0. Charisteas, who had scored against Spain and France, had now scored three times in the tournament, all of them goals that earned points or settled ties. Zagorakis, whose cross had beaten France, was named man of the match in the final and player of the tournament, the choice of UEFA’s technical study group.

For Portugal it was a different kind of history. They were the first hosts to reach the final since France in 1984, and UEFA notes that until France lost at home in 2016 they were the only host nation to lose one. The team that beat them had arrived in the country as the side most people expected to go home first.

> It is an unusual achievement for Greek football and especially for European football.
> — Otto Rehhagel after the final, as reported by UEFA.com, 4 July 2004

Rehhagel added that Portugal had been technically better and that Greece had taken their chances. It was a fair summary of a whole month. UEFA records him as the only coach to win the European Championship with a country other than his own.

## What it tells us

Greece were not lucky once; they were organised six times. Their summer is the clearest case in modern football that a team with no tradition and modest individual reputations can beat better sides if every player accepts the same rules. The odds differ depending on who you ask, but the result never does: the hosts were beaten twice, and the holders once, by a side that had never won a match at a finals.
