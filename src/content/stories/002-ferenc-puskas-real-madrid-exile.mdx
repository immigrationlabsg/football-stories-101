On 20 December 1956, in the Heysel Stadium in Brussels, Ferenc Puskás led Budapest Honvéd out for a European Cup tie that no longer felt like football. It was meant to be the home leg of a first-round tie against Athletic Bilbao, but Budapest was out of the question. Soviet tanks had crushed the Hungarian uprising the previous month, and the Honvéd players, who had been on tour in western Europe preparing for the first leg when the fighting began, did not know whether their families were safe. They had lost 3–2 in Bilbao. In Brussels they drew 3–3 and went out 6–5 on aggregate. Nobody in the dressing room thought the score was the important result of the night.

Then came the decision that defined the rest of Puskás's life. Instead of going home, the players sent for their families and set off on a fundraising tour of Italy, Portugal, Spain and Brazil, against the wishes of FIFA and the Hungarian football authorities. When the tour ended, most of the squad went back. Puskás did not.

He was 29, the captain of the best national team in the world, and he had just made himself an exile.

## The Galloping Major

To understand what Puskás gave up, start with what he had built. He made his debut for Kispest, his neighbourhood club on the edge of Budapest, as a teenager in 1943. When the Hungarian army took the club over in 1949 and renamed it Honvéd, the players were given nominal officer ranks, and that military status produced the nickname that followed him everywhere: the Galloping Major. He was short and thick-set, with a left foot that did almost everything.

Hungary won Olympic gold in Helsinki in 1952, and on 25 November 1953 they went to Wembley and beat England 6–3. No team from outside the British Isles had ever won there, and England had never lost at home to a side from continental Europe. Puskás scored twice. The first of his goals, Hungary's third, is still the one most people picture when they hear his name. Close to goal, with the England captain Billy Wright sliding in to block, he dragged the ball back with the sole of his foot, Wright went past him and on to the ground, and Puskás finished with his left foot.

Geoffrey Green's report in The Times compared Wright's lunge to a fire engine racing to the wrong fire. The image stuck, although the wording has drifted with every retelling, and no two later accounts quote it the same way.

In the return match in Budapest in May 1954 Hungary won 7–1, still England's heaviest defeat. Bobby Robson, a 20-year-old professional who had watched the Wembley game from the crowd, later described what he had seen in terms that many of his generation shared.

> We saw a style of play, a system of play that we had never seen before ... they were men from Mars as far as we were concerned.
> — Bobby Robson, interviewed on BBC One's Football Focus

Then came the 1954 World Cup in Switzerland, and the afternoon that still hurts in Hungary. In the 8–3 group win over West Germany, a heavy challenge from the centre-half Werner Liebrich injured Puskás's ankle. He limped off and missed the 4–2 wins over Brazil and Uruguay in the quarter-final and semi-final. He insisted on playing the final in Bern on 4 July 1954, short of fitness, and for eight minutes it looked like the right call: he scored after six minutes, Zoltán Czibor added a second and Hungary led 2–0. West Germany came back to win 3–2, with Helmut Rahn's winner six minutes from time. In the last minutes Puskás put the ball in the net for what would have been the equaliser, and it was ruled out for offside. Puskás always blamed the Welsh linesman, Mervyn Griffiths, although some accounts credit the decision to the English referee, Bill Ling. It was the only match Hungary lost between 1950 and 1956. Two years later, Puskás was gone.

## Thirty-one, heavy and unwanted

FIFA responded to the players who stayed abroad with a ban. For Puskás it meant no competitive football until 1958. He drifted, putting on weight and fielding interest from clubs that could not get him registered. By the summer of 1958 he was 31, by one account more than 18 kilos over his playing weight, and in the view of most of European football, finished.

The man who disagreed was Emil Östreicher, who had worked with him at Honvéd and was now at Real Madrid. Madrid already had Alfredo Di Stéfano and Francisco Gento, and had won the first three European Cups. They did not need an overweight Hungarian who had not played a competitive match since 1956. They signed him anyway, and used their influence to get the suspension lifted. He made his debut on 14 September 1958.

What happened next is the reason the story is told. Puskás trained the weight off, settled in beside Di Stéfano rather than competing with him, and started scoring at a rate that made the previous two years look like a rumour. He was La Liga's top scorer, the Pichichi, four times in five seasons from 1959–60 to 1963–64, and Real Madrid won the league five years running from 1961 to 1965. By the usual count he scored 156 goals in 180 league matches (at least one Spanish obituary gave 154), and 242 in 262 official games in all competitions by the club's own figures. He had arrived at an age when most forwards of his era were thinking about stopping.

He did not beat defenders with pace. He beat them with a first touch that put the ball exactly where his left foot wanted it, and then he hit it before anyone had worked out that he was going to shoot.

## Hampden Park, 18 May 1960

Real Madrid had won the 1959 European Cup final against Reims without him. A year later, in front of more than 127,000 people at Hampden Park in Glasgow, he played the final that fixed his place in the game's memory. The opponents were Eintracht Frankfurt, who had swept Rangers aside in the semi-final, and Richard Kress gave them the lead after 18 minutes. Di Stéfano scored twice before half-time, and then Puskás took over: a goal on the stroke of half-time, a disputed penalty, and two more in the next quarter of an hour, the last of them a shot on the turn, high into the net, that made it 6–1. Di Stéfano completed his own hat-trick, and Real Madrid won 7–3, their fifth European Cup in a row. No player has scored more than four goals in a European Cup or Champions League final.

Puskás played two more finals. In Amsterdam in 1962 he scored a hat-trick and still finished on the losing side, 5–3 to a Benfica team inspired by a young Eusébio. In Vienna in 1964, with Di Stéfano still beside him, Madrid lost 3–1 to Helenio Herrera's Inter. He remains the only player to score a hat-trick in two European Cup finals.

There was one more twist. Having been told he would never play for Hungary again, he took Spanish citizenship in 1961 and won four caps for Spain: a World Cup qualifier against Morocco and three matches at the 1962 World Cup in Chile. He did not score. His international goals stayed where they were: 84 in 85 matches for Hungary, by the count most sources now use, after the Hungarian federation recognised two further matches as official in 2002.

## The turn

The decision that explains the outcome was taken in the winter of 1956, and it was not really a decision about football. Puskás chose not to go back to a country whose uprising had just been crushed, knowing it meant a ban, the loss of his place in the national team and, as far as anyone could tell, the end of his career. What made the difference afterwards was less the talent, which had never been in doubt, than a stubbornness about his own body. At 31, and heavy, he was the one who got fit again.

He did go home eventually. He coached around the world after retiring in 1966, most memorably taking Panathinaikos of Athens to the 1971 European Cup final at Wembley, where they lost 2–0 to the Ajax of Johan Cruyff. He settled back in Budapest, and in 1993 he took temporary charge of the national team for four matches. In 2002 the Népstadion in Budapest was renamed in his honour. When he died on 17 November 2006, aged 79, the government declared the day of his funeral, 9 December, a day of national mourning; after a ceremony at the stadium, the Mass was said in St Stephen's Basilica, where he was laid to rest. The country that had cast him out buried him like a king.

## What it tells us

Age and condition are facts, but they are not verdicts. Puskás was written off at 31 by people looking at a photograph rather than at a player, and the club that ignored the photograph won five league titles with him. It is also a reminder of how much of football history is really political history. The greatest Hungarian team there has ever been was broken up not by opponents but by tanks, and the goals Puskás scored in white were, in a sense, goals that Hungary lost.
