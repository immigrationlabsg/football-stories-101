On 20 December 1956, in a stadium in Brussels, Ferenc Puskás led Budapest Honvéd out for a European Cup tie that no longer felt like football.

It was meant to be their home leg against Athletic Bilbao. Home was out of the question.

Soviet tanks had crushed the Hungarian uprising the month before. The players had been on tour in western Europe when the fighting began, and they did not know whether their families were safe.

They drew 3–3 and went out 6–5 on aggregate.

Then they did something extraordinary. Instead of going home, they sent for their families and set off on a fundraising tour of Italy, Portugal, Spain and Brazil, against the wishes of FIFA and their own federation.

When the tour ended, most of the squad went back.

Puskás did not.

He was 29, captain of the best national team in the world, and he had just made himself an exile.

The army had taken over his club in 1949, renamed it Honvéd and given the players nominal officer ranks. That is where the nickname came from: the Galloping Major.

On 25 November 1953 Hungary went to Wembley and won 6–3. No team from outside the British Isles had ever won there.

Puskás scored twice. The first is still the goal people picture. With England's captain Billy Wright sliding in, he dragged the ball back with the sole of his foot, Wright went past him and on to the ground, and Puskás finished with his left.

Bobby Robson, 20 years old, was in the crowd. As he later put it on BBC One's Football Focus: "they were men from Mars as far as we were concerned."

Six months later Hungary beat England 7–1 in Budapest. It is still England's heaviest defeat.

Between 1950 and 1956 they lost one match. The 1954 World Cup final, 3–2 to West Germany, after leading 2–0 inside eight minutes.

Two years later, it was broken up.

FIFA banned the players who stayed abroad. For Puskás it meant no competitive football until 1958.

He drifted. By the summer of 1958 he was 31 and, by one account, more than 18 kilos over his playing weight.

Most of European football thought he was finished.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The club that took the gamble was the one club that least needed him.

Real Madrid had already won the first three European Cups. They had Alfredo Di Stéfano and Francisco Gento. They did not need an overweight Hungarian who had not played a competitive match since 1956.

Emil Östreicher, who had worked with him at Honvéd and was now at Madrid, disagreed. They signed him anyway. He made his debut on 14 September 1958.

Puskás trained the weight off. He settled in beside Di Stéfano rather than competing with him.

Then he started scoring.

He was La Liga's top scorer four times in five seasons. Madrid won the league five years running. By the usual count he scored 156 goals in 180 league games, though at least one Spanish obituary gave 154.

Then came the part that surprised me.

On 18 May 1960, at Hampden Park, in front of more than 127,000 people, Madrid met Eintracht Frankfurt in the European Cup final.

Puskás scored four. A goal on the stroke of half-time, a disputed penalty, and two more in the next quarter of an hour, the last a shot on the turn high into the net.

Madrid won 7–3.

No player has scored more than four in a European Cup or Champions League final.

In 1962 he scored a hat-trick in the final and still lost, 5–3 to Benfica. He remains the only player with hat-tricks in two European Cup finals.

He did go home in the end. When he died on 17 November 2006, aged 79, Hungary declared the day of his funeral a day of national mourning.

The country that had lost him buried him like a king.

Here's what stays with me.

Age and weight are facts, not verdicts.

Puskás was written off by people looking at a photograph rather than a player.

And the greatest Hungarian team of all was broken up not by opponents, but by tanks.
