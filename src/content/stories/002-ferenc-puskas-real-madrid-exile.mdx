In the summer of 1958 Ferenc Puskás was 31 and, by one account, more than 18 kilos over his playing weight.

He had not played a competitive match since 1956. Most of European football thought he was finished.

Two years later, at Hampden Park, he scored four goals in a European Cup final.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

He had made himself an exile.

He was captain of the best national team in the world. In 1953 Hungary won 6–3 at Wembley. No team from outside the British Isles had ever won there.

Puskás scored twice. Six months later Hungary beat England 7–1 in Budapest. It is still England's heaviest defeat.

Then, in 1956, Soviet tanks crushed the Hungarian uprising. His club, Budapest Honvéd, were on tour in western Europe when the fighting began.

Instead of going home, they toured Italy, Portugal, Spain and Brazil, against the wishes of FIFA and their own federation.

Most of the squad went back. Puskás did not. FIFA banned the players who stayed abroad.

But here's where it gets interesting.

The club that took the gamble needed him least.

Real Madrid had already won the first three European Cups. They had Alfredo Di Stéfano. They signed him anyway.

Puskás trained the weight off. Then he started scoring.

He was La Liga's top scorer four times in five seasons. Madrid won the league five years running. By the usual count he scored 156 goals in 180 league games.

In the 1960 final, in front of more than 127,000 people, Madrid met Eintracht Frankfurt.

Puskás scored four. Madrid won 7–3.

No player has scored more than four in a European Cup or Champions League final.

When he died in 2006, aged 79, Hungary declared the day of his funeral a day of national mourning.

Here's what stays with me.

Age and weight are facts, not verdicts. Puskás was written off by people looking at a photograph rather than a player.

And the greatest Hungarian team of all was broken up not by opponents, but by tanks.
