With about a quarter of an hour left of the 1956 FA Cup final, Manchester City's goalkeeper threw himself head first at a forward's feet.

He got the ball. The forward's knee caught him in the neck.

He got up, unsteady, and stayed in goal to the end.

Nobody at Wembley knew his neck was broken. Not even him.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

His name was Bert Trautmann. Eleven years earlier he had been a German paratrooper.

He volunteered for the Luftwaffe at 17, fought on the Eastern Front and won the Iron Cross.

Late in the war British troops caught him. He expected to be shot.

Instead, as he told it, a British soldier offered him a cup of tea.

He was held at Camp 50 in Lancashire, where he played in goal.

In 1948 he was offered the chance to go home. He turned it down.

In October 1949 he signed for Manchester City, and the reaction was ferocious.

Supporters threatened to stay away. Letters of protest appeared in the papers.

Then came the part that surprised me.

The man who calmed it was a rabbi.

Alexander Altmann, the communal rabbi of Manchester, was himself a refugee from Nazi Germany. He wrote to the local press that a German who had committed no crime should not be punished for his country's crimes.

As Trautmann later put it: "Thanks to Altmann, after a month it was all forgotten."

Two days before the 1956 final, he became the first goalkeeper voted Footballer of the Year.

City were 3–1 up against Birmingham when Peter Murphy broke through.

On the Wembley steps, the Duke of Edinburgh remarked on how crooked his neck looked.

He was told it was a crick. An X-ray was later reported to show five dislocated vertebrae, the second cracked in two.

Here's what stays with me.

The neck is the part everyone remembers. The harder thing came first. A city with every reason to hate him chose to judge the man in front of it.

The broken neck only confirmed what Manchester had already decided.
