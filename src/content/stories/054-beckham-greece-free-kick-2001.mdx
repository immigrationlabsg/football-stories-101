Old Trafford, 6 October 2001. The 93rd minute. England were losing 2–1 to Greece, and their World Cup place was slipping away.

David Beckham stood over a free kick about 30 yards out.

He had already failed with five long-range free kicks that afternoon.

The sixth curled over a four-man wall and into the top corner. England were going to the 2002 World Cup.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Beckham had been captain for under 11 months, and he got the armband from a caretaker.

After Kevin Keegan resigned, Peter Taylor took charge for one game, against Italy in Turin in November 2000. He made Beckham, aged 25, captain. England lost 1–0.

At the 1998 World Cup, with Argentina level at 2–2 early in the second half, Beckham had been sent off for kicking out at Diego Simeone. England went out on penalties.

He was blamed. He was booed at grounds across England, and, his father said, bullets turned up in letters.

So the Greece game carried more than a ticket to Japan and South Korea.

With Germany, their group rivals, held 0–0 by Finland, a draw would put England top. Defeat meant a play-off against Ukraine.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Greece led through Angelos Charisteas, nine minutes before half-time. In the 68th minute Teddy Sheringham, 35, came on for Robbie Fowler and headed in a Beckham free kick about 10 seconds later.

Within a minute, Demis Nikolaidis had put Greece back in front.

England pushed. Beckham kept shooting from set pieces, and kept missing.

Then, deep in stoppage time, Sheringham was fouled about 30 yards from goal, dead centre.

Sheringham wanted to take it himself. Beckham would not give up the ball.

The man who had failed five times stepped up for a sixth, in front of the crowd at his own club's ground.

Here's what stays with me.

Five failures do not stop him asking for one more.

He takes the ball from a team-mate who wants it.

And the player blamed for one World Cup exit is the one who sends England to the next.
