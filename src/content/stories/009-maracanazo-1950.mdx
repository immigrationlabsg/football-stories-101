On the morning of 16 July 1950 the Rio de Janeiro newspaper O Mundo printed a photograph of Brazil's footballers beneath a headline that read, in FIFA's translation, "Here Are The World Champions". The match that was meant to make it true had not yet been played. That afternoon, in the new Maracanã stadium, Brazil needed only a draw against Uruguay to win the World Cup for the first time. The official attendance was 173,850, the largest crowd ever recorded for a World Cup match, and FIFA notes that it is widely believed more than 200,000 were inside.

By the end of the afternoon the noise had gone. A Uruguayan winger called Alcides Ghiggia had beaten the Brazilian goalkeeper at his near post, and a country that had built a new stadium to watch itself win had watched someone else do it.

## The final that was not a final

The 1950 tournament did not have a final. After the group stage, four teams, Brazil, Uruguay, Sweden and Spain, played each other once in a final pool, and whoever finished top was champion. Brazil played as if the formality were being observed in advance. They beat Sweden 7–1 and Spain 6–1. Uruguay drew 2–2 with Spain and needed two late goals to beat Sweden 3–2. Going into the last match, Brazil had four points and Uruguay three. A draw would do.

Almost nobody in Rio thought a draw would be needed. The press and the public treated the title as settled, and before kick-off the Brazilian players were addressed by a senior politician who hailed them as the victors of the tournament. Accounts differ on who it was: one names Rio's mayor, Ângelo Mendes de Moraes, another the state governor. Jules Rimet, the 76-year-old FIFA president whose name the trophy carried, had prepared a speech in Portuguese for the presentation. FIFA's own history of the match notes that he had written nothing in Spanish.

## Varela's afternoon

The Uruguay dressing room was a different place. The coach, Juan López, had planned to defend, to keep the score down and hope. The captain, Obdulio Varela, disagreed. In FIFA's account, when López left the room Varela told the players to ignore him; Uruguayan accounts have him arguing that sitting back would only bring the fate that had befallen Sweden and Spain. He was also, by several Uruguayan tellings, angered that officials of his own federation had asked the squad simply to avoid a heavy defeat. The line he is remembered for, that the people outside the pitch were made of wood and that on the field it would be eleven against eleven, has been handed down in several versions, and its exact wording and setting differ from one to the next. There is also a story, told by FIFA and others with varying detail, that Varela bought up copies of that morning's papers and spread them across the floor of the team's hotel bathroom for his team-mates to use as they saw fit.

The first half was goalless. Two minutes after the restart, Friaça scored for Brazil. The stadium erupted, and the draw that was enough had become a lead.

What Varela did next is the most studied minute of the afternoon. He walked into the Uruguay net, picked the ball up, tucked it under his arm and went to the English referee, George Reader, to protest that Friaça had been offside. He argued, repeatedly. Later, FIFA's history records, he admitted that he had known the goal was fair. He was not disputing it. He was letting the crowd's noise burn itself out before the game started again, and reminding his own team that the match was not over.

Uruguay began to push forward, and their route was down the right, where Ghiggia kept running at the Brazilian left-back, Bigode. In the 66th minute he got past him and pulled the ball back for Juan Alberto Schiaffino, who scored. It was 1–1, and a draw was still enough for Brazil.

In the 79th minute Ghiggia went past Bigode again. This time the Brazilian goalkeeper, Moacyr Barbosa, had seen the first goal and expected another cut-back. Ghiggia said later that Barbosa moved early, leaving a gap between himself and the near post, and that he had a matter of seconds to decide. He shot into the gap. Uruguay led 2–1, and that was the score at the end.

Ghiggia spent the rest of his long life repeating, in slightly different versions, the line that only three people had ever silenced the Maracanã, the Pope, Frank Sinatra and himself.

The presentation was a muddle. Rimet handed the trophy to Varela on the pitch, and the speech in Portuguese had been written for a different afternoon.

## The turn

The match turned on a captain's refusal to accept the role his own side had been given. Varela overruled his coach before kick-off, and when Brazil scored, the moment at which most teams in that stadium would have been swept away, he chose to slow everything down. He bought Uruguay a minute of quiet in front of the largest crowd in World Cup history, and in that minute the game stopped being a coronation and became a contest again. None of it would have counted for anything without what came after. The last act belonged to Ghiggia, but it depended on a half-step by a goalkeeper reading the previous goal, and on a winger with the nerve to shoot where nobody expected.

## What Brazil did with it

Brazil looked for people to blame, and found them. Barbosa and the two defenders most closely associated with the goals, Bigode and Juvenal, were Black, and they were made scapegoats in what later writers have described as a racist reckoning. Barbosa carried it longest. He told of a woman who, years later, pointed him out to her small child as the man who had made all of Brazil cry; in his tellings the place changes, a shop, a bar, a supermarket, but the sentence does not. In the early 1990s, by several accounts, he was turned away when he tried to visit the Brazil squad preparing for the 1994 World Cup, for fear he would bring bad luck. He told reporters that the maximum prison sentence in Brazil was 30 years, and that he had been paying for longer than that for a crime he had not committed. He died in 2000, half a century after the afternoon that defined him, and the goalkeeper who had been good enough to keep goal for the host nation at a World Cup is remembered almost entirely for one half-step.

The country also changed its clothes, and the change says as much about the wound as anything written at the time. Brazil had played the match in white, and the white shirt was dropped. In 1953 the newspaper Correio da Manhã ran a competition for a new kit that had to use the colours of the national flag. It was won by Aldyr Garcia Schlee, a young man from Pelotas who later became a cartoonist, whose design of a yellow shirt with green trim, blue shorts and white socks Brazil wore at the 1954 World Cup and have worn ever since. Schlee, it was reported when he died in 2018, supported Uruguay.

The playwright Nelson Rodrigues called the defeat "our Hiroshima". A boy of nine or ten saw his father, Dondinho, sobbing beside the radio that evening, and told him not to cry: one day he would win the World Cup for him. Eight years later, aged 17, Pelé did.

Ghiggia died in Montevideo on 16 July 2015, aged 88. His son, Arcadio, said he had suffered a heart attack. It was 65 years to the day since the goal.

## What it tells us

Certainty is the most dangerous thing a favourite can carry into a match. Brazil had scored 13 goals in their previous two matches, but they walked out to a newspaper headline, a politician's speech and a crowd that had come for a party, and Uruguay walked out with a captain who had decided that none of it applied to them. The Maracanazo is remembered as an upset, and it was one, but it is also a lesson in what one person can do with the ball under his arm and a minute of silence.
