On the morning of 16 July 1950, a Rio paper printed Brazil's team photo. Its headline, in FIFA's translation: "Here Are The World Champions".

The match had not yet been played.

That afternoon, in the new Maracanã, Brazil needed only a draw against Uruguay. The official crowd was 173,850.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

There was no final in 1950, just a four-team pool. Brazil had beaten Sweden 7–1 and Spain 6–1.

Jules Rimet, FIFA's president, had a speech ready in Portuguese, none in Spanish.

Uruguay's coach planned to defend. In FIFA's account, the captain, Obdulio Varela, told the players to ignore him.

Just after half-time, Friaça scored for Brazil. The stadium erupted.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Varela walked into his own net, tucked the ball under his arm and went to argue offside with the referee.

He later admitted, FIFA's history records, that the goal was fair. He was letting the noise burn itself out.

Alcides Ghiggia kept running at the left-back, Bigode. In the 66th minute he pulled the ball back for Juan Alberto Schiaffino to score.

A draw was still enough.

In the 79th minute Ghiggia beat Bigode again. The goalkeeper, Moacyr Barbosa, expected a cut-back and moved early. Ghiggia shot into the gap at his near post.

Uruguay won 2–1.

Rimet handed the trophy to Varela on the pitch. The speech in Portuguese had been written for a different afternoon.

Brazil looked for people to blame. Barbosa, Bigode and the defender Juvenal were Black, and later writers have called what followed a racist reckoning.

The white shirt was dropped. Its yellow replacement was designed by Aldyr Garcia Schlee, who, it was reported when he died, supported Uruguay.

Here's what stays with me. Brazil walked out to a headline, a speech and a party. Uruguay walked out with a captain who had decided none of it applied. One man, a ball under his arm and a minute of quiet.

A whole country changed its shirt.
