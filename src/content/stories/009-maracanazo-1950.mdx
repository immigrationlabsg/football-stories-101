On the morning of 16 July 1950, the Rio de Janeiro newspaper O Mundo printed a photograph of Brazil's footballers. Its headline read, in FIFA's translation: "Here Are The World Champions".

The match that was meant to make it true had not yet been played.

That afternoon, in the new Maracanã, Brazil needed only a draw against Uruguay to win the World Cup for the first time.

The official crowd was 173,850, the largest ever recorded for a World Cup match.

By the end of the afternoon, the noise had gone.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

The 1950 tournament had no final. Four teams played each other once in a final pool. Brazil beat Sweden 7–1 and Spain 6–1.

Going into the last match, a draw would do for Brazil.

Almost nobody thought it would come to that. Before kick-off a senior politician hailed the players as the victors. Accounts differ on whether it was Rio's mayor or the state governor.

Jules Rimet, the 76-year-old FIFA president, had prepared a speech in Portuguese for the presentation. He had written nothing in Spanish.

Uruguay's coach, Juan López, planned to defend and keep the score down. His captain, Obdulio Varela, disagreed. In FIFA's account, when López left the room, Varela told the players to ignore him.

There is also a story, told with varying detail, that Varela bought up copies of that morning's papers and spread them across the floor of the team's hotel bathroom, for his team-mates to use as they saw fit.

The first half was goalless. Two minutes after the restart, Friaça scored for Brazil.

The stadium erupted. FIFA notes that many believe more than 200,000 were inside.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Varela walked into his own net, picked up the ball, tucked it under his arm and went to the referee to protest that Friaça had been offside.

He argued, repeatedly.

Later, FIFA's history records, he admitted he had known the goal was fair. He was not disputing it. He was letting the crowd's noise burn itself out, and reminding his own team that the match was not over.

Uruguay began to push down the right, where Alcides Ghiggia kept running at the left-back, Bigode. In the 66th minute he got past him and pulled the ball back for Juan Alberto Schiaffino, who scored.

It was 1–1. A draw was still enough.

In the 79th minute Ghiggia went past Bigode again. The goalkeeper, Moacyr Barbosa, expected another cut-back and moved early. Ghiggia shot into the gap at his near post.

Uruguay won 2–1.

For the rest of his long life Ghiggia repeated, in slightly different versions, that only three people had ever silenced the Maracanã: the Pope, Frank Sinatra and himself.

Rimet handed the trophy to Varela on the pitch. The speech in Portuguese had been written for a different afternoon.

Brazil looked for people to blame. Barbosa, Bigode and the defender Juvenal were Black, and later writers have described what followed as a racist reckoning.

Barbosa carried it longest. He told of a woman who pointed him out to her small child as the man who had made all of Brazil cry. In his tellings the place changes. The sentence does not.

The country even changed its clothes. The white shirt was dropped. In 1953 a newspaper competition for a new kit in the colours of the flag was won by Aldyr Garcia Schlee.

His yellow shirt with green trim is the one Brazil have worn ever since.

Schlee, it was reported when he died, supported Uruguay.

That evening, a boy of nine or ten saw his father, Dondinho, sobbing beside the radio, and promised to win the World Cup for him. Eight years later, aged 17, Pelé did.

Ghiggia died on 16 July 2015, aged 88. It was 65 years to the day since the goal.

Here's what stays with me.

Brazil walked out to a headline, a speech and a party. Uruguay walked out with a captain who had decided none of it applied to them. One man, a ball under his arm and a minute of quiet. A whole country changed its shirt.
