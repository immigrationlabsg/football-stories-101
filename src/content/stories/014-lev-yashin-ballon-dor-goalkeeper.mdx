The summer of 1962 was the worst of Lev Yashin's career.

Accounts describe the windows of his flat being broken and abusive letters arriving. At Dynamo Moscow, his name was whistled.

He seriously considered giving up.

A year and a half later, he won the Ballon d'Or. He remains the only goalkeeper who ever has.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Yashin was not supposed to be a footballer at all. During the Second World War, aged about 12, he was sent to work in a Moscow munitions factory.

He kept goal at ice hockey. The factory had no hockey team, so he joined the football side.

They started him as a forward. They kept moving him back until, reluctantly, he went in goal.

Then came Olympic gold in 1956 and the first European Nations' Cup in 1960. In his almost black kit, he became the Black Spider.

Then came Chile.

On 3 June 1962 the Soviet Union led Colombia 4–1 with about 20 minutes left. It finished 4–4. A week later Chile knocked them out 2–1.

Both times, the goalkeeper took the blame.

Then came the part that surprised me.

On 23 October 1963, the day after his 34th birthday, he walked out at Wembley for the Rest of the World against England.

He was the only Soviet player in the side, due to play only the first half. England attacked for most of it, and nothing went past him.

The timing mattered as much as the saves. The Ballon d'Or voters were journalists from across Europe. Most had never seen a Soviet league match.

A few weeks before they voted, they watched him keep out England for a whole half.

He finished with 73 points, ahead of Gianni Rivera and Jimmy Greaves.

Since 2019 the ceremony has given a separate prize to the best goalkeeper. It is called the Yashin Trophy.

Here's what stays with me.

One summer, people broke his windows. The next autumn, he gave them 45 minutes they could not argue with.

The gap between blame and honour can be one more season of refusing to quit.
