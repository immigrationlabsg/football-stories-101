On Wednesday 23 October 1963, the day after his 34th birthday, Lev Yashin walked out at Wembley in front of a full house to keep goal for the Rest of the World. The Football Association was 100 years old, and to celebrate it had invited FIFA to pick a side to play England. Yashin was the only Soviet player in it, standing behind a team that included Ferenc Puskás, Denis Law and a 21-year-old Eusébio.

He was due to play only the first half. England, roared on by their own crowd, attacked for most of it, and for 45 minutes nothing went past him. At half-time he was replaced with the score still 0–0. In the second half Terry Paine put England ahead, Law equalised and Jimmy Greaves scored the winner late on. England won 2–1.

Eusébio had already seen it coming in the warm-up.

> We’d been practising before the game – shots, penalties – and he’d been saving everything.
> — Eusébio, recalling the 1963 Wembley match, as quoted by FIFA

Two months later, Europe’s football journalists chose their player of the year. They chose the goalkeeper.

## A factory team and an ice rink

Yashin was born in Moscow on 22 October 1929. When the Soviet Union entered the Second World War he was still a boy, and like millions of others he was put to work for the war effort: at about 12 he was sent to a munitions factory. He had kept goal at ice hockey, but the factory had no hockey team, so he joined the football side. According to FIFA, they started him as a forward and kept moving him back until, reluctantly, he went in goal and found he liked it. It was there, playing for the works team, that Dynamo Moscow spotted him.

At Dynamo he joined a club that already had a famous goalkeeper, Alexei Khomich, and he had to wait. Accounts differ on when he arrived, with dates given from 1945 to 1949, but they agree on the waiting. He made his first appearances for the football team around 1950 and did not establish himself until 1953. In the meantime he kept goal for Dynamo’s ice hockey team too, and for a while he was more successful on the ice than on grass. Britannica credits Khomich with spotting what Yashin could become and training him as his successor.

Once he had the shirt he never gave it back. He spent his whole career at Dynamo, and with the Soviet Union he won Olympic gold in Melbourne on 8 December 1956, beating Yugoslavia 1–0 in the final. Four years later he won the first European Nations’ Cup. In the final at the Parc des Princes in Paris on 10 July 1960, Yugoslavia had much the better of the match and led through Milan Galić, but Yashin kept them to that one goal. Slava Metreveli equalised, and in extra time Viktor Ponedelnik headed the winner. UEFA’s own history of the tournament treats Yashin as the hero of the night.

What made him different was not only what he stopped but where he stood to stop it. Most goalkeepers of the 1950s stayed on their line. Yashin came off it, to claim crosses and to sweep up balls played in behind his defenders, sometimes well outside his penalty area, and he punched the ball clear at a time when most keepers tried to catch everything. He was loud, too, directing his defence throughout a match. FIFA calls him a forefather of the sweeper-keepers of today. He played in a dark kit, almost black, and he became known as the Black Spider and the Black Panther.

## Chile, 1962

The year before Wembley was the worst of his career. At the 1962 World Cup in Chile the Soviet Union were drawn in a group played in Arica, in the far north of the country. On 3 June they led Colombia 4–1 with about 20 minutes left. Then Marcos Coll scored direct from a corner, an Olympic goal, and Colombia scored twice more. The match finished 4–4, and much of the blame landed on the goalkeeper.

The Soviet Union still reached the quarter-final, and on 10 June in Arica met the hosts in the quarter-final. Leonel Sánchez scored from a free-kick after about 11 minutes, Igor Chislenko equalised, and a few minutes later Eladio Rojas scored from long range. Chile won 2–1, and Yashin took the blame. Later accounts, including one by Ponedelnik, said he had been concussed during the tournament and was not himself, but at the time nobody was making allowances.

At home it turned ugly. Accounts of that summer describe the windows of his flat being broken, his car being vandalised and abusive letters arriving, and he was whistled when his name was read out before Dynamo’s first matches after the tournament. He seriously considered giving up.

## The turn

He did not give up, and in 1963 he played the best football of his life. Dynamo won the Soviet league. Then came Wembley, and the timing mattered as much as the saves. The Ballon d’Or was voted for by journalists from across Europe, most of whom had never seen a Soviet league match. In October 1963, with a few weeks to go before they voted, the best of them saw Yashin in one of the most watched friendlies ever staged, in the stadium most of them knew best, keeping out England for a whole half. ESPN, looking back at why a goalkeeper had won, points to two things: his style, which took him far from his goal and made him a spectacle, and that Wembley match.

The vote was organised by France Football. Twenty-one journalists took part, and Yashin finished with 73 points. Gianni Rivera, whose Milan had beaten Eusébio’s Benfica in the European Cup final at Wembley that year, was second, and Greaves third. Yashin was the first Soviet player to win it, and he remains the only goalkeeper. In the spring of 1964 the editor of France Football flew to Moscow to hand him the trophy in person.

The award comes with an asterisk that Yashin never knew about. Until 1995 only European players were eligible, and in 2016, for the prize’s 60th anniversary, France Football re-examined its early winners. It concluded that, had the award been open to everyone, Pelé would have won in 1963. It is a fair point about Pelé and no reflection on Yashin, who could only beat the players he was allowed to face.

## The penalty and the hug

The same names kept meeting. At the 1966 World Cup in England, the Soviet Union reached the semi-finals, their best result, and played Portugal for third place at Wembley on 28 July. Early in the game Portugal were given a penalty and Eusébio, the friend Yashin had made at the same stadium three years earlier, took it. Yashin was the most famous penalty-stopper in the world. FIFA credits him with more than 150 saved over his career, though nobody has a match-by-match record of them. Eusébio scored. The two men hugged. Portugal won 2–1, and Eusébio finished the tournament as its top scorer.

A line often attributed to Yashin, though nobody has traced where he first said it, is that the joy of seeing Yuri Gagarin fly into space was surpassed only by the joy of a good penalty save. Whatever its origin, it caught how he saw the job.

In 1971 he played his farewell match at the Lenin Stadium in Moscow, with famous players from abroad in the opposing team and a crowd reported at around 100,000. In the mid-1980s, after thrombophlebitis, one of his legs was amputated. He died on 20 March 1990, aged 60.

The goalkeepers who came after him did not forget. Gordon Banks, England’s goalkeeper when they won the World Cup in 1966, was asked by a Russian journalist in 2018 whether he had been better than Yashin.

> Yashin is the greatest goalkeeper and it is out of the question.
> — Gordon Banks, as reported by TASS, 2018

Since 2019 the Ballon d’Or ceremony has handed out a separate prize to the best goalkeeper in the world. France Football named it the Yashin Trophy. No goalkeeper has won the main award since 1963.

## What it tells us

Awards measure what voters see, and in 1963 the voters saw Yashin at the right moment, in the right place, doing something no goalkeeper had made look so exciting. His Ballon d’Or is not only a record but a puzzle: the only time the people who judge football agreed that stopping goals could be worth as much as scoring them. The comeback from Chile is the part that makes it a story.
