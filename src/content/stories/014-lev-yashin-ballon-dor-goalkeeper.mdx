The summer of 1962 was the worst of Lev Yashin's career.

Accounts of it describe the windows of his flat being broken, his car being vandalised and abusive letters arriving. When his name was read out before Dynamo Moscow's first matches after the World Cup, he was whistled.

He seriously considered giving up.

A year and a half later, Europe's football journalists voted him their player of the year. He remains the only goalkeeper who has ever won the Ballon d'Or.

The trouble had started in Arica, in the far north of Chile.

On 3 June 1962 the Soviet Union led Colombia 4–1 with about 20 minutes left. Then Marcos Coll scored direct from a corner, and Colombia scored twice more.

It finished 4–4, and much of the blame landed on the goalkeeper.

A week later Chile beat the Soviet Union 2–1 in the quarter-final, and Yashin took the blame again. Later accounts, including one by Viktor Ponedelnik, said he had been concussed during the tournament.

At the time, nobody was making allowances.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Yashin was not supposed to be a footballer at all. Born in Moscow in 1929, he was sent to work in a munitions factory at about 12, during the Second World War.

He kept goal at ice hockey, but the factory had no hockey team, so he joined the football side.

They started him as a forward and kept moving him back until, reluctantly, he went in goal and found he liked it. Dynamo Moscow spotted him there.

Even then he had to wait. Dynamo already had a famous goalkeeper, Alexei Khomich, and Yashin did not establish himself until 1953.

In the meantime he kept goal for Dynamo's ice hockey team too. For a while he was more successful on the ice than on grass.

He won Olympic gold in 1956 and the first European Nations' Cup in 1960. He came off his line when other keepers stayed on it, and shouted at his defence all match.

In his dark kit, almost black, he became the Black Spider.

In 1963 he answered.

Dynamo won the Soviet league. Then, on 23 October, the day after his 34th birthday, he walked out at Wembley for the Rest of the World against England, in a match to mark the Football Association's centenary.

He was the only Soviet player in a side that included Ferenc Puskás, Denis Law and a 21-year-old Eusébio.

He was due to play only the first half. England attacked for most of it, and nothing went past him. At half-time it was still 0–0.

Eusébio had seen it coming. As he recalled, as quoted by FIFA: "We’d been practising before the game – shots, penalties – and he’d been saving everything."

Then came the part that surprised me.

The timing mattered as much as the saves. The Ballon d'Or voters were journalists from across Europe, most of whom had never seen a Soviet league match.

A few weeks before they voted, they watched Yashin keep out England for a whole half, in the stadium most of them knew best.

He finished with 73 points, ahead of Gianni Rivera and Jimmy Greaves. In the spring of 1964 the editor of France Football flew to Moscow to hand him the trophy in person.

There is an asterisk he never knew about. In 2016 France Football concluded that Pelé, then ineligible as a non-European, would have won in 1963.

Yashin could only beat the players he was allowed to face.

Three years after Wembley, the two friends met there again, in the third-place match at the 1966 World Cup. Portugal were given an early penalty. Eusébio took it against the most famous penalty-stopper in the world.

Eusébio scored. The two men hugged.

Since 2019 the Ballon d'Or ceremony has given a separate prize to the world's best goalkeeper. It is called the Yashin Trophy.

Here's what stays with me.

One summer, people broke his windows. The next autumn, he gave them 45 minutes they could not argue with. The gap between being blamed and being honoured is sometimes just one more season of refusing to quit.
