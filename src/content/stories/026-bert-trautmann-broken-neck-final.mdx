With about a quarter of an hour left of the 1956 FA Cup final, Manchester City's goalkeeper threw himself head first at a forward's feet.

He got the ball. The forward's knee caught him in the neck.

He stayed down and was given first aid in his goalmouth. Then he got up, unsteady, went back between the posts and stayed there until the final whistle.

Nobody at Wembley knew his neck was broken. Not even him.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

His name was Bert Trautmann. Eleven years earlier he had been a German paratrooper.

He grew up in Hitler's Germany, joined the Hitler Youth like many boys of his generation, and volunteered for the Luftwaffe at 17. He fought on the Eastern Front and was awarded the Iron Cross.

The Russians captured him. He escaped. Late in the war British troops caught him, and by his own account he expected to be shot.

Instead, as he told it for the rest of his life, a British soldier offered him a cup of tea.

Because he had volunteered, the British graded him category C: a convinced Nazi. He was later regraded and moved to Camp 50 at Ashton-in-Makerfield, between Wigan and St Helens. In the camp he played in goal.

In 1948 Trautmann was offered the chance to go home. He turned it down.

A former paratrooper with an Iron Cross chose to stay among the people who had held him prisoner.

In October 1949 he signed for Manchester City, and the reaction was ferocious.

Supporters threatened to withdraw their support. Letters of protest appeared in the papers. Many accounts put the street protest at 20,000 people; at least one writer has questioned whether it happened at all.

Then came the part that surprised me.

What nobody disputes is the letter, and who wrote it.

Alexander Altmann, the communal rabbi of Manchester, was himself a refugee from Nazi Germany. He wrote to the local press that an individual German who had committed no crime should not be punished for the crimes of his country. Each case, he argued, should be judged on its merits.

As Trautmann told the interviewer Louise Taylor in 2010: "Thanks to Altmann, after a month it was all forgotten."

He never tidied up his own past. To his biographer, Catrine Clay, he described stumbling with a friend on a massacre of Jews by SS men in a forest in Ukraine.

Only later, in an English prisoner-of-war camp, was he told the full scale of the concentration camps. He never claimed to have understood more at the time than he had.

By 1956 he was City's goalkeeper, and something more. Two days before the final, England's football writers voted him Footballer of the Year, the first goalkeeper to win it.

City were 3–1 up against Birmingham when Peter Murphy broke through. Accounts put the collision in the 73rd or the 75th minute.

On the Wembley steps, the Duke of Edinburgh remarked on how crooked his neck looked.

He was told it was a crick. An X-ray in Manchester a few days later was reported to show five dislocated vertebrae, the second of them cracked in two.

Within months, his five-year-old son, John, was killed by a car near the family home.

Trautmann came back and kept goal for City until 1964, more than 500 games in all. He died in Spain in 2013, aged 89. It was the German football federation that announced it.

Here's what stays with me.

The neck is the part everyone remembers. The harder thing came first: a crowd with every reason to hate him was persuaded to judge the man in front of them. The broken neck did not win Manchester over. It confirmed what the city had already decided.
