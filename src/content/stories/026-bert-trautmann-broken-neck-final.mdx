There was about a quarter of an hour left at Wembley on 5 May 1956, and Manchester City were 3–1 up against Birmingham City in the FA Cup final, when the Birmingham forward Peter Murphy broke through on goal. City's goalkeeper came off his line and threw himself head first at Murphy's feet. He got the ball. Murphy's knee caught him in the neck. The goalkeeper stayed down and was given first aid in his goalmouth. When he got up, unsteady on his feet, he went back between the posts and stayed there until the final whistle.

His name was Bert Trautmann. Eleven years earlier he had been a German paratrooper. Two days before the final, England's football writers had voted him their Footballer of the Year, the first goalkeeper to receive the award. Nobody at Wembley, including Trautmann, knew that his neck was broken. A news-agency photograph of the scene in the City goalmouth went out that evening with a caption that recorded only that the German-born goalkeeper had injured his neck diving for the ball, and that he was unsteady for a while but did not stop playing.

## A boy of the Reich

Trautmann grew up in Hitler's Germany, which meant growing up inside its youth movement. He joined the Hitler Youth, like many boys of his generation, and at 17 he volunteered for the Luftwaffe. He served as a paratrooper, fought on the Eastern Front, and was awarded the Iron Cross. Late in life he was blunt about what that upbringing had done to him.

> Growing up in Hitler's Germany, you had no mind of your own.
> — Bert Trautmann, interviewed by Louise Taylor, 2010

That was not offered as an excuse, and it was not the whole of his own account. In a 2010 newspaper interview, and to his biographer Catrine Clay, he described the moment he first sensed that something had gone badly wrong: in Ukraine, he and a friend stumbled on a massacre of Jews by SS men in a forest. It was only later, in an English prisoner-of-war camp, that he was told the full scale of what had been done in the concentration camps. He did not claim to have been a secret dissenter, and he did not pretend that he had understood more at the time than he had. That refusal to tidy up his own past is part of why his later accounts carry weight.

His war ended in a run of captures. The Russians took him prisoner, and he escaped. Late in the war, on the Western Front, he was caught by British troops. By his own account he expected to be shot. Instead, the story he told for the rest of his life went, a British soldier greeted him with an offer of a cup of tea. The line is quoted in several slightly different forms, but the point of it never changed: the first thing his enemy offered him was a hot drink.

## Camp 50

The British screened their prisoners for ideology. Because Trautmann had volunteered, he was graded category C, which meant the authorities regarded him as a convinced Nazi. He was later regraded to the non-Nazi category B and moved north to Prisoner of War Camp 50 at Ashton-in-Makerfield, between Wigan and St Helens in Lancashire, where he stayed until 1948.

In the camp he played football, in goal, and word of the tall German's talent travelled beyond the wire. When the chance came in 1948 to be sent home, Trautmann turned it down and stayed in Lancashire. In October 1949 he signed for Manchester City, then a First Division club.

The reaction was ferocious. Supporters threatened to withdraw their support, and open letters of protest appeared in the newspapers. The size of the street protest has grown in the retelling. Many accounts put it at 20,000 people, one at 25,000, and at least one writer has questioned whether a demonstration on that scale ever happened. What is not disputed is the intervention of Alexander Altmann, the communal rabbi of Manchester and himself a refugee from Nazi Germany. In an open letter to the local press he argued that an individual German who had committed no crime should not be punished for the crimes of his country, and that each case should be judged on its merits. Accounts differ on which Manchester paper printed it. They agree on the effect.

> Thanks to Altmann, after a month it was all forgotten.
> — Bert Trautmann, interviewed by Louise Taylor, 2010

## The turn

The decision that made everything else possible was Trautmann's in 1948, when he turned down the chance to go home. He was a former paratrooper with an Iron Cross, in a country that had been bombed by his air force, and he chose to stay among the people who had held him prisoner. A rabbi's letter eased his way into Maine Road, but it was the staying that put him there. It meant that by 1956 he was not a curiosity at Manchester City but its goalkeeper, chosen by the country's football writers as the best player in England. He played more than 500 games for the club.

There is a second hinge inside the first. Staying was not a single choice made once in 1948; it had to be made again in 1949, when the city that had signed him seemed to want him gone. He stayed then too, and by his own account the hostility had faded within a month. The broken neck seven years later did not win him acceptance. It confirmed something Manchester had already decided.

## Wembley, 5 May 1956

The final itself had begun at a gallop. Joe Hayes put City ahead in the third minute and Noel Kinsey equalised for Birmingham in the 15th. Midway through the second half City pulled away with two goals in quick succession from Jack Dyson and Bobby Johnstone; match records disagree on which of them came first. Birmingham had to throw players forward. That was how Murphy came to be running at Trautmann's goal with the game almost over.

Accounts disagree slightly on the minute of the collision. Some give the 75th, others say there were 17 minutes left, which would make it the 73rd. All of them agree on what followed. Trautmann played on, dazed and in visible pain, and City held their lead. When he went up the Wembley steps to collect his medal, the Duke of Edinburgh remarked on how crooked his neck looked.

He was told at first that it was a crick in the neck. Only when he had it X-rayed in Manchester, three days after the final in most accounts and four in some, did the extent of it become clear. The X-ray was reported to show five dislocated vertebrae, the second of them cracked in two.

That was not the end of the year's damage. Within months his five-year-old son, John, was killed when he was hit by a car near the family home in Manchester.

He came back from the neck injury and kept goal for City until 1964. His testimonial at Maine Road that April drew one of the biggest crowds ever seen for such a match. He settled in later life in Spain, where he died at La Llosa, near Valencia, on 19 July 2013. He was 89. It was the German football federation that announced his death, a last reminder that he had belonged to two countries at once.

## What it tells us

Trautmann's story is usually told as a tale about physical courage, and the neck is the part everyone remembers. The harder thing is what happened before the final: a crowd that had every reason to hate him was persuaded, by one rabbi's letter and by his own decision to stay, to judge the man in front of them. Forgiveness in football rarely comes from the terraces first. Here it did, and it was rewarded.
