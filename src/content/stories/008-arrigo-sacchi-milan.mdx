On 19 April 1989, Real Madrid came to San Siro for a European Cup semi-final.

They lost 5–0, to five different scorers.

Milan's coach had never played professional football. For years he had sold shoes.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Arrigo Sacchi got the Milan job by beating Milan.

In 1986–87 his Parma side, then in Serie B, knocked Milan out of the Coppa Italia.

Silvio Berlusconi, Milan's new owner, remembered. In July 1987 he hired Sacchi, aged 41.

The Italian press was baffled. Sacchi's reply, in one widely reported translation: "I never realised that to be a jockey you had to be a horse first."

He was given Ruud Gullit, Marco van Basten and later Frank Rijkaard.

Italian football was built on man-marking. Sacchi marked space instead.

His Milan pressed in groups and pushed the defence high. He rehearsed it without a ball, calling out where the imaginary ball was.

At first it failed. Espanyol knocked Milan out of the UEFA Cup, and there was open talk of the sack.

Then came the part that surprised me.

By Sacchi's account, Berlusconi spoke to the squad for less than a minute. Sacchi was his coach, he said in substance. Those with him would stay. The rest could leave.

In May 1988 Milan won 3–2 at Diego Maradona's Napoli, the leaders, and were soon champions.

Next season, fog saved them. Milan were losing to Red Star in Belgrade and heading out of the European Cup when the match was abandoned. They won the replay on penalties.

In the final Gullit and Van Basten scored twice each. Milan beat Steaua Bucharest 4–0.

In 1988 Van Basten, Gullit and Rijkaard filled the Ballon d'Or podium, the first team-mates to do so.

In 1990 Milan beat Benfica 1–0 and kept the European Cup.

Here's what stays with me. A man who once sold shoes taught some of the world's best players to move as one. Pressing and a high line are now football's ordinary grammar. Someone had to make them work first.

And he had never been a horse.
