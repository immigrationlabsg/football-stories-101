On 19 April 1989 Real Madrid came to San Siro for the second leg of a European Cup semi-final with the tie level at 1–1. They left having lost 5–0. Carlo Ancelotti scored first, and then Frank Rijkaard, Ruud Gullit, Marco van Basten and Roberto Donadoni scored one each. Five goals from five different players, and a 6–1 aggregate win over the club that had won the European Cup more often than anyone. The more remarkable figures were at the other end. Madrid's attack, led by Hugo Sánchez, spent the evening squeezed into a few metres of grass by a back four, Mauro Tassotti, Franco Baresi, Alessandro Costacurta and Paolo Maldini, that kept stepping forward until there was nowhere left to stand.

The man on the Milan bench had never played professional football. Two years earlier he had been coaching Parma.

## A shoe salesman from Fusignano

Arrigo Sacchi was born on 1 April 1946 in Fusignano, a small town in northern Italy. He played as an amateur and was not good enough to go further. For years he earned his living as a shoe salesman, and he started coaching at 26 at a local club, Baracca Lugo, while still in the shoe trade.

From there he worked his way up the hard way: Bellaria, the youth team at Cesena, two spells at Rimini in Serie C1, the youth set-up at Fiorentina. In 1985 Parma, then in the third tier, made him their head coach. He won promotion to Serie B in his first season.

In his second, Parma were drawn against Milan in the Coppa Italia. Milan had just been bought by the businessman Silvio Berlusconi. Parma beat them 1–0 in the group stage, at San Siro, and then knocked them out 1–0 on aggregate in the first knockout round. Berlusconi did not forget the team that had beaten his twice. In July 1987 he appointed Sacchi, then aged 41, as Milan's coach.

The Italian football press found the choice baffling: how could a man who had achieved next to nothing as a player succeed as coach of Milan? Sacchi's answer has been quoted ever since, in several slightly different translations.

> I never realised that to be a jockey you had to be a horse first.
> — Arrigo Sacchi, replying to critics of his playing record on his appointment at Milan, 1987 (as widely reported in English translation)

Berlusconi gave him a remarkable squad. Gullit arrived from PSV Eindhoven in 1987 for a world-record fee and won the Ballon d'Or that December. Van Basten, the Ajax centre-forward, came the same summer. Rijkaard joined them a year later. Behind them stood a defence led by the captain, Baresi, and the young Maldini.

## Football without a ball

What Sacchi did with them was new to Italy. Italian football had been built on man-marking. Sacchi marked space, not men. His Milan played 4–4–2 with the defence pushed high, an aggressive offside trap, and the lines so close together that, by UEFA's description, the gap between defence and midfield was never more than 25 to 30 metres. When an opponent had the ball, Milan's players pressed in groups to close the passing lanes and win it back quickly.

The training was where it was built. Sacchi staged full matches without a ball, calling out where the imaginary ball was so that all 11 players could move into position as a unit. He set up exercises in which one side was outnumbered and repeated them until the players found the answers themselves. The sessions were relentless, and his players have said so.

> Arrigo completely changed Italian football – the philosophy, the training methods, the intensity, the tactics.
> — Carlo Ancelotti, quoted by UEFA.com

At first it did not work. In the autumn of 1987 Milan were knocked out of the UEFA Cup by Espanyol, who won 2–0 at San Siro and held on 0–0 in the return. There was open talk that the new coach would be dismissed.

## The turn

What happened next is Sacchi's own account, told years later after Berlusconi's death in 2023. The owner came to the training ground at Milanello, called the coach and the players together, and spoke for less than a minute. Sacchi, he said in substance, was the coach he had chosen; the players who were with him would stay at Milan, and those who were not could leave.

That was the decision that made the era: an owner backing a coach without a playing pedigree against a dressing room full of stars, at the moment it would have been easiest to sack him. Players who had doubted the methods now had no alternative to them.

Milan began to climb the table. On 1 May 1988, with two matches left, they went to Napoli, who had Diego Maradona and led the table by a point, and won 3–2. Two weeks later a 1–1 draw at Como made Milan champions of Italy. They had the best defence in Serie A.

The following season the method was tested in Europe, and it needed luck. On 9 November 1988, in Belgrade, Milan were losing 1–0 to Red Star, and heading out of the European Cup, when thick fog forced the referee to abandon the match. It was replayed from the start the next day. Just before half-time Donadoni was knocked unconscious in a collision and his jaw was fractured; Milan's staff forced his mouth open and cleared his airway on the pitch. In his memoir Sacchi called the team that survived that week the Immortals, and the name stuck. The match ended 1–1 and Milan won the penalty shoot-out.

Five months later came Real Madrid, and on 24 May 1989 the final against Steaua Bucharest at the Camp Nou, in front of more than 90,000 people, most of them from Milan. The club still describes the trip as the largest group outing in football history. Gullit scored after 18 and 38 minutes, Van Basten after 28 and 46. Milan won 4–0, their first European Cup since 1969.

The individual awards confirmed what the team had done. In the Ballon d'Or voting for 1988, Van Basten, Gullit and Rijkaard finished first, second and third, the first time team-mates had filled the podium. For 1989 it happened again: Van Basten first, Baresi second, Rijkaard third.

## The end of the era

On 23 May 1990, at the Prater in Vienna, Milan beat Benfica 1–0 with a goal from Rijkaard in the 67th minute and retained the European Cup. They also won the Intercontinental Cup in 1989 and 1990. In Serie A, though, the title of 1988 remained Sacchi's only one.

The era ended badly. In the European Cup quarter-final second leg at Marseille in March 1991, Milan were losing 1–0 to a goal by Chris Waddle when some of the floodlights failed near the end. When the lights came back, Milan refused to return to the pitch. UEFA awarded the match to Marseille 3–0 and banned Milan from European competition for a year. That summer Sacchi left to coach the Italian national team, whom he would take to the final of the 1994 World Cup, and Fabio Capello took over at Milan.

## What it tells us

Sacchi's Milan show that a team can be coached into being more than the sum of its stars, and that the coach does not need to have been one of them. Pressing, compactness and a high line are now the ordinary grammar of elite football, which makes it easy to forget that someone had to make them work first. That someone had once sold shoes.
