On 19 April 1989 Real Madrid came to San Siro for a European Cup semi-final, the tie level at 1–1.

They left having lost 5–0.

Five goals from five different players: Carlo Ancelotti, Frank Rijkaard, Ruud Gullit, Marco van Basten and Roberto Donadoni.

The man on the Milan bench had never played professional football. For years he had earned his living as a shoe salesman.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Arrigo Sacchi got the Milan job by beating Milan.

He was born in 1946 in Fusignano, a small town in northern Italy. He played as an amateur and was not good enough to go further.

He started coaching at 26, at Baracca Lugo, while still in the shoe trade.

In 1985 Parma, then in the third tier, made him head coach. He won promotion to Serie B in his first season.

In his second, Parma were drawn against Milan in the Coppa Italia. Milan had just been bought by Silvio Berlusconi.

Parma beat them 1–0 at San Siro, then knocked them out 1–0 on aggregate.

Berlusconi did not forget. In July 1987 he appointed Sacchi, aged 41, as Milan's coach.

The Italian press was baffled. How could a man who had achieved next to nothing as a player succeed at Milan?

His reply survives in slightly different translations. As he put it: "I never realised that to be a jockey you had to be a horse first."

Berlusconi gave him Gullit, Van Basten and, a year later, Rijkaard, with the captain, Franco Baresi, and the young Paolo Maldini behind them.

What Sacchi did with them was new to Italy.

Italian football had been built on man-marking. Sacchi marked space, not men.

His Milan pushed the defence high, pressed in groups and kept the lines so tight that, by UEFA's description, defence and midfield were never more than 25 to 30 metres apart.

And he rehearsed it without a ball.

He staged full matches, calling out where the imaginary ball was so that all 11 players moved as a unit.

At first it did not work. In the autumn of 1987 Espanyol knocked Milan out of the UEFA Cup, winning 2–0 at San Siro. There was open talk that the new coach would be dismissed.

Then came the part that surprised me.

By Sacchi's own account, told years later, Berlusconi came to the training ground at Milanello and spoke to the squad for less than a minute.

Sacchi, he said in substance, was the coach he had chosen. The players who were with him would stay. Those who were not could leave.

The players who had doubted the methods now had no alternative to them.

On 1 May 1988 Milan won 3–2 at Napoli, who had Diego Maradona and led the table. Two weeks later Milan were champions of Italy, with the best defence in Serie A.

The next season needed luck. In Belgrade, in November 1988, Milan were losing 1–0 to Red Star and heading out of the European Cup when thick fog forced the referee to abandon the match.

It was replayed from the start the next day. Milan drew 1–1 and won on penalties.

On 24 May 1989 came the final against Steaua Bucharest at the Camp Nou, in front of more than 90,000 people, most of them from Milan.

Gullit scored twice. Van Basten scored twice. Milan won 4–0.

The Ballon d'Or voting said it even more plainly. For 1988, Van Basten, Gullit and Rijkaard finished first, second and third, the first time team-mates had filled the podium.

For 1989 it happened again: Van Basten, Baresi, Rijkaard.

In 1990 Milan beat Benfica 1–0 in Vienna and kept the European Cup.

Sacchi had kept a notebook through that 1988–89 season. Decades later it became his memoir, published in English in 2021 under the name the team had earned: The Immortals.

Here's what stays with me.

A man who once sold shoes taught some of the best players in the world how to move together. Pressing, compactness and a high line are now the ordinary grammar of elite football. Someone had to make them work first, and he had never been a horse.
