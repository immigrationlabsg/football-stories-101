Over Christmas 1920, tens of thousands packed into Goodison Park to watch two teams of women.

Newspapers reported 53,000. The National Football Museum settles on at least 46,000.

Either way, it was the largest recorded crowd for a women’s match of its time.

Dick, Kerr Ladies beat St Helens Ladies 4–0.

Here’s what I didn’t know until recently.

On the winning side was a 15-year-old from St Helens. A year earlier she had played for the team they were beating.

Her name was Lily Parr.

In 1919 Dick, Kerr, a Preston works team, offered her a factory job and 10 shillings a game in expenses. She was 14, by most accounts.

She moved from left back to the left wing, where her left foot made her the star.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Less than a year after Goodison, the crowds had become the problem.

On 5 December 1921 the FA Council declared football “quite unsuitable for females”. It asked its clubs to refuse women’s teams the use of their grounds.

The FA did not ban women from playing. It had no power to.

It banned them from the places where football was watched.

Dick, Kerr looked abroad. In 1922 they toured the United States, playing men’s teams.

Parr trained as a nurse and worked at Whittingham Hospital, outside Preston. She kept playing into the early 1950s.

Estimates of her goals run from more than 900 to nearly 1,000.

At the hospital she met her partner, Mary. They lived together in Preston until Parr died in 1978, aged 73.

The FA voted to rescind the resolution in 1970. The ban had lasted 50 years.

In 2002 she was the only woman among the first inductees to the National Football Museum Hall of Fame. In 2019 the museum unveiled a life-size bronze of her, the first statue of a female footballer in the UK.

Here’s what stays with me.

The FA’s resolution did not stop women playing. It stopped people watching them, which was the point.

A player who could help fill Goodison spent most of her career out of sight.
