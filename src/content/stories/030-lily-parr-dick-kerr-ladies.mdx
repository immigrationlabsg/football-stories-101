Over Christmas 1920, tens of thousands of people packed into Goodison Park to watch two teams of women.

The Lancashire Evening Post reported 53,000, with around 10,000 more thought to have been locked out. The National Football Museum, weighing the reports, settles on at least 46,000.

Either way, it was the largest recorded crowd for a women’s match of its time. About £3,000 was raised for ex-servicemen.

Dick, Kerr Ladies beat St Helens Ladies 4–0.

Here’s what I didn’t know until recently.

On the winning side was a 15-year-old from St Helens. A year earlier she had been playing for the team they were beating.

Her name was Lily Parr.

She grew up playing football and rugby with her brothers on waste ground near home. Dick, Kerr Ladies were the works team of a Preston engineering factory that had turned to munitions in the war. In 1919 she caught the eye of Alfred Frankland, the office worker who had suggested the team in the first place.

He offered her a factory job and 10 shillings a game in expenses.

She was 14, by most accounts. She started at left back and moved to the left wing, where her left foot made her the team’s star.

She was also, by several accounts, a heavy smoker of Woodbine cigarettes.

The best-known story about her shot comes from her teammates, not from any match report. As the National Football Museum tells it, a male professional goalkeeper once dared her to score past him from the penalty spot. Her left-foot drive broke his arm.

It cannot be checked. It says something that it was told at all.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Less than a year after Goodison, the crowds had become the problem.

On 5 December 1921 the Football Association Council declared that the game was “quite unsuitable for females and ought not to be encouraged”. It asked its clubs to refuse women’s teams the use of their grounds. Its members were told not to referee their matches.

The FA did not ban women from playing. It had no power to.

It banned them from the places where football was watched.

The Council also complained that too much of the women’s gate money went on expenses rather than charity. Men’s clubs were under no obligation to give their takings to charity at all.

Dick, Kerr looked abroad.

In 1922 they sailed for North America, were barred from playing in Canada, and in the United States were booked against men’s teams instead. The RSSSF record lists nine matches: three wins, three draws, three defeats. One American history counts eight.

American newspapers, according to FIFA and History, called Parr the most brilliant woman player in the world.

At home, football did not pay a living. Parr trained as a nurse and worked at Whittingham Hospital outside Preston, which the team had raised money for, until she retired. She kept playing into the early 1950s.

Her goals for Dick, Kerr are estimated at anything from more than 900 to nearly 1,000, over some 30 years. Nobody can be sure. The records are incomplete.

At the hospital she met her partner, Mary. They lived together in Preston, making no secret of it, until Parr died of breast cancer in 1978, aged 73.

The FA had voted to rescind the resolution in January 1970. The ban had lasted 50 years.

In 2002 she was the only woman among the first inductees to the National Football Museum Hall of Fame. In June 2019 the museum unveiled a life-size bronze of her, the first statue of a female footballer in the UK.

It stands some 25 miles from the waste ground in St Helens where she learnt to play.

Here’s what stays with me.

The FA’s resolution did not stop women playing. It stopped people watching them, which was the point. A player who could help fill Goodison spent most of her career out of sight, and her goals went largely uncounted.
