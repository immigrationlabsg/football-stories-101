Over Christmas 1920, tens of thousands of people packed into Goodison Park, Everton’s ground in Liverpool, to watch two teams of women. Dick, Kerr Ladies, the works side of an engineering factory in Preston, were playing St Helens Ladies for the benefit of ex-servicemen. Nobody can now say exactly how many people were inside. The Lancashire Evening Post reported 53,000, with around 10,000 more thought to have been locked out; some accounts put the number left outside as high as 14,000. The National Football Museum, weighing the reports, settles on at least 46,000. Either way it was the largest recorded crowd for a women’s match of its time, and about £3,000 was raised for charity.

Charity was the reason the team existed in the form it did. Dick, Kerr Ladies had grown out of the women who filled the Preston works during the First World War, when it turned to munitions, and their matches raised money for causes such as the National Association of Discharged Sailors and Soldiers. Goodison was the grandest of those occasions.

Even the date has two versions. The match is remembered as the Boxing Day game of 26 December 1920, but the 26th fell on a Sunday that year, and some accounts place it on the bank holiday Monday, the 27th.

Dick, Kerr were without their star striker. Florrie Redford, according to accounts of the day, missed her train to Liverpool. So the captain, Alice Kell, normally a right back, moved up to centre forward. Jennie Harris gave Dick, Kerr a 1–0 lead at half-time, and in the second half Kell scored a hat-trick. It finished 4–0.

In the Dick, Kerr team that day was a 15-year-old from St Helens. A year earlier she had been playing for the side they were beating. Her name was Lily Parr, and within twelve months the men who ran English football would decide that what she did on a pitch should not be encouraged.

## The girl from St Helens

Lily Parr was born in St Helens, Lancashire, on 26 April 1905. She grew up playing football and rugby with her brothers on waste ground near home, and by 1919 she was turning out for St Helens Ladies. In a match against Dick, Kerr Ladies she caught the eye of their manager, Alfred Frankland, the office worker at the Dick, Kerr & Co. works who had suggested forming a team in the first place.

Frankland made her an offer: a job at the factory and 10 shillings a game in expenses. The sum was not special to her; other recruits of the period were offered the same rate. Parr was 14, by most accounts; one says 15. She started at left back and moved to the left wing, where her left foot made her the team’s star. She was also, by several accounts, a heavy smoker of Woodbine cigarettes.

The best-known story about her shot comes from her teammates rather than from any match report. As the National Football Museum tells it, a male professional goalkeeper once dared her to score past him from the penalty spot, and her left-foot drive broke his arm. Joan Whalley, who later played alongside her, is one of those credited with passing the story on. It cannot be checked, but it says something that it was told at all.

Her goals are hard to count for the same reason: the records are incomplete. Estimates of her total for Dick, Kerr range from more than 900 to nearly 1,000, scored over some 30 years with the club. The National Football Museum puts it at around 1,000.

## The turn

Less than a year after Goodison, the crowds had become the problem. Women’s matches, many of them staged for war charities, were drawing gates that men’s clubs envied, and the governing body was watching. On 5 December 1921, the Football Association acted. After complaints, the FA Council passed a resolution that is still preserved in its minute book, now held by the National Football Museum.

> The Council feel impelled to express their strong opinion that the game of football is quite unsuitable for females and ought not to be encouraged.
> — The Football Association Council, resolution of 5 December 1921

The resolution went on to question the money. Complaints had been made about how matches were arranged, and the Council said it was of the opinion that “an excessive proportion of the receipts are absorbed in expenses and an inadequate percentage devoted to charitable objects”. No such scrutiny was applied to the gate receipts of men’s clubs, which were under no obligation to give their takings to charity at all. Then came the instruction that mattered.

> The Council request the Clubs belonging to the Association to refuse the use of their grounds for such matches.
> — The Football Association Council, resolution of 5 December 1921

The wording was careful. The FA did not ban women from playing football, which it had no power to do. It banned them from the places where football was watched. Its members were also told not to act as referees or linesmen at women’s games. Without Goodison, Deepdale or any other affiliated ground, women’s teams were left with whatever pitches they could find, and without the crowds that had made them matter.

The reaction from the players was furious. The captain of Plymouth Ladies said the FA was “a hundred years behind the times” and called the decision “purely sex prejudice”. Many teams carried on regardless, wherever they could find a pitch.

Dick, Kerr looked abroad. In the autumn of 1922 they sailed for North America, expecting a long tour against women’s teams. They were barred from playing in Canada, and in the United States they were booked against men’s teams instead. The RSSSF record of the tour lists nine matches, with three wins, three draws and three defeats, and 40 goals scored and 40 conceded; one American history counts eight games. Parr was part of the touring party, and American newspapers, according to FIFA and History, called her the most brilliant woman player in the world.

## Nurse Parr

Football did not pay a living, and the factory work did not last. Parr trained as a nurse and went to work at Whittingham Hospital, the large mental hospital outside Preston. The connection was not an accident: the team had raised money for the hospital, and several players who lost their jobs found work there. Parr stayed until she retired.

She kept playing long after the ban had pushed the women’s game to the margins, into the early 1950s. At the hospital she met her partner, Mary, and the two lived together in Preston, making no secret of their relationship, until Parr’s death from breast cancer in 1978, at the age of 73.

The FA Council voted to rescind the 1921 resolution in January 1970, and the change was completed in 1971. The ban had lasted 50 years. Parr lived to see it lifted, but by then she was in her sixties and long retired from the game. The women’s game had spent half a century shut out of the grounds where crowds gathered.

## The statue in Manchester

Recognition came after her death, and slowly. When the National Football Museum opened its Hall of Fame in 2002, Parr was in the first group of inductees, and she was the only woman among them.

On 3 June 2019, days before the Women’s World Cup began in France, the museum in Manchester unveiled a life-size bronze of her by the sculptor Hannah Stewart, commissioned by the FA’s sponsor Mars. It was the first statue of a female footballer in the UK. It stands inside the museum, some 25 miles from the waste ground in St Helens where she learnt to play.

## What it tells us

The FA’s resolution did not stop women playing football; it stopped people from watching them, which was the point. Parr’s career shows what that cost: a player good enough to draw crowds that rival clubs envied spent most of her career out of sight, and her own goals went largely unrecorded. The game remembers her now partly because, for 50 years, it was told not to.
