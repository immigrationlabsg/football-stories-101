On 11 September 2018, the president of Liberia started a football match.

George Weah was 51. He played 79 minutes of a friendly against Nigeria, arranged to retire his No. 14 shirt.

Twenty-three years earlier, he had been voted the best footballer in the world.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Weah was born in Clara Town, a poor district of Monrovia, and learned the game in its streets.

At Tonnerre Yaoundé, in Cameroon, Claude Le Roy saw him and recommended him to Monaco's coach, Arsène Wenger.

At Paris Saint-Germain he was the 1994–95 Champions League top scorer. Milan knocked PSG out in the semi-final.

So Milan bought him.

That year the Ballon d'Or opened to any player at a European club. Weah was the first winner under the new rules, and the first African. He was FIFA World Player of the Year too, and dedicated it to Wenger.

In 1996 he picked up a cleared Verona corner in his own box, ran the length of San Siro and scored.

Then came the part that surprised me.

While he was winning in Italy, Liberia was at war with itself. Its national team, the Lone Star, existed in large part because of him. ESPN reported him paying flights and allowances himself.

A Ballon d'Or winner, paying for his team-mates' travel.

For all that, he never played at a World Cup.

In 1995 FourFourTwo had labelled him Liberia's next head of state. As he told the magazine: "No, it's not my wish to become president of Liberia."

He stood in 2005, and lost. In December 2017 he won the presidency, with more than 60 per cent.

It was 22 years after the interview.

In 2022 his son, Timothy, scored for the United States at the World Cup his father never reached. A year later Weah lost the presidency by 50.64 per cent to 49.36, and conceded.

Here's what stays with me. The run against Verona lasted seconds. The years of paying for a national team at war lasted far longer. What a player does for others can outlast what he does with the ball.
