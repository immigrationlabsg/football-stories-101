There were four minutes of normal time left at San Siro on 8 September 1996, Milan against Hellas Verona, when a Verona corner was cleared and the ball fell to George Weah inside Milan's own penalty area. He set off with it and did not stop. He ran the length of the field with it, and at the far end he put the ball in the net. Milan won 4–1. Weah described it later as a goal he scored running from his own penalty area, which is exactly what it was.

He was 29, and a few months earlier he had become the first African to win the Ballon d'Or. Within a decade he would be running for president of Liberia.

## Clara Town

Weah was born on 1 October 1966 in Clara Town, a poor district of the Liberian capital, Monrovia, and learned the game in its streets before playing for a string of clubs in the city. His first move abroad was to Cameroon, to Tonnerre Yaoundé, and it was there that Claude Le Roy, then coaching Cameroon's national team, saw him. Le Roy recommended him to a French coach at Monaco. The coach was Arsène Wenger, and in 1988, by Monaco's own account and most others (Britannica dates the move to 1987), Weah arrived in the principality.

The arrival was hard. Weah has described Wenger as a father figure who treated him like a son, and has credited him with helping him through the racism he met in his first years in French football. In 1989 Weah was voted African Footballer of the Year, the first of three times he would win it.

In 1992 he moved to Paris Saint-Germain. PSG were French champions in 1993–94, and in the 1994–95 Champions League Weah was the competition's top scorer with seven goals by UEFA's count, one more than Ajax's Jari Litmanen (PSG's own records give him eight). PSG went out in the semi-final to Milan, beaten 3–0 over the two legs, and the club that had knocked him out decided it wanted him. That summer Milan bought him.

## The award that changed its rules

Until 1995 the Ballon d'Or was open only to European players. That year the rules changed: any player at a European club, whatever his nationality, could be voted for. Weah was the first winner under the new rules, and the first African to win it. The award that had been given to Johan Cruyff, Michel Platini and Marco van Basten now carried the name of a striker from Clara Town. He was also named FIFA World Player of the Year for 1995, again the first African to take the award.

When he was named World Player of the Year, he dedicated the award to Wenger, who by then had left Monaco to coach in Japan. Wenger later recalled the gesture more simply: Weah, he said, gave him his award.

At around the same time, the English magazine FourFourTwo asked Weah, just voted the best player in the world, whether he saw himself as Liberia's next head of state. The magazine later headlined his answer: "No, it's not my wish to become president of Liberia."

At Milan he won Serie A twice, in 1995–96 under Fabio Capello and in 1998–99 under Alberto Zaccheroni, and he scored the goal against Verona that defines his time in Italy. But the story that made him different from the other great strikers of the 1990s was happening a long way from San Siro.

## The national team he paid for

Liberia was at war with itself for much of Weah's career. Through those years the national team, the Lone Star, existed in large part because of him. Britannica describes him as having played for, coached and, to a large extent, financed the side, and ESPN's reporting has him paying out of his own pocket for flights and allowances when neither the football association nor the government had the money to do so.

The high point came in the qualifiers for the 2002 World Cup. On 9 July 2000, in Paynesville, on the edge of Monrovia, Liberia beat Nigeria 2–1. By May 2001 a result in the return match in Port Harcourt would have put Liberia in control of their group. Nigeria won 2–0 and went to the World Cup in South Korea and Japan. Liberia went instead to the 2002 Africa Cup of Nations, and after it Weah retired from international football. He never played at a World Cup.

## The turn

The turn in Weah's life was the decision, taken while he was still the most famous Liberian in the world, to treat the national team as his own responsibility. Nobody required a Ballon d'Or winner at Milan to fund flights for a team that had no realistic hope of a World Cup, and the money and time he spent on it could have bought him a quieter retirement. What it bought instead was standing. When he stopped playing, he was not only a famous footballer but a man who had held something Liberian together when the state could not, and that is the reputation he took into politics. It did not make him an instant winner. It took him the best part of 12 years, and a seat in the Senate along the way, to get from his first run for the presidency to the inauguration. But the name he had built on the pitch, and in the departure lounges where he paid for his team-mates' tickets, kept him in the contest long enough to win it.

He stood for president in 2005, two years after the civil war ended. He led the first round and lost the November run-off to Ellen Johnson Sirleaf. In December 2014 he was elected senator for Montserrado County, which includes Monrovia, with 78 per cent of the vote; the runner-up was Robert Sirleaf, the president's son. In the presidential run-off of 26 December 2017 he beat Joseph Boakai, Sirleaf's vice-president, with more than 60 per cent, and on 22 January 2018 he was sworn in, in what was described as Liberia's first peaceful democratic handover of power in more than seven decades.

On 11 September 2018 the president of Liberia played for his country. He was 51. Liberia had arranged a friendly against Nigeria in Monrovia to retire his No. 14 shirt, and Weah started the match and played 79 minutes before being substituted to a standing ovation. Nigeria won 2–1. That August he had bestowed Liberia's highest honour on Wenger, and honoured Le Roy at the same ceremony: the two coaches who had taken him to Europe. Some critics in Liberia said the awards reflected personal ties rather than service to the country.

In the run-off of November 2023 Boakai beat him by 50.64 per cent to 49.36. Weah conceded before the final results were declared and telephoned Boakai to congratulate him, and Boakai was sworn in on 22 January 2024.

There was a coda in Qatar. On 21 November 2022, in the United States' opening match of the World Cup, against Wales, the first goal was scored by Timothy Weah, George's son. He was the first member of the family to play at a World Cup, the tournament his father, for all the Ballon d'Or votes and the money spent on Liberian flights, never reached.

## What it tells us

Individual greatness in football is usually measured in trophies, and Weah's are real: a Ballon d'Or, a FIFA world award, two Italian titles. But his career is a reminder that what a player does for those around him can outlast what he does with the ball. The run against Verona lasted a few seconds; the years of paying for a national team in a country at war built a name that carried him, in the end, to the highest office in Liberia.
