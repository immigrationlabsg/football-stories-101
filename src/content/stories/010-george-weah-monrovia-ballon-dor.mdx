On 11 September 2018, the president of Liberia started a football match.

He was 51. Liberia had arranged a friendly against Nigeria in Monrovia to retire his No. 14 shirt, and George Weah played 79 minutes before being substituted to a standing ovation.

Nigeria won 2–1.

Twenty-three years earlier, the same man had been voted the best footballer in the world.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Weah was born in 1966 in Clara Town, a poor district of Monrovia, and learned the game in its streets.

His first move abroad was to Tonnerre Yaoundé, in Cameroon. There Claude Le Roy, Cameroon's national coach, saw him and recommended him to a French coach at Monaco.

The coach was Arsène Wenger.

Weah arrived in 1988, by Monaco's own account; Britannica says 1987. He has described Wenger as a father figure, and credited him with helping him through the racism he met in French football.

In 1992 he moved to Paris Saint-Germain. In the 1994–95 Champions League he was the top scorer, and PSG went out in the semi-final to Milan.

So Milan bought him.

That year the Ballon d'Or changed its rules. Until 1995 it had been open only to European players. Now any player at a European club could be voted for.

Weah was the first winner under the new rules, and the first African to win it. He was also FIFA World Player of the Year.

He dedicated that award to Wenger, by then coaching in Japan. Wenger later remembered it more simply: Weah gave him his award.

Then there was the run. On 8 September 1996, with four minutes left against Hellas Verona at San Siro, a Verona corner was cleared to Weah inside Milan's own penalty area.

He ran the length of the field and scored.

Then came the part that surprised me.

While he was winning in Italy, Liberia was at war with itself. Through those years its national team, the Lone Star, existed in large part because of him.

Britannica describes him as having played for, coached and, to a large extent, financed the side. ESPN's reporting has him paying for flights and allowances out of his own pocket when neither the football association nor the government had the money.

A Ballon d'Or winner, paying for his team-mates' travel.

They came close. In the qualifiers for the 2002 World Cup, Liberia beat Nigeria 2–1 in Paynesville. In May 2001 a result in the return in Port Harcourt would have put Liberia in control of their group.

Nigeria won 2–0 and went to the World Cup. Weah retired from international football after the 2002 Africa Cup of Nations.

He never played at a World Cup.

Around the time he was voted the world's best player, FourFourTwo had labelled him Liberia's next head of state. He insisted politics was not for him. As he told the magazine: "No, it's not my wish to become president of Liberia."

In 2005, two years after the civil war ended, he stood anyway. He led the first round and lost the run-off to Ellen Johnson Sirleaf.

In December 2017 he won the presidential run-off with more than 60 per cent. On 22 January 2018 he was sworn in, in what was described as Liberia's first peaceful democratic handover of power in more than seven decades.

It was 22 years after the interview.

There was a footballing coda. On 21 November 2022, the United States' first goal of the World Cup in Qatar, against Wales, was scored by Timothy Weah, George's son.

He was the first member of the family to play at the tournament his father never reached.

A year later, in November 2023, Weah lost the presidential run-off to Joseph Boakai by 50.64 per cent to 49.36. He conceded before the final results were declared, and telephoned Boakai to congratulate him.

Here's what stays with me.

The run against Verona lasted a few seconds. The years of paying for a national team in a country at war lasted far longer. That is the name he took into politics. What a player does for others can outlast what he does with the ball.
