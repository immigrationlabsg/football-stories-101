On 31 August 2008 Barcelona opened their league season away at Numancia, a club that had just been promoted, and lost 1–0.

In their next league game they drew 1–1 at home to Racing Santander.

The man in charge had never coached in the top flight.

His name was Pep Guardiola. Four seasons later he left with 14 trophies.

Here's what I didn't know until recently.

Guardiola’s entire coaching record, when he got the job, was one season long.

In 2007–08 he took Barça B, then in the Tercera División, the fourth tier of Spanish football, to the top of their Catalan group and up through the play-offs. That was it.

At his first press conference he said he did not want Ronaldinho, Deco or Samuel Eto’o in his squad.

In their place Guardiola brought up two players he had coached in the B team a few months earlier: a defensive midfielder called Sergio Busquets and a winger called Pedro Rodríguez.

So picture it. A coach with one season of experience, three divisions below La Liga, moves on the club’s most famous player and promotes reserves.

Then he loses his first game.

Had Barcelona kept losing that September, it would have looked reckless.

After the Racing match, one of his midfielders walked into his office unprompted. As Andrés Iniesta later told FourFourTwo, he wanted to reassure his coach: “Don’t worry, everything will be fine.”

He was right. Barcelona won 19 of their next 20 league games.

On 2 May 2009 they went to the Santiago Bernabéu, went a goal down, and won 6–2.

That night, by UEFA’s account, Guardiola had Lionel Messi start on the right and drop deep into the middle for the first time. The position would become known as the false nine.

Days later the season nearly collapsed.

At Stamford Bridge, in the Champions League semi-final, Barcelona were 1–0 down to Chelsea and down to ten men. In stoppage time Iniesta shot from the edge of the area, and the ball went in.

They went through on away goals.

Then came the part that surprised me.

In the final in Rome they beat Manchester United, the holders, 2–0, with Messi scoring a looping header. With the league and the Copa del Rey already won, Guardiola had the treble in his first season as a top-flight coach.

And he was not finished with the year.

Barcelona added both Super Cups, then played Estudiantes in the Club World Cup final. Estudiantes led until Pedro equalised near the end of normal time. In extra time Messi bundled in the winner with his chest.

Barcelona had won all six trophies they competed for in a calendar year.

No club had done that before.

The secret was not a signing. Xavi Hernández, Iniesta and Messi had all come through La Masia, the club’s academy, and Guardiola built the team around their passing.

The 2010 Ballon d’Or showed what that meant. Messi won it. Iniesta was second and Xavi third.

In 2011 Barcelona met Manchester United in the Champions League final again, this time at the rebuilt Wembley, on the site where Guardiola had won the European Cup as a 21-year-old player 19 years earlier.

They won 3–1. Pedro scored the first. Busquets set up the third.

The two reserves from the summer of 2008 had made a goal each in a Champions League final.

Sir Alex Ferguson said afterwards that they were the best team he had faced.

It ended on Guardiola’s terms. In April 2012 he announced he would leave, saying that four years as Barcelona coach was an eternity and that he needed to recharge.

His last match, a 3–0 Copa del Rey final win over Athletic Club, brought his 14th trophy in four seasons, out of a possible 19.

Here's what stays with me.

One of football’s great eras began with a defeat at a newly promoted side. Its coach had one season of experience, and he trusted the players he already knew. The hardest part of a great era is often the first month, and it can turn on a midfielder knocking on an office door.
