In stoppage time at Old Trafford on 21 September 2003, with Manchester United and Arsenal level at 0–0, Martin Keown and the United substitute Diego Forlán tangled in the Arsenal penalty area and the referee, Steve Bennett, pointed to the spot. Arsenal were already down to ten men: their captain, Patrick Vieira, had been sent off for a second booking after kicking out at Ruud van Nistelrooy, setting off what Arsenal’s own account calls a 21-man scuffle. Now van Nistelrooy stood over the penalty. Jens Lehmann, Arsenal’s new goalkeeper, moved along his line. Van Nistelrooy hit the crossbar.

At the final whistle Keown, by his own later admission, made sure van Nistelrooy knew exactly how he felt. The FA later fined the club and banned several of its players, Keown among them. Nobody at Old Trafford could have known it, but a kick against the woodwork had just saved what would become the most famous unbeaten season in English football. The Premier League’s own account of that season picks out the miss as perhaps the most notable moment when the run was threatened.

> When Ruud van Nistelrooy missed his late penalty, I showed how I felt.
> — Martin Keown, interviewed by FourFourTwo

## A season too early

The idea that a team could go through an English league season without losing had been treated as faintly ridiculous. Preston North End had done it in the Football League’s first season, 1888–89, but no top-flight side had managed it since. In 2002, Arsène Wenger said that his Arsenal could. He had some evidence. In 2001–02 his side had won the Double and gone the whole league season unbeaten away from home, by the Premier League’s count 14 wins and five draws on the road.

Within weeks the prediction had been turned into a joke. On 19 October 2002 at Goodison Park, Everton’s Wayne Rooney, five days short of his 17th birthday, scored a late winner from long range, his first Premier League goal, to end Arsenal’s 30-match unbeaten run. Arsenal later gave up top spot with five games left, and Manchester United took the title. Wenger was mocked for his prediction, and Arsenal’s own history records how the Everton defeat in particular was thrown back at him. He did not forget it.

> Somebody threw me a T-shirt which read “Comical Wenger says we can go the whole season unbeaten”. I was just a season too early.
> — Arsène Wenger in 2004, as quoted by Arsenal.com and FourFourTwo

## The team

The side that did it was mostly already there. The most important summer addition was Lehmann, signed from Borussia Dortmund in July 2003. Kolo Touré, who had arrived as a midfielder, was converted into a centre-back alongside Sol Campbell. Vieira was the captain, Robert Pires scored and made goals from wide, and Dennis Bergkamp played alongside Thierry Henry.

Henry was the season’s defining player. He scored 30 league goals, about two in every five that Arsenal scored, won the Golden Boot for the second time and was named the Premier League’s player of the season. His fellow professionals voted him the PFA Players’ Player of the Year for the second year running. Behind him, Lehmann, according to the club, played every minute of the league season, and Touré started 36 of the 38 matches. But the record was not built on goals alone. Twelve of the 38 matches were draws, and some of those points were saved rather than won. What they had, from September onwards, was the habit of not losing. Vieira, looking back for the Premier League, said the players did not think of themselves as part of something special while it was happening, because nobody knows they are going to do it until the end. The danger was always the next match. Through the winter Arsenal kept drawing the games they could not win, and kept winning more than anyone else. By the spring they were clear at the top, and still in the FA Cup and the Champions League as well.

## The turn

The test came in April 2004, and it came in a single week. On 3 April Arsenal lost the FA Cup semi-final 1–0 to Manchester United at Villa Park, Paul Scholes scoring. Three days later at Highbury they went out of the Champions League quarter-final to Chelsea: level 1–1 from the first leg, Arsenal led through José Antonio Reyes at half-time, Frank Lampard equalised, and with three minutes left Wayne Bridge scored the winner. A season that had been heading for three trophies was down to one.

Three days after that, on Good Friday, 9 April, Liverpool came to Highbury. Sami Hyypiä scored after five minutes. Henry equalised, Michael Owen put Liverpool back in front before half-time, and Arsenal went in 2–1 down, a team that looked as though the week had broken it. In the second half Pires equalised and Henry scored twice more, one of them a solo goal through the Liverpool defence that Arsenal’s own history still singles out. Arsenal won 4–2.

That afternoon was the turn of the season. Arsenal did not lose again. On 25 April, after Chelsea had lost at Newcastle earlier in the day, they needed a point at White Hart Lane. Vieira and Pires gave them a 2–0 lead in the first half, Jamie Redknapp pulled one back, and Robbie Keane equalised from the penalty spot with the last kick of the game. It finished 2–2, and the point won them the title on their neighbours’ ground, just as the 1970–71 Double side had clinched the championship at Tottenham.

With four matches left, Wenger worried that, as he later put it, champions usually lose the next game because everybody switches off. He told his players that they had been champions; now they could become “immortal”. He has told the story in slightly different words over the years, but the message was always the same: the only way to be remembered for ever was to refuse to lose any of the remaining games.

On the final day, 15 May 2004, already-relegated Leicester City came to Highbury, and their striker Paul Dickov, a former Arsenal player, put them in front after 26 minutes. At half-time the unbeaten season was 45 minutes from being lost, and Wenger later said he had been angry with his players in the dressing room. Two minutes after the restart Henry scored a penalty. In the 66th minute Vieira scored from a fine Bergkamp pass. Arsenal won 2–1.

The final record read: played 38, won 26, drawn 12, lost none, 90 points, 11 clear of Chelsea. The Premier League had a one-off gold version of the trophy made to mark the achievement, and Arsenal still keep it. On the day, though, what the players celebrated was simpler. A year and a half after a 16-year-old’s shot at Goodison had turned their manager into a punchline, they had done exactly what he said they could.

The run did not stop when the season did; it went on into the following one. In August 2004 Arsenal passed the 42-match record set by Brian Clough’s Nottingham Forest, and they reached 49 league games unbeaten before losing 2–0 at Old Trafford on 24 October 2004, van Nistelrooy scoring a penalty and Rooney, by then a United player, the second.

## What it tells us

The Invincibles are remembered for Henry’s brilliance, but their record rests on something duller: the refusal to lose games they were not winning. The week in April that should have ended their season is the reason they are remembered at all, because it was when a team with nothing left but the league decided that the league would be enough. A prediction that was laughed at in 2002 became the standard against which every unbeaten run in England is now measured.
