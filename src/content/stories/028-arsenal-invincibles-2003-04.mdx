In stoppage time at Old Trafford on 21 September 2003, Ruud van Nistelrooy stood over a penalty with the score 0–0.

Arsenal were down to ten men. Their captain, Patrick Vieira, had been sent off. Jens Lehmann, their new goalkeeper, moved along his line.

Van Nistelrooy hit the crossbar.

Nobody knew it then, but a kick against the woodwork had just saved the most famous unbeaten season in English football.

Here’s what I didn’t know until recently.

A year earlier, the man who predicted that season had been laughed at for it.

In 2002 Arsène Wenger said his Arsenal could go a whole league season without losing. Preston North End had done it in the Football League’s first season, 1888–89. No top-flight side had managed it since.

Within weeks the idea was a joke. On 19 October 2002 at Goodison Park, Everton’s Wayne Rooney, five days short of his 17th birthday, scored a late winner from long range. It was his first Premier League goal, and it ended Arsenal’s 30-match unbeaten run.

Arsenal later gave up top spot, and Manchester United took the title.

Somebody threw Wenger a T-shirt mocking his prediction. As he put it in 2004: “I was just a season too early.”

When it came, the unbeaten season was not built on winning. Arsenal drew 12 of their 38 league games, and some of those points were saved rather than won.

Thierry Henry scored 30 league goals and won the Golden Boot. Lehmann, according to the club, played every minute. Kolo Touré, who had arrived as a midfielder, was turned into a centre-back.

But what the team really had was the habit of not losing.

Vieira later said the players did not feel part of something special while it was happening. Nobody knows until the end.

The test came in a single week in April 2004.

On 3 April Arsenal lost the FA Cup semi-final to Manchester United. Three days later Chelsea knocked them out of the Champions League at Highbury, Wayne Bridge scoring with three minutes left.

Three trophies had become one.

Then came the part that surprised me.

Three days after that, on Good Friday, Liverpool came to Highbury and led 2–1 at half-time. A team that looked as though the week had broken it came out and won 4–2. Henry scored a hat-trick, one of them a solo run through the Liverpool defence.

Arsenal did not lose again.

They clinched the title with a 2–2 draw at White Hart Lane, on their neighbours’ ground, just as the 1970–71 Double side had done. With four games left, Wenger told his players they had been champions; now they could become immortal. He has told that story in slightly different words over the years.

On the final day, relegated Leicester City went ahead at Highbury through Paul Dickov, a former Arsenal player. At half-time the unbeaten season was 45 minutes from being lost.

Henry scored a penalty two minutes after the restart. Vieira added the winner from a Dennis Bergkamp pass.

Played 38, won 26, drawn 12, lost none. The Premier League had a one-off gold trophy made. Arsenal still keep it.

The run went on into the next season, all the way to 49 league games. It ended at Old Trafford on 24 October 2004, with a van Nistelrooy penalty and a goal from Rooney, by then a United player.

Van Nistelrooy and Rooney, again.

Here’s what stays with me.

The Invincibles are remembered for Henry’s brilliance, but their record rests on something duller: refusing to lose the games they were not winning. The week that should have ended their season is the reason they are remembered at all. A prediction that was laughed at became the standard.
